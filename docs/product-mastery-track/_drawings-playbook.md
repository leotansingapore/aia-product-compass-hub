# APA Drawings Playbook

## How to use this playbook

This is the reference document for every drawing you'll ever need in an APA appointment. Each entry tells you:

- **What to draw** — the structure, step-by-step
- **When to use it** — which phase of the appointment flow, or which prospect type
- **The script** — what you say while drawing
- **Variations** — how to adapt it for different prospect profiles

The drawings are organised in three tiers:

- **Tier 1: The 10 must-memorize core drawings** — drill these until you can produce them on a blank whiteboard in 60 seconds each, without reference
- **Tier 2: Drawings by appointment phase** — the supporting diagrams for each phase of the standard flow
- **Tier 3: Drawings by use case** — coverage planning, retirement planning, and restructure / cross-shop attack diagrams

## Why drawings beat slides (the meta-principle)

> *"I close most of my plans now with pen/paper drawings. The illustrator/decks/slides are the 'final outcome' of the concept that I am trying to present. The pen/paper is to allow the prospect to understand my POV / follow closely the train of thought to arrive at 'that outcome.'"* — Leo

Three reasons drawings outperform slides:

1. **The prospect engages.** A pre-made slide is consumed passively. A drawing being built in real-time pulls the prospect's eye, their attention, and often their pen — they start drawing along. Engagement is the lead indicator of close rate.
2. **You control the pacing.** Slides splash all the information at once. Drawings reveal one concept at a time, in the order you want, at the pace the prospect can absorb.
3. **The prospect takes the drawing home.** Pen-and-paper drawings become an artefact the prospect can refer back to after the meeting. This reduces buyer's remorse and gives you a touchpoint for the post-meeting summary text — *"refer to the circle drawing on page 1 — that's the 1/3 rule we talked about."*

**Common mistake to avoid:** switching screens between an illustrator tool and your drawing tool during a Zoom call is jarring. Keep the illustrator on your local computer for *your* calculations, and draw separately on the Zoom whiteboard (or hold up paper to the camera). The prospect should not see the illustrator directly — it's a tool for you, not for them.

## Tools to use

- **Pen and paper** — best for in-person appointments. Buy a thick A4 notebook with detachable pages. Leave the drawings with the prospect at the end of the meeting.
- **[Excalidraw](https://app.excalidraw.com/)** — best for Zoom appointments. Hand-drawn aesthetic, infinite canvas, exports cleanly to PNG for the post-meeting text. Login with the shared agency Google account.
- **Zoom whiteboard** — works for quick drawings during Zoom, but Excalidraw is better for anything you'll want to save.
- **iPad + Apple Pencil** — best of both worlds: hand-drawn feel + shareable file. Apps like Procreate or GoodNotes work well.

## Drawing 1 — The 1/3 Rule circle

**When to use:** Phase 2 (Financial Health Check) or Phase 3 (Problems of Investing). Use it whenever the prospect needs to see how to allocate income.

**What to draw:**
1. Draw a circle.
2. Divide it into three equal wedges with two lines from the centre.
3. Label the wedges:
   - **1/3 Short-term needs** — monthly expenses, daily living
   - **1/3 Medium-term needs** — house renovation, car, wedding, holidays, big-ticket purchases
   - **1/3 Long-term needs** — retirement, future planning (wealth accumulation + protection)
4. Inside the long-term wedge, sub-divide: **10-15% Risk Management** (insurance) and **15-25% Wealth Accumulation** (investing).
5. Write absolute dollar amounts for the prospect: if they earn $4,500/mo, label each third as "$1,500" so they can see the breakdown in their currency.

**The script:**
> *"Let's draw out where your income should go. Take a circle — that's your monthly take-home. Divide it into three equal parts. The first third is for your short-term needs — your daily expenses, rent, food, transport. The second third is for medium-term — the big stuff coming up in the next few years: house renovation, car, wedding, holidays. The last third is for long-term — your future self. That's split between protection — insurance, about 10-15% — and wealth building, about 15-25%.*
>
> *Most people get the short-term right. Most people get the medium-term wrong (they over-spend on this slot). And most people skip the long-term entirely. The 1/3 rule keeps all three slots funded without overthinking it. At your $4,500 monthly income, that's about $1,500 to each bucket."*

**Variation:** If the prospect already has high savings (e.g., $300K in the bank but no investments), use this drawing to surface the *imbalance* — they've over-allocated to short/medium term and starved the long term.

## Drawing 2 — The diversified portfolio pie chart

**When to use:** Phase 3 (Problems of Investing) or when the prospect says "I'll just buy S&P 500" or compares APA against DIY.

**What to draw:**
1. Draw a circle.
2. Divide it into wedges along **three diversification axes**:
   - **Country**: US (largest wedge), Global (developed markets), Asia, China, India, Emerging Markets
   - **Sector**: Technology, Healthcare, Finance, Consumer, Industrials
   - **Asset class**: Equities (large wedge), Bonds, REITs, Covered Calls, Equity-Linked Bonds
3. On the page, draw three small horizontal timelines below the pie — labelled US, China, Global — and draw squiggly business-cycle lines on each that peak and trough at *different times*.
4. Annotate: *"different cycles smooth out the portfolio"*.

**The script:**
> *"Most people think diversification just means 'buy a lot of stocks.' But real diversification is across three dimensions: countries, sectors, and asset classes. Different countries peak and trough at different times — when US is in recession, Asia might be growing. Different sectors do the same — tech crashes while consumer staples hold steady. Different asset classes do the same — equities drop while bonds rise.*
>
> *AIA's Adventurous Index Fund is structurally built across all three dimensions. When you buy S&P 500, you've only diversified across the US sector and within US large-cap equities — you're concentrated, not diversified."*

**Variation for DIY-platform users:** Add a label *"50% China + 50% Tech"* on a separate circle next to the diversified one — that's the real-world allocation Leo has seen on prospects who bought ILPs without rebalancing. Side-by-side, the contrast is obvious.

## Drawing 3 — The 4-quadrant coverage grid

**When to use:** Phase 1 / Phase 2, or whenever the prospect needs to see what coverage areas exist. This is the canonical CST opener.

**What to draw:**
1. Draw a 2×2 grid.
2. Label the quadrants:
   - **Top-left:** Death / TPD (10-20× annual income)
   - **Top-right:** CI / ECI (5-10× annual income, recurring)
   - **Bottom-left:** Hospital (full private/public coverage)
   - **Bottom-right:** Accident (reimbursement + accidental DD)
3. In each quadrant, write two lines: **Current cover** (in red if there's a gap) and **Recommended cover** (in green).
4. Circle any quadrant with a material gap.

**The script:**
> *"There are four areas of coverage everyone needs to plan for. Death and TPD — protects your family if you can't earn. CI and ECI — replaces your income if you can't work due to illness. Hospital plan — covers your medical bills. Accident plan — covers minor injuries and accidental death/disability.*
>
> *Let me write out what you have in each box and what's recommended for someone in your situation. Anywhere we have red — that's a gap we should plan for. Anywhere we have green — you're already in good shape."*

**Variation:** For young prospects with company insurance only, the grid often shows red in all four boxes despite the prospect believing they're "covered." Use this drawing to expose under-coverage.

## Drawing 4 — The retirement-gap calculation

**When to use:** Phase 2 or Phase 3 — the canonical retirement-shortfall reveal.

**What to draw:**
1. Top of the page, write: **"What you spend today: $X/mo"** (use the prospect's actual number, e.g., $3,500).
2. Below that, write: **"Inflated to age 65 (2.5%/yr): $Y/mo"** (use the inflation calculator output, e.g., $11,000).
3. Below that, two columns:
   - **What you'll have at 65:** CPF Life payout (e.g., $3,000) — write this with a green tick
   - **What you'll need at 65:** $Y/mo (from above)
4. Subtract: **"Shortfall: $Z/mo"** (e.g., $8,000)
5. Below that: **"Total pot needed at 65: $Z × 300 ≈ $Z × 12 ÷ 5% dividend yield = $X million"** (e.g., $1.9M)
6. Below that, two routes:
   - **Save in bank** at 0.5% → $Save/mo needed (massive number, e.g., $5,000/mo)
   - **Invest in APA** at 8% → $Invest/mo needed (manageable, e.g., $1,500/mo)

**The script:**
> *"You said you spend about $3,500 a month today. Sounds reasonable. But money loses about 2.5% of buying power every year due to inflation. By the time you're 65, that same $3,500 lifestyle will cost about $11,000 a month — that's the same kind of life you live today, just in 2050's dollars.*
>
> *CPF Life will pay you about $3,000 a month. So your shortfall is roughly $8,000 a month — $96,000 a year. To generate that as dividends at 5% yield, you need about $1.9 million sitting somewhere by age 65.*
>
> *Two paths to get there. Path 1: save it in the bank at 0.5% interest. You'd need to save $5,000 a month for the next 35 years. That's not feasible on your current income. Path 2: invest it at 8% expected return. Same end goal, but you only need to put in $1,500 a month. That's the structural reason investing matters — it's not optional, it's the only way the math works."*

## Drawing 5 — The BTIR comparison (decoupling)

**When to use:** Phase 4 / Phase 5, or when the prospect has an existing whole-life policy or hybrid ILP.

**What to draw:**
1. Draw two large boxes side by side.
2. **Left box:** label it "Whole-life / Hybrid ILP". Inside, draw two smaller boxes labelled "Insurance" and "Investment", crossed by a single arrow showing they share the same premium. Label: *"Premium $X/mo, both jobs done badly"*.
3. **Right box:** label it "BTIR — Decoupled Structure". Inside, draw two separate boxes side by side: "Term cover" (~$50/mo, $500K-$1M coverage) and "APA — pure investment" (~$300-450/mo, 100% to investment). Label: *"Same $X/mo, two jobs done well"*.
4. Below both boxes, write the projected outcomes at 65 (with verified APA numbers).
5. Add a note: *"CI claim on Term policy doesn't touch the APA — accumulation keeps compounding."*

**The script:**
> *"Your current whole-life is doing two jobs at once — providing cover AND building cash value. The problem is, doing both at once means doing neither well. The insurance is more expensive than a pure term plan would be, and the investment side is conservative because it has to fund the cover.*
>
> *The cleaner structure is BTIR — Buy Term, Invest the Rest. Same monthly budget, split into two separate plans: a low-cost term policy for the cover, and APA for the pure investment. Each piece is optimised. And — this is the structural killer — if you ever claim on the CI rider of your whole-life, your death and TPD cover gets reduced and your cash value goes to zero. With BTIR, a CI claim on your term policy doesn't touch the APA. The accumulation side keeps compounding regardless."*

## Drawing 6 — The Term vs Life comparison

**When to use:** When the prospect asks "should I buy a life plan or a term plan?" or when restructuring an old life policy.

**What to draw:**
1. Two columns, labelled "Term" and "Life".
2. Row 1: **Premium** — Term: $1,600/yr | Life: $2,400/yr (use the prospect's actual figures)
3. Row 2: **Coverage** — Term: $500K death + $200K CI till 65 | Life: $200K death/TPD/CI lifelong
4. Row 3: **What happens on CI claim** — Term: cover continues for death/TPD until claim exhausts | Life: death/TPD cover *reduced or eliminated*, cash value forfeited
5. Row 4: **Cash value at 65** — Term: $0 (but you've saved $800/yr × 35 yrs invested at 8% = ~$130K-150K extra) | Life: ~$100-200K (depending on plan)
6. Below the table, draw arrows showing the $800/yr savings from Term flowing into an "Invested" box that grows to ~$150K at 65.

**The script:**
> *"Term and Life are doing the same job — providing cover. But the cost structure is different. Term is cheaper monthly, but you don't get cash value at the end. Life is more expensive monthly, but you get some cash value back.*
>
> *The trade-off: with Term, the cost savings — $800 a year in your case — if you invest them in APA, compound to about $150K at 65. Plus you still have the cover. Total wealth from the Term route: cover during your working years + $150K cash at 65. Total wealth from the Life route: cover for life + $100-150K cash at 65, but if you ever claim CI, the cover and cash both shrink or disappear.*
>
> *The other angle: Life plans are limited-pay, lifelong coverage. Term plans are lifelong pay, limited coverage. But the 'lifelong coverage' of a Life plan is illusory once you claim — the cover gets reduced. Term + APA gives you both pieces, structurally separate."*

## Drawing 7 — The AIA-vs-S&P-500 / DIY structural list

**When to use:** Phase 4 — when the prospect says "I'll just buy S&P 500" or "DIY is cheaper."

**What to draw:**
1. Two columns: "AIA APA" and "DIY (S&P 500 / Tiger / IBKR)".
2. Five rows, each comparing a structural attribute:
   - **Dividend tax:** None | 30% US dividend withholding
   - **Estate tax:** None | 40% above USD 60K (US estate tax for non-US residents on US-listed securities)
   - **Currency risk:** SGD-denominated, hedged | USD exposure, 1.5-2% p.a. depreciation against SGD long-term
   - **Capital protection on death:** 100% of premiums paid (contractually guaranteed floor) | Whatever the market shows that day
   - **Secondary insured / inheritance:** Spouse inherits a managed policy, dividends continue | Cash lump sum (heirs may mismanage)
3. Add a sixth row: **Fund stewardship** — AIA: Wellington / BlackRock / Baillie Gifford / Capital Group | DIY: you (the prospect) manage it yourself
4. At the bottom: *"Same long-term equity exposure. Different structural protections."*

**The script:**
> *"S&P 500 is great — buy it, put it at the top of your wealth pyramid. APA isn't competing with it on raw equity exposure. APA is doing a different job — it's the foundation layer of your wealth, with structural protections that DIY can't replicate.*
>
> *Five structural differences. No dividend tax (DIY pays 30% on US dividends). No estate tax (DIY pays 40% above USD 60K on US-listed securities at death — your family inherits 60 cents on the dollar). No currency risk (DIY is USD-denominated, loses 1.5-2% per year to SGD strengthening). Capital protection on death (DIY pays whatever the market shows that day). Secondary insured (your spouse inherits a managed policy with dividends still flowing, not a cash lump sum they have to figure out).*
>
> *Different jobs. Both at the same time. That's the answer."*

## Drawing 8 — The Welcome + Loyalty bonus stack

**When to use:** Phase 4 — when the prospect asks about fees, or when justifying the 3.9% supplementary charge.

**What to draw:**
1. Draw a horizontal timeline from Y1 to Y30+.
2. Above Y1, Y2, Y3 — three bars labelled with the prospect's tier bonus: e.g., **15% | 18% | 20%** for $12K+/yr premium.
3. Below the bars, write the dollar values: "$1,800 + $2,160 + $2,400 = **$6,360 in Y1-3 alone**".
4. From Y10 to Y20 — a continuous bar labelled **5% Special Bonus per year of annualised premium**.
5. From Y21 onwards — a continuous bar labelled **8% Special Bonus per year**.
6. Below the entire timeline, write the cumulative totals: e.g., **$18,000 / $23,000 total bonus stack across the policy life** (with verified numbers from iPOS).
7. Above the timeline, draw the supplementary charge curve: **3.9% Y1-10, then drops to 0% from Y11**.
8. Annotate: *"Welcome stack offsets the charge in Y1-3. Special Bonus accrues every year you keep paying."*

**The script:**
> *"There's a structural offset to the 3.9% supplementary charge that most prospects don't see. In year 1, you get a 15% Welcome Bonus on your premium. Year 2 it's 18%. Year 3 it's 20%. That's 53% of your annualised premium credited as a bonus across the first three years — $6,360 of bonus at your premium tier, before compounding even starts.*
>
> *Then from year 10 onwards, the Special Bonus credits 5% of your annualised premium every year you keep paying — going to 8% from year 21. Over a 25-30 year hold, that totals roughly $18,000 - $23,000 of bonus stack from iPOS.*
>
> *Net effective fee over the policy life is materially lower than competitors who have a 'low headline charge but perpetual deduction' curve. APA's structure: heavy in Y1-10, zero from Y11, with bonus credits accumulating the whole way. Different category of fee math."*

## Drawing 9 — The hospital-income "pit" drawing

**When to use:** When closing a hospital income plan, or when the prospect asks why they need hospital income on top of a regular hospital plan.

**What to draw:**
1. Draw a horizontal timeline of the prospect's working life from age 25 to age 65.
2. At some midpoint (e.g., age 45), draw a downward dip — a "pit" — labelled *"hospitalisation: 30 days off work"*.
3. To the left of the pit, draw a horizontal line at the prospect's monthly income level (e.g., $5,000/mo). Label: *"normal monthly income"*.
4. Inside the pit, draw the line at zero. Label: *"$0 income during hospitalisation"*.
5. Calculate the dollar value of the pit: *"30 days × $5,000/mo ÷ 20 working days = $7,500 lost income"*.
6. Draw a second line filling the pit, at a hospital-income-plan payout level (e.g., $250/day × 30 days = $7,500). Label: *"hospital income plan fills the pit"*.

**The script:**
> *"Your regular hospital plan covers the hospital bill itself — surgery, ward fees, medication. What it doesn't cover is the income you lose while you're hospitalised. If you're hospitalised for 30 days, your salary doesn't keep coming in — that's the 'pit.'*
>
> *Your monthly income is $5,000. If you're out for 30 days, you've lost roughly $7,500 in earnings (one month of salary plus some recovery time). A hospital income plan pays you a daily benefit while you're warded — typically $250 to $500 per day. So if you're warded for 30 days at $250/day, you get $7,500 — that exactly fills the pit.*
>
> *Pricing for this plan starts at about $20-30 a month. It's not expensive, and it's the single most useful add-on once you have a regular hospital plan in place."*

**Variation for self-employed / business-owner prospects:** the pit is deeper because they have no employer sick pay safety net. Scale the income loss accordingly.

## Drawing 10 — The before / after restructure

**When to use:** End of every restructure case — Phase 5 / Phase 5B — when closing a multi-policy restructure.

**What to draw:**
1. Divide the page into two columns: **BEFORE** and **AFTER**.
2. **BEFORE column:** list every existing policy with monthly premium, coverage, and projected value at 65 (or relevant horizon).
3. **AFTER column:** list every new policy with the same fields.
4. At the bottom of each column, sum: **Total monthly outflow** and **Total wealth at 65** and **Total coverage**.
5. Use red ink to highlight gaps in BEFORE. Use green ink to highlight improvements in AFTER.
6. At the very bottom: write the three-line summary — *"Same monthly. More coverage. More cash at 65."* (or whichever combination applies).

**The script:**
> *"Let me draw out what we just decided. On the left, what you have today. On the right, what you'll have after we restructure. Same monthly outflow — $807 a month. But on the right, your death cover has gone from $35,000 to $1.5 million. Your hospital plan is now in place. Your retirement projection at 65 has gone from $38,000 to $545,000. Same money. Dramatically different structure."*

**The take-home function:** Leave this drawing with the prospect. Their family will see it. They'll re-read it. It dramatically reduces buyer's remorse and helps them explain the restructure to their spouse.

# Tier 2 — Drawings by appointment phase

## Phase 2 — Financial Health Check

### The 4-ratio liquidity grid

**What to draw:** A 2×2 grid with the four ratios (liquidity, asset-to-net-worth, debt-to-asset, solvency). For each, write the prospect's current value, the healthy range, and a "green / amber / red" indicator.

### Income / expense waterfall

**What to draw:** Draw the prospect's monthly income at the top, then a vertical bar showing expenses flowing out (rent, food, transport, etc.). What's left over is the "investable surplus." Quantify it — that's the prospect's budget headroom.

## Phase 3 — Problems of Investing

### The procrastination compounding curve

**What to draw:** Two compounding curves on the same chart. Curve A starts at age 25 and reaches $1M at 65. Curve B starts at age 35 (10 years later) and only reaches $400K at 65 with double the monthly contribution. Annotate: *"The cost of waiting 10 years isn't just 10 years of contribution — it's 60% of your retirement pot."*

### The 25-year-old anchor

**What to draw:** $3,000 today × inflation (2.5%/yr) × 40 years = $10,000/mo at 65. Then: $10,000/mo × 12 / 5% dividend yield = $2.4M pot needed. Then: $2.4M at 5% yield = $10K/mo of dividends for life.

### Lump sum vs dividend mode (the "3 birds" reveal)

**What to draw:** Three boxes labelled "Capital" (the pot itself), "Dividends" (paid out monthly), and "Bequest" (left to family on death). Draw arrows showing all three flow simultaneously from the same APA. Annotate: *"One plan, three benefits at retirement."*

### Savings vs investing comparison

**What to draw:** A table comparing $1,000/mo saved in the bank (0.5%) vs invested in APA (8%) over 40 years. Bank: $480K. APA: $3.5M. Annotate: *"Same money, $3M difference."*

## Phase 4 — Investment Illustrations

### The supplementary charge curve

**What to draw:** A horizontal timeline from Y1 to Y30. Above Y1-Y10, a bar at **3.9%**. From Y11 onwards, drop the bar to **0%**. Annotate: *"AIA: high early, zero forever after. Competitors: low early, perpetual."*

### Cost-of-delay compounding

**What to draw:** Same as the procrastination curve above, but specifically applied to APA's bonus stack — start at 25 vs start at 30 vs start at 35, showing the cumulative bonus stack difference at 65.

### Premium holiday / limited-pay variant

**What to draw:** Two scenarios side-by-side. Scenario A: pay $600/mo for 10 years (10-yr IIP), then stop. Scenario B: pay $600/mo continuously to 65. Show the cash value at 65 for both. Annotate: *"10-yr IIP is the minimum. Most clients elect to continue paying for the Special Bonus stack."*

### Dividend mode mechanic

**What to draw:** A horizontal timeline. Years 1-30: a growing bar labelled "Accumulation phase" — APA value growing to $1M+ at 60. Year 60 onwards: a horizontal bar at $1M, with arrows pointing down labelled "$6,000/mo dividends paid out". The capital stays the same; only the dividends flow.

## Phase 5 / 5B — Quotation + Close

### The Three Cost Circles

**What to draw:** Three overlapping circles in a Venn-diagram style:
- Circle 1: **Insurance / risk management** (insurance against catastrophic loss)
- Circle 2: **Investment / wealth accumulation** (compounding for retirement)
- Circle 3: **Lifestyle / current needs** (the prospect's day-to-day expenses)
The overlap region is where APA sits — covering risk-management while accumulating wealth, leaving lifestyle untouched.

### Option A / B close

**What to draw:** Two boxes labelled "Option A: $500/mo" and "Option B: $800/mo". Inside each, write the projected APA value at 65 and the projected retirement income. Annotate: *"Both work. A is the floor, B is the path to your stated retirement income goal."*

### Free-advisor / zero-fees structure

**What to draw:** A diagram showing the prospect, the APA policy, and AIA's institutional fund managers (Wellington / BlackRock / Baillie Gifford / Capital Group). Annotate: *"You pay AIA for the policy. AIA pays the fund managers. You don't pay an ongoing advisor fee — your financial planner is included."*

# Tier 3 — Drawings by use case

## Coverage planning (CST opener)

### Hospital plan with / without rider

**What to draw:** Two horizontal bars representing a $10,000 hospital bill.
- **Without rider:** $3,500 deductible (paid by prospect) + 10% co-insurance (paid by prospect) + 90% covered by base plan. Total out-of-pocket: $3,500 + $650 = $4,150.
- **With rider:** $0 deductible + 5% co-insurance capped at $3,000 (paid by prospect) + 95% covered. Total out-of-pocket: max $3,000 (often less).

Annotate: *"Rider waives the deductible and caps the co-insurance. Single biggest add-on for hospital coverage."*

### Accident vs hospital coverage scope

**What to draw:** A Venn diagram with two overlapping circles:
- **Hospital plan:** covers if you're warded ≥6 hours or undergoing surgery.
- **Accident plan:** covers minor injuries even without hospitalisation — TCM, sprains, dengue, food poisoning, accidental amputation, accidental death.

Most claims in the overlap region. But accident plan is much wider for minor incidents.

### Plan A vs B vs C ward comparison

**What to draw:** A 3-row table:
- **Plan A:** can stay A, B, or C ward. Most expensive, most flexibility.
- **Plan B:** can stay B or C ward (not A).
- **Plan C:** can stay C ward only.

Annotate: *"You can always step down (B-plan holder can stay C). You can never step up (C-plan holder can't stay B without paying the difference)."*

### CI / ECI / Relapse buffet analogy

**What to draw:** Draw three buffet tables labelled "Major CI", "Early CI", and "Relapse".
- **Major CI table:** unlimited servings (claim as many times as needed) across unlimited dishes (all 73 CIs covered). Wait 1 year between servings.
- **Early CI table:** up to 5 servings (5 claims), across all dishes. Wait 1 year between servings.
- **Relapse table:** 6 special dishes only (heart attack, stroke, organ transplant, cancer, paralysis, plus one more). Each can be claimed 1 more time, 2 years between.

**The script:** *"Think of CI claims like a buffet. The Major CI table has unlimited servings — every illness covered, every claim payable, as many times as you need. The Early CI table is smaller — 5 claims total. The Relapse table is for 6 specific illnesses you might face again."*

### GPP vs UCC comparison

**What to draw:** Two columns:
- **GPP (Guaranteed Protect Plus — term plan):**
  - Cheaper premium
  - Coverage until specified age (e.g., 65, 75, 85)
  - 3X or 2X multiplier
  - Optional ECI rider
  - No cash value
- **UCC (Ultimate Critical Cover — whole-life CI):**
  - More expensive premium
  - Coverage to whichever age you choose (65, 75, 85, or lifelong)
  - Cash value
  - Multi-claim across 73 CIs / 150 condition-stages

Annotate: *"GPP for term protection at lowest cost. UCC for CI cash value + multi-claim depth."*

### Early CI vs Major CI definitions

**What to draw:** A timeline of illness progression. Left side: early stages (e.g., Carcinoma in Situ, Stage 0 cancer, mild stroke). Right side: late stages (Stage 3+ cancer, severe stroke). ECI covers everything from left side onwards. Major CI only covers late-stage illnesses.

Annotate: *"ECI is the wider definition. By the time you trigger Major CI, treatment costs have already mounted. ECI catches you earlier."*

## Retirement planning

### CPF Life FRS / ERS / BRS

**What to draw:** Three boxes labelled BRS / FRS / ERS with current dollar amounts (verify the year). Inside each, write the projected monthly payout at 65. Annotate:
- BRS: ~$1,000/mo (basic)
- FRS: ~$1,800/mo (full)
- ERS: ~$2,700/mo (enhanced)

Then show the prospect's projected RA balance at 55 and which sum they'll qualify for.

### Retirement healthcare funding angle

**What to draw:** Two scenarios.
- **Do nothing:** OA savings deplete to $0 by age 80-85 because hospital plan premiums and out-of-pocket costs eat into them. Bequest at death: $0.
- **Do something:** OA savings → APA / PWV dividend pot. Dividends fund the hospital plan premiums. Capital stays intact. Bequest at death: full capital + ongoing dividends for spouse.

Annotate: *"Same money. One plan loses it all. The other preserves it for three generations."*

### Source-of-funds vs needs (LHS / RHS ledger)

**What to draw:** A vertical line down the middle of the page. Left side header: **Sources of funds**. Right side header: **What you need**.
- LHS: list all sources — CPF Life, AIA APA dividends, Manulife endowment maturity, GE endowment maturity, rental income, etc., with monthly dollar values.
- RHS: list what the prospect wants — desired retirement income, hospital plan premium, etc.
- At the bottom: subtract — show the shortfall or surplus.

Annotate: *"Existing policies on the left are ammunition, not obstacles. Use them to fund the shortfall."*

### Dividend income vs lump-sum drawdown

**What to draw:** Two boxes side-by-side.
- **Box 1 — Drawdown:** $1M pot, withdraw $4K/mo. Annotate "pot depletes by age 85" with a downward-sloping line.
- **Box 2 — Dividends:** $1M pot, withdraw $5K/mo as 6% yield. Annotate "pot stays at $1M, income flows forever" with a flat line and arrows pointing out.

Annotate: *"Drawdown is finite. Dividends are infinite. Same starting capital, different sustainability."*

## Restructure / cross-shop attack diagrams

### Whole-life cash value redirect (pre-retiree)

**What to draw:**
1. Draw the existing whole-life policy as a box on the left. Inside, label "Cash value: $50K (paid-up)". Below: "Monthly premium: $400/mo".
2. Draw two arrows pointing right:
   - Arrow 1 (cash value): point to a new APA box with "Lump-sum top-up: $48,500 (after 3% top-up charge)"
   - Arrow 2 (monthly premium): point to the same new APA box with "Regular premium: $400/mo continuous"
3. Inside the new APA box, project the combined value at 65. Annotate: *"~$256K at 65 (8%), vs the old whole-life's ~$120K cash value at 70."*

### Decoupling diagram (term + standalone CI + pure invest)

**What to draw:** A horizontal line representing the prospect's monthly budget (e.g., $500/mo). Below it, three separate boxes:
- **Box 1:** Pure term policy — $50/mo, $500K-$1M cover till 65.
- **Box 2:** Pure CI / ECI policy (UCC or GPP) — $100/mo, $200K multi-claim.
- **Box 3:** Pure APA — $350/mo, full investment allocation.

Annotate: *"Each piece does one job. CI claim on Box 2 doesn't touch Box 3. Death claim on Box 1 doesn't touch Box 3. Each piece optimised independently."*

### Pulsar / Tokio / Manulife net-yield exposure

**What to draw:** Three columns: **Stated projection rate** | **Fees deducted** | **Net yield delivered**.
- **HSBC Pulsar:** 8% → 4.7% net | 4% → 0.83% net
- **Tokio Marine ILP:** 8% → ~4.5% net | 4% → ~1% net
- **Manulife / single-fund competitors:** 8% → ~5% net | 4% → ~1.5% net
- **AIA APA:** 8% → 6.68% net | 4% → 2.74% net

Annotate: *"The 'projection rate' on the brochure is gross. The real number is net of fees. That's where the structural difference shows up."*

### 168% / 120% startup-bonus gimmick exposure

**What to draw:** A line chart. Y-axis = policy value. X-axis = years.
- The competitor (e.g., Pulsar with 168% bonus): line shoots up dramatically in Y1-3, peaks, then plateaus or declines as perpetual fees eat away.
- AIA APA: line starts lower (no aggressive startup bonus), but rises steadily as supplementary charge cliffs to 0% and Special Bonus credits accrue.
- Lines cross around year 10-12. By year 20, APA is well above.

Annotate: *"The startup bonus is real. The perpetual fees are also real. Over a 25+ year hold, the second one wins."*

# Drilling protocol

The drawings are only useful if you can produce them under pressure. Drill them:

## Daily drill (5 min)

Pick 3 random drawings from Tier 1. Set a 60-second timer for each. Produce them from memory on a blank page. Score yourself: ✅ all elements present | ⚠️ missing 1-2 elements | ❌ couldn't reproduce.

After 14 days of daily drilling, you should hit ✅ on all 10 Tier 1 drawings in under 60 seconds each.

## Pair drill (15 min)

Partner with another FC. The partner picks a prospect scenario from the [Day 5 Case Study Vault](./day-05.md#case-study-vault) — e.g., "30-yo with HSBC Pulsar, $1,000/mo, married, no kids yet."

You draw the appropriate diagrams for that scenario in real-time, narrating what you'd say. Partner gives feedback on:
- Which drawing did you pick? (Was it the right one?)
- Did the script line up with the drawing?
- Was the pacing right?

## Solo timing drill

Time yourself: produce all 10 Tier 1 drawings in under 5 minutes total. If you can't, you don't know them yet. Drill harder.

## Live-appointment drill

In your next 3 client appointments (test cases, not high-stakes), force yourself to draw at least 5 of the Tier 1 drawings during the meeting. The discomfort is the learning.
