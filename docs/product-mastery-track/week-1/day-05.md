# Day 5 — Competitor Analysis + Restructure Plays

Day 4 covered the cross-shop objections that surface in the appointment ("I'll just buy S&P 500", "fees are too high", etc.). Day 5 is the deeper layer: a structural breakdown of how to **attack** the alternatives the prospect is comparing APA against, and how to **restructure** prospects who are already in a competitor product or hybrid setup into APA.

Two parts:

- **Part 1:** competitor-by-competitor analysis. Where each competitor's wealth product is structurally weaker than APA, the verified numbers, and the cushion-then-attack scripts.
- **Part 2:** restructure plays. How to convert a prospect from whole-life / hybrid ILP / DIY-platform into APA. The BTIR (Buy Term + Invest the Rest) angle using APA as the investment vehicle.

# Part 1 — Competitor Analysis (attack moves + defend moves)

The frame: **never attack a competitor directly to the prospect.** That reads as desperate and erodes trust. Instead, structurally explain *why* APA is built differently, then let the prospect draw the conclusion themselves.

For the deep per-competitor product breakdowns, refer to the **[APA Competitor Analysis deck](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1o6rnafqQPV6z4n0NcKWOBTFs_6kntjKUolSB3-v_zOc/edit?usp=sharing)** — that's the source of truth for the verified specs across FWD, Manulife, Great Eastern, Prudential, iFAST, Endowus, StashAway, and the rest. 

---

## The structural pattern across every competitor

Across virtually every alternative the prospect will compare APA against — competitor ILPs (FWD Invest Plus, Manulife Invest Ready, GE GREAT Wealth Advantage, Prudential PRUSelectVantage), participating plans (PRUWealth, GE GREAT Wealth Multiplier), and DIY platforms (iFAST, Endowus, StashAway, Tiger Brokers, IBKR) — **the same pattern repeats**:

> **Competitors have lower headline charges in the early years, but they keep charging in some form for the entire life of the policy.** APA's curve is the opposite: higher charges in the first 10 years, then **0% supplementary charge from Year 11 onwards** — plus the Welcome Bonus stack in Y1-Y3 and the Special Bonus from Y10 onward that no DIY platform or competitor ILP replicates.

That's the structural insight. Once a prospect sees the curve, the "fees are too high" objection collapses on its own.

### The cushion-then-walk-the-curve script

When a competitor name surfaces in the appointment, don't argue the competitor down. Cushion, then walk the structure:

> *"[Competitor name] is a great plan, that's for sure. However, what's different is that they have lon term supplementary charges which compound over time. In a long enough time frame, and with a large enough portfolio, imagine whats a 1% charge on a $1mill policy? That's around $10k per year that compounds against you. So even though their supplementary charges are lower than 3.9% in the first 10 years, it never actually drops to zero.
> In fact, for APA, after 10 years, there are no supplementary charges, and there will be a 5% loyalty bonus of annual premiums per year, which increases to 8% per year after 20 years. 
>So you have more charges earlier on, but then over time, the nett charges actually reduce and even drop to zero eventually the longer you hold the plan.

In fact, you can illustrate the above point using our presenter here: https://present.financeillustrator.com/long-term-investment-illustrator/other-details?section=break-even-fees

### Where APA structurally wins, in one row per battleground

| Battleground | The structural APA edge |
|---|---|
| **Long-term supplementary charge** | APA: 0% from Y11. Most competitors keep charging in some form for the lifetime of the policy. |
| **Welcome Bonus stack** | APA: 5-75% across Y1-Y3 (53% at $12K+ premium / 10-yr IIP). DIY platforms have no equivalent; competitor ILP Welcome Bonuses run roughly 1/3 to 1/5 of APA's at the same tier. |
| **Special Bonus loyalty stack** | APA: 5% from Y10, 8% from Y21, perpetual. Competitor ILPs and DIY platforms have no equivalent. |

| **Currency / tax exposure** | APA is SGD-denominated. US-listed ETFs (via Tiger / IBKR) carry US estate tax (~30% above $60K) and US dividend withholding (30%) — structural drags APA avoids. |

### A note on DIY platforms specifically

DIY platforms (Tiger, IBKR, Endowus, StashAway, iFAST) aren't really a structural competitor to APA — they're solving a different job. They're for the **trading-account** layer of the prospect's wealth ($10K-$50K, short-to-medium-term, high-effort, high-emotion). APA is the **retirement-portfolio** layer ($1M-$3M target, low-effort, automated, professionally managed).

If a prospect is already doing DIY, the move isn't "switch from DIY to APA." It's *"keep your DIY for what it does well, and add APA as the long-term retirement layer alongside."* The DIY portfolio gets supplemented by APA, not restructured into it. Same money, two different jobs, two different tools.

The structural reasons DIY can't replace APA for the retirement-portfolio job:
- DIY platforms don't have the Welcome Bonus stack ($6,360+ over Y1-Y3 on a $12K premium)
- No perpetual Special Bonus from Year 10
- No secondary insured feature (spouse inherits cash to figure out, not a managed policy)
- US-listed ETFs carry US estate tax and dividend withholding that APA structurally avoids by being SGD-domiciled

This is the Three Cost Circles framework from [Day 2 Phase 5B](./day-02.md#phase-5b--value-proposition--final-close-5-8-min) applied at the structural level.

# Part 2 — Restructure Plays (convert existing portfolios into APA)

The frame: most prospects already have *something* — a whole-life policy, a hybrid ILP, a DIY portfolio. So our job may not even to add existing policies. Its to optimise their existing portfolio, so as to increase their long term retirement returns, or increase their coverage, with the same premiums, or maintain the same returns and coverage with lesser premiums.

---

## Play A — Restructure a whole-life policy into APA + low-cost term rider (the BTIR move)

**The setup:** prospect has a whole-life policy taking $300-500/mo in premium, projected to deliver $X cash value at age 65. They've been paying for 5-15 years already. They feel locked in.

**The attack:**

> *"Let me draw out the structure of what you have. A whole-life policy is doing two jobs at once — life cover AND cash-value accumulation. The trade-off is that the cash-value side is conservative and the cover side is bundled, so neither piece is being optimised separately.*
>
> *The alternative structure most modern prospects use is what the industry calls BTIR — Buy Term, Invest the Rest. Split the two jobs. Buy a low-cost term policy for the cover (cheaper than the whole-life cover for the same sum assured), and invest the difference in APA — where the bonus stack and 100% allocation work much harder on the accumulation side."*

**The math (illustrative, run the actual numbers in iPOS):**

| Setup | Monthly | Cover | Projected at 65 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole-life (existing) | $400/mo | ~$300K | ~$250K cash value |
| BTIR: Term + APA | $400/mo (≈$50 term + $350 APA) | $500K-$1M term cover | ~$700K-1.2M APA value (8% IIRR, 10-yr IIP) |

The split structure delivers **higher cover AND higher accumulation** at the same monthly outflow. The whole-life policy is doing both jobs badly because it can't optimise either.

> Worked example with real Pru Active Life numbers: see **[Case A — The Pru Active Life prospect](#case-a--the-pru-active-life-prospect-play-a-receipt)** below.

### The accelerated-CI / cash-value-depletion problem (the deeper attack)

There's a second structural flaw most prospects don't realise about their whole-life policy: **the cover erodes itself if it's ever used.**

Most whole-life policies in Singapore include an accelerated CI / ECI rider — meaning if the prospect claims for critical illness (or early-stage critical illness), the death / TPD coverage is **reduced by the amount paid out** (or eliminated entirely on a full CI claim). And the projected cash value at 65 — the part the prospect is actually planning to retire on — disappears the moment the policy pays out.

So the prospect is paying decades of premium for a structure that cannibalises itself. Make a CI claim, and the death cover shrinks. Make a TPD claim, and the cash-value retirement plan goes to zero.

> *"There's something most whole-life prospects don't see. If you actually claim for CI or ECI on your existing policy, your death and TPD cover gets reduced — and your projected cash value at 65 is gone the moment the policy pays out. You're paying every month for cover that erodes itself if you ever use it.*
>
> *The decoupled structure — pure term policy for the cover, APA for the wealth accumulation — means a CI claim on the term policy doesn't touch your APA. The accumulation side keeps compounding regardless of what happens on the cover side."*

That's the structural argument for separating the two jobs.

### The pre-retiree variant — redirect existing cash value into APA

For pre-retirees (50+) with a long-running whole-life policy, the case is often even cleaner. The whole-life policy may no longer be doing meaningful work for them:

- They've already accumulated **sufficient cash value** inside the policy
- The original cover need has expired — **dependants are independent, mortgage is paid, no one is materially dependent on their income**
- They're approaching retirement, so the cover the policy was designed to provide is no longer the bottleneck

In that case, the smart move isn't to keep paying premiums on cover they no longer need. It's:

1. **Stop paying further premiums** on the whole-life (paid-up status, or surrender if the cash value justifies it)
2. **Redirect the accumulated cash value** as a lump-sum top-up into a fresh APA policy
3. **Free up the monthly premium** they used to pay on the whole-life — redirect that into APA regular premium too

The result: the dormant cash value becomes a working retirement-income engine. The monthly premium that was buying redundant cover becomes monthly contribution to a plan that actually compounds. The prospect's retirement picture improves materially without needing to find new money.

Always run the numbers honestly:
- What's the current cash value if surrendered today?
- What are the surrender penalties (most whole-life policies have minimal or zero penalty after 15+ years)?
- What's the projected APA value at 65 if the cash value is redirected as a top-up?
- Is the cover loss material (i.e. do they have dependants who'd be exposed)?

If all four answer in favour of the move, the restructure is a clear win.

## Play B — Restructure a hybrid ILP (with insurance bundled) into APA + standalone insurance

**The setup:** prospect has Pro Lifetime Protector, FWD Invest Plus with a death-benefit rider, or similar — an ILP with insurance charges drawn from the units. The insurance and investment are bundled.

**The attack:**

> *"Hybrid ILPs are doing the same dual-job problem as whole-life — insurance and investment combined in one premium. The structural issue is the insurance charges get pulled from your investment units every month, which slows down compounding. Two pieces of the same money working against each other.*
>
> *APA is a pure investment ILP — 100% of every dollar of premium goes to investment. No insurance charges are drawn from your units. If you want insurance cover, you add a standalone term policy alongside APA — cheaper cover, cleaner structure."*

**The math (illustrative — verify in iPOS):**

| Structure | Monthly | Insurance charge drag | Effective investment rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hybrid ILP (PLP-style) | $500/mo | $30-80/mo drawn from units | ~$420-470/mo effective |
| APA + standalone term | $500/mo ($50 term + $450 APA) | $0 drawn from APA units | $450/mo effective |

For prospects who genuinely need life cover, this isn't a "drop the cover" move — it's a "separate the two jobs" move. The cover stays; the investment slot gets more efficient.

## Play C — The BTIR (Buy Term + Invest the Rest) angle, with APA as the investment

**The setup:** prospect is shopping pure-term vs whole-life and undecided. Or they've read enough finance content to know BTIR is a "smart" framing.

**The attack:**

> *"BTIR is a legitimate strategy. The challenge most people run into is the 'invest the rest' part — they buy the term, then they don't actually invest the difference disciplined, or they invest it in something that loses to inflation. The strategy only works if the investment side is structurally disciplined.*
>
> *That's exactly the slot APA fills. Term policy for the cover (cheap, decade-by-decade). APA for the 'invest the rest' — auto-debited monthly, professionally managed, 100% allocated, with the Welcome and Special Bonus stack that DIY can't replicate. BTIR done properly."*

**Configuration:**
- Term cover: $500K-$1M, decreasing term to age 65 (cheapest), or level term
- APA: $300-1,000/mo on a 10-year IIP, 100% Self-Select Adventurous Index Fund
- Total monthly: $400-1,100/mo

This is the cleanest setup for prospects who want the BTIR philosophy but actually want to execute it. The "invest the rest" piece is the bit that almost always fails when prospects DIY it.

## Case A — The Pru Active Life prospect (Play A receipt)

**Profile:** prospect was sold Pru Active Life as the "all-in-one" whole-life bundle. Three policies stacked: base whole-life, supplementary cover rider, accident rider.

### What they're paying

| Line item | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Pru Active Life (base) | $236.50 |
| Pru Active Protect (rider) | $117.59 |
| Pru Accident | $17.77 |
| **Total** | **$371.86/mo (≈ $4,462/yr)** |

### What they think they're getting

- Base: $100K Death/TPD, $10K ECI, $10K LCI
- Rider (till 80): scales Death/TPD to $500K, $100K Late CI, $100K Early CI (claim up to 5x), $100K severe infection cover (includes dengue)
- Promo sweetener: 30,000 Prupoints (≈ $300 voucher)

### What the policy illustration actually shows (verified from the deduction table)

At **8% projection** (the upper bound the illustration is allowed to show):

| Age | Total surrender value |
|---|---|
| 70 | **$124,038** |
| 80 | **$168,575** |

At **4% projection** (the lower bound, closer to long-run reality for participating funds):

- At 80, total surrender value is meaningfully below $150K despite ~$170K in premiums paid by then

So after ~30 years of paying $371.86/mo (~$133K in premiums), the policy returns roughly break-even at 8% and net-negative at 4%. The "investment side" of the whole-life is essentially capital preservation at best — and the cover side erodes itself the moment a CI claim is paid.

### The structural attack

> *"You've been paying $371.86 a month. At 70, even on the most optimistic 8% projection in your own illustration, your surrender value is $124,038. You've paid about $134,000 in premium by then. That's not a wealth plan — that's an expensive emergency fund.*
>
> *And the part most people don't realise: if you ever claim on the CI rider, your death and TPD cover drops by the amount paid out. If you claim TPD, the cash value you're hoping to use at 70 is gone the moment the policy pays. The structure cannibalises itself the one time you actually need it.*
>
> *Same $371.86 a month, decoupled: ~$50/mo for $500K-$1M of pure term cover till 65, and $321/mo into APA on a 10-year IIP. The APA stops taking premium after year 10 — your budget is freed from year 11 onwards. And the cover stays intact even if you claim."*

### The restructure setup (verified APA numbers — 35-year-old, 10-yr IIP, 8% IIRR)

Numbers below come from running the actual APA calculation engine (`growing-age-calculator` repo, `simulateTraditionalGrowth`) — not illustrative placeholders.

| Setup | Monthly | Total paid over 30 years | Cover for 30 years | Cash at 65 (8%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pru Active Life bundle (existing) | $371.86 | ~$133,870 | $500K Death/TPD + $150K CI (erodes on claim) | $124,038 (@70, not 65) |
| BTIR: APA + standalone term | $371 Y1-10, $50 Y11-30 | ~$56,520 | $500K-$1M term till 65 (does not erode on claim) | **$175,724** APA |

The APA projection comes from `simulateTraditionalGrowth(321, 35, 65, 8)` with 10-year IIP cap — runs $321/mo for 10 years ($38,520 total premium), then compounds at 8% with no further deductions until 65.

**Net effect for the prospect:**
- ~$77,350 less premium paid over 30 years
- ~$51,700 more cash at 65 (and at 65, not 70)
- 20 years of freed budget (~$321/mo) to invest however they want — at 8% compounding, that's another ~$176K by 65 in any sensible vehicle
- Pure term cover for the full 30 years that **doesn't erode if claimed**

### The 4% floor (honesty check)

The APA projection at the **regulatory 4% lower bound** (running the same engine with `interestRate=4`):
- APA value at 65: **$69,246**

So even on the worst-case projection APA itself is allowed to show, the restructure beats Pru's 8% projected surrender value at age 70 ($124,038) by paying ~$77K less in premium, with 20 years of freed budget left over. The 8% case is the upside. The 4% case is the floor. Pru's 4% case looks materially worse than $124K — verify in their own illustration when running the actual numbers in the appointment.

> Caveat: surrender penalty and lost in-flight bonuses on the existing Pru Active Life must be costed honestly before any move. Run the math in writing. Don't push the restructure if the surrender penalty wipes the gain.

## Case B — The FWD Invest First Summit prospect (Play B / fee attack receipt)

**Profile:** 25-year-old non-smoker, sold FWD Invest First Summit at $600/mo on a 25-year premium term, policy till age 100. Sum insured: 105% of policy value (a near-zero death benefit — this is a pure investment ILP with a token life-cover wrapper).

### What they're paying

- $600/mo for 25 years = **$180,000 total premiums by age 50**
- 5-fund allocation: 35% Allianz Best Styles Global Equity, 35% Guinness Global Innovators, 10% GS Global Credit (Hedged), 10% UOB High Grade Corporate Bond, 10% Guinness Global Equity Income
- Stated FMC (fund management fee): **1.30% p.a.**

### The total distribution cost (TDC) table — directly from FWD's own illustration

| Policy year | Age | Total premiums paid | Total distribution cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 35 | $72,000 | **$9,026 (12.5%)** |
| 15 | 40 | $108,000 | $10,144 (9.4%) |
| 20 | 45 | $144,000 | $11,698 (8.1%) |
| 25 | 50 | $180,000 | $13,683 (7.6%) |
| 30 | 55 | $180,000 | $15,841 (8.8%) |
| 35 | 60 | $180,000 | $18,000 (10.0%) |
| 40 | 65 | $180,000 | **$20,158 (11.2%)** |

**TDC keeps growing for the entire life of the policy — even after premium payments stop at year 25.** That's the structural flaw: there's no "0% supplementary charge from Y11" cliff like APA has.

### The total deductions table — at 8% projection

| Policy year | Age | Value of premiums paid (8%) | Total deductions to-date |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 35 | $108,770 | **$33,457 (30.8%)** |
| 25 | 50 | $548,904 | $106,520 (19.4%) |
| 40 | 65 | $1,741,215 | **$434,343 (24.9%)** |

By age 65, **~25% of the prospect's hypothetical compounded value is gone to deductions.** The surrender value at age 65 is $1,306,872 on $180,000 premiums paid — looks good in isolation, but the equivalent APA setup at the same premium and time horizon projects materially higher because APA's deduction curve cliffs to 0% supplementary from year 11.

### The TDC ratio comparison (the headline attack)

The structural one-liner: **APA's TDC ratio is roughly half of FWD's at the same horizon.**

- FWD: $20,158 TDC on $180,000 premiums = **~11%**
- APA equivalent: ~$18K TDC on ~$288K premiums = **~5-6%**

> *"FWD positions itself as the low-fee ILP. That's true in year 1 — their headline charge is lower than APA's first-10-year supplementary charge. But pull up their own illustration and walk to year 40. Their total distribution cost is 11.2% of premiums paid. APA's equivalent is around half that, because APA cliffs the supplementary charge to 0% from year 11. FWD never cliffs. They just keep deducting."*

### The cover-side gap (the second attack)

The FWD Invest First Summit sum-insured field reads "105% of policy value" — meaning the death benefit is effectively whatever the units are worth plus 5%. If the units crash, the cover crashes with them. That's not life cover — that's a refund.

> *"Look at your sum insured: 105% of policy value. If the market drops 30%, your life cover drops with it. APA's death benefit floor is 100% of total premiums paid, contractually guaranteed. Your worst-case payout in APA is what you put in. Your worst-case in FWD is whatever the units are worth that day."*

### The same-money APA comparison (verified APA numbers — 25-year-old, 10-yr IIP, 8% IIRR)

Run from `simulateTraditionalGrowth(600, 25, 65, 8)` with 10-yr IIP cap.

| Setup | Monthly | Total paid | At age 65 (8%) | Premium-to-value multiple |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FWD Invest First Summit (existing illustration) | $600 × 25 years | $180,000 | $1,306,872 surrender | **7.26x** |
| APA on 10-yr IIP (same monthly) | $600 × 10 years | $72,000 | $665,828 | **9.25x** |

Same monthly outflow. APA stops taking premium after 10 years; FWD keeps charging for 25.

**The killer line for the FWD prospect:** *"You pay $600 a month for 25 years to FWD, $180K total. You get back $1.3M at 65 — a 7.26x multiple on your money. With APA at the same $600/mo on a 10-year premium term, you only pay in $72K total and get back $666K — a 9.25x multiple. APA delivers more cash per dollar invested. And you stop paying after year 10, so years 11-25 your $600 a month goes wherever you want."*

**At the 4% projection floor:** APA returns $179,179 at 65 from $72K premium (2.49x). FWD's floor is meaningfully worse because of the perpetual deduction layer (run their own 4% column to confirm in the appointment).

### The restructure setup

For an FWD prospect already locked in: the math has to be run case-by-case (surrender vs continue, lost booster bonuses, etc.). For a prospect comparing FWD vs APA *before* signing — APA wins on TDC, on premium-to-value multiple, on the Welcome + Special Bonus stack (FWD has neither), and on the death benefit floor.

## Case C — The fragmented-coverage young adult (consolidation receipt)

**Profile:** 24-year-old, working professional. Already holds five separate plans across two insurers — small premiums each, no holistic plan, big gaps in protection.

### What they're paying

| Plan | Insurer | Monthly | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Singlife Choice Saver | Singlife | $296.30 | Endowment at 4.25% PIRR, maturity ~$38K at age 35 |
| Singlife Multipay CI | Singlife | $94.00 | $45K CI cover, $5K death |
| AIA CI plan (small) | AIA | $30.00 | $45K CI/ECI |
| AIA CI plan (larger) | AIA | $87.50 | $150K CI/ECI |
| AIA Investment | AIA | $300.00 | Investment plan |
| **Total** | | **$807.80/mo** | |

### The gap analysis (against planning norms for income protection)

| Need | Current cover | Norm |
|---|---|---|
| Death | $35,000 | 10-20x annual income |
| TPD | $34,500 | 10-20x annual income |
| Occupational Disability Income | Nil | 70% of annual income |
| Critical Illness | $45,000 | 5-10x annual income |
| Early Critical Illness | Nil | 5-10x annual income |
| Hospitalisation | Nil | Full private hospital |
| Accidental Death | Nil | 5-10x annual income |
| Accident Expense Reimbursement | Nil | $2K-$5K per accident |

Five policies, **$807.80/mo**, and still underwater on every major protection line. The Singlife Choice Saver at 4.25% PIRR is the worst offender — $296/mo going into a low-return endowment when the same money at APA's 8% projection nets a materially higher retirement number.

### The structural attack

> *"Let me draw out what you actually have. Five plans, $807 a month, across two insurers. Your Singlife Choice Saver is the biggest piece — $296 a month — and the projected maturity at 35 is $38,000. You're putting $61,000 in over 17 years to get $38,000 out at 4.25% projected. That's worse than CPF OA's guaranteed 2.5%.*
>
> *Meanwhile your death cover is $35,000 and your TPD cover is $34,500. If anything serious happened, you'd be exposed.*
>
> *Same $807 a month, restructured: keep the AIA CI policies because they're already paid into. Replace the Singlife Choice Saver and the AIA Investment with a single APA at $500/mo on a 10-year premium term — total premium $60K over 10 years. Projected at 65 at 8% is $545,463 (verified from the APA engine). At the 4% floor, $147,078. Versus the Singlife Choice Saver's projected $38,135 at 35 on $60,560 of premium paid in. The remaining $200-250/mo plugs the protection gap with a term policy at $500K-$1M, plus a hospital plan."*

### The restructure setup

| Plan | Old monthly | New monthly | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Singlife Choice Saver | $296.30 | $0 (paid-up or surrendered) | Cash value redirected as APA top-up |
| Singlife Multipay CI | $94.00 | $0 (replaced by AIA CI consolidation) | Premium freed |
| AIA CI ($45K) | $30.00 | $30.00 (kept) | — |
| AIA CI ($150K) | $87.50 | $87.50 (kept) | — |
| AIA Investment | $300.00 | $0 (rolled into APA) | Consolidated |
| **APA (new or upgraded)** | — | **~$500/mo on 10-yr IIP** | Wealth slot consolidated |
| **Term policy ($500K-$1M)** | — | **~$50/mo** | Protection gap closed |
| **Hospital plan (HSGM-style)** | — | **~$100/mo** | Hospitalisation gap closed |
| **Accident plan** | — | **~$15/mo** | Accident gap closed |
| **Total** | **$807.80** | **~$782.50** | Same monthly outflow, holistic coverage |

Same premium outlay. Five policies down to four. Death/TPD gap closed from $35K to $1M+. Hospital and accident gaps closed. Wealth slot moved from 4.25% endowment to 8% APA projection.

**The verified APA numbers (engine output):**

| Age | APA value at 8% | APA value at 4% (floor) |
|---|---|---|
| 35 | $70,394 | $58,889 |
| 45 | $143,082 | $82,069 |
| 55 | $279,367 | $109,866 |
| 65 | **$545,463** | $147,078 |

Premium paid: $60,000 (10 × $500 × 12). After year 10, no further APA premium — pure compounding to 65. Singlife Choice Saver cash value (if surrendered) can be added as a lump-sum top-up to accelerate the base.

If surrender penalty wipes the gain, paid-up status (stop paying, let it run to maturity, redirect the $296 elsewhere) is often the cleaner move.

## Quiz

1. **The structural difference between APA's supplementary charge and FWD Invest Plus's supplementary charge is:**
- A) APA's is higher overall
- B) APA charges 3.9% in Y1-10 then drops to 0% from Y11; FWD Invest Plus charges 1.2% perpetually from month 25 onward, never zero ✓
- C) Both are perpetual
- D) Neither charges a supplementary

**Why:** Verified from APA-Competitor-Analysis.pdf. APA's curve is "high early, then zero." FWD's is "low early, then perpetual." Over a 40-year hold, FWD accumulates significantly more in charges.

2. **A prospect has a whole-life policy at $400/mo and is undecided about restructuring. What's the BTIR setup you'd propose?**
- A) Surrender the whole-life and put everything into APA
- B) Keep $400/mo total, split into ~$50/mo low-cost term + ~$350/mo into APA on a 10-yr IIP ✓
- C) Increase the whole-life premium
- D) Buy a second whole-life

**Why:** BTIR separates the two jobs whole-life is doing badly. Term for cover (cheaper per dollar of cover); APA for accumulation (Welcome Bonus + Special Bonus stack + 100% allocation). The whole-life policy isn't auto-surrendered — the decision depends on cash value and surrender cost.

3. **When should you NOT recommend restructuring an existing whole-life policy into APA + term?**
- A) Whenever the prospect already has a policy
- B) When the whole-life is 15+ years in with substantial cash value, when the participating bonus structure is active, when the prospect values what they have, or when you can't show the math clearly ✓
- C) Only when AIA doesn't sell whole-life
- D) Only when the prospect asks

**Why:** Restructuring isn't free — there's a surrender penalty, lost in-flight bonuses, and a compliance burden to show the move is a net positive. If any of the four signals fire, don't push the restructure.

4. **The "invest the rest" part of BTIR almost always fails for self-directed investors because:**
- A) Term policies are too expensive
- B) People don't actually invest the difference with discipline, or they invest it in something that loses to inflation ✓
- C) BTIR is regulatory-restricted
- D) Singapore tax law penalises BTIR

**Why:** BTIR is conceptually sound but the investment side requires monthly discipline and a sensibly-allocated portfolio. APA fills that slot structurally — auto-debit, professionally managed, with the Welcome + Special Bonus stack DIY can't replicate.

5. **A prospect compares APA against a robo-advisor charging 1% perpetual. The structural counter is:**
- A) APA has no charges
- B) APA's supplementary charge is higher early (3.9% Y1-10) but drops to 0% from Y11; the robo's 1% perpetual on a $1M portfolio at retirement costs $10K/year forever ✓
- C) Robo-advisors are illegal in Singapore
- D) APA returns are guaranteed

**Why:** The math: 1% × $1M = $10K/yr forever. APA's curve is high early, zero from Y11. Cumulative charges over a 40-year hold heavily favour APA — and the Investing Success deck slide 40 shows APA pulls ahead from policy year 17 onwards on a $12K-a-year premium scenario.
