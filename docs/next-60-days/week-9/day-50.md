# Day 50: Closing II, assumptive, choice and urgency

> **Today in one line:** Different prospects respond to different closes. Know all three, and know which one fits the person in front of you.

**What to remember**

1. Use the assumptive close on a D whose trial closes came back clean: go straight to the logistics.
2. Use the choice close on an S or a C once they've agreed to go ahead, with two or three real options.
3. Use the urgency close with a deadline the client can check for themselves: an insurer's notice, their own dates or their health.
4. A good close confirms a yes you earned earlier in the meeting.

## Three closes for the main ask

[Day 49](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/49) covered trial closes (early and repeated) and the main close (the moment you ask for the decision). Today is about three ways to phrase that main close. Each one puts a different kind of pressure on the moment, so pick the one that suits the person.

![The 3 mid-tier closes: Assumptive (assume the yes); Choice (A or B?); Urgency (now vs later)](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-50-m0.webp)

| Profile | Works well | Use with care |
|---|---|---|
| D | Trial, assumptive (best), urgency with a real deadline | Choice (too many options), keep any summary short |
| I | Trial, summary with the story, assumptive after a warm yes | Choice (they hedge), urgency (feels pushy) |
| S | Trial, choice (best, feels safe), reassurance | Assumptive (pressure), urgency (they withdraw) |
| C | Trial, choice (they like options), summary with data | Assumptive (pushy), urgency unless it's real and documented |

The most common Week 9 mistake is reaching for the assumptive close because it feels confident. Most of your prospects won't be D profiles.

## The assumptive close

You skip the "will you?" and go straight to the logistics, on the basis that the yes has already happened.

- "Great, I'll go ahead with the application now, and the first premium goes straight to AIA by card or GIRO, whichever you prefer."
- "Since you want both girls covered, I'll set up a plan for each of them, correct?"
- "If everything looks good, would Tuesday evening or Saturday morning work better to sign?"

The second line only works because the client said earlier that they want both children covered. The assumptive close confirms a decision they've already made. It doesn't slip in one they haven't.

It suits D profiles, prospects whose trial closes all came back clean, and later-stage prospects you've met more than once.

It backfires in three places:

- S profiles feel pressured. They either agree to keep the peace and back out within 48 hours, or pull away there and then.
- C profiles find it pushy, and it sends them back into analysis.
- Cold or early prospects haven't built any momentum for it to ride on.

## The choice close

You lay out two or three clear options and the prospect picks one. Any pick is a yes to the decision underneath.

- "We've looked at two structures: A is about $400 a month for $500K of cover, and B is about $550 a month for $750K, and both fit your budget. Which feels right for where you are now?"
- "Would you prefer to pay annually or monthly? Annual usually works out a little cheaper over the year (the quote shows the exact difference), while monthly is easier on cash flow."
- "Would Tuesday at 7pm or Saturday at 10am work better for signing?"

The figures are only an example, so use the numbers from the client's actual quote.

It suits S profiles (control without pressure), C profiles (they like comparing) and prospects who hedge at a single ask.

It backfires with D profiles, who usually want your best recommendation and find a comparison slow. It also backfires with prospects who haven't decided whether to go ahead at all. Asking "A or B?" before they've agreed to the decision underneath feels like a trick. The choice close is for how, once they've agreed to whether.

### Two options or three

Two options are simpler and quicker, which suits S profiles and faster meetings. Three give a C the range they like to see, and people often settle on the middle one. Only show options that genuinely fit the client. Adding a plan you'd never recommend so the other one looks better is steering, and it has no place in needs-based advice.

Stop at three. With more choices people often put the decision off. Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper's 2000 jam study is the famous example, though later studies found the effect varies a lot from one situation to the next.

## The urgency close

You point to something with a real time limit that makes acting now better than acting later. Real urgency comes from three places:

- product changes the insurer has actually announced, such as a premium revision with a date on it
- the client's own timing, such as a baby, a wedding or a job change where waiting has a real cost
- the client's health, where a delay could change how the application is underwritten (say this once, factually, and never use a health scare as a lever)

Here's how each sounds:

> You: "You mentioned wanting this in place before the baby arrives. Once AIA has everything it needs, it can take up to 30 days to accept, counter-offer or decline the application, and any change in your health before it's accepted has to be declared. Starting this week gives us room."

> You: "AIA has announced a premium revision for this plan from [date on the notice]. Here's the notice. If you were going to go ahead anyway, it makes sense to do it before then. If not, that's fine too."

> You: "You told me you're changing jobs in March and your company cover stops when you leave. If we start the application now, we can aim to have your own cover sorted before that date."

Each of these points to something the client can check: their own dates, or a notice in writing. Before you use the second one, confirm with your manager exactly how the revision applies to applications already in progress, and never estimate how much prices will go up.

It suits D profiles, who respect a real deadline, and prospects who have been on the fence for weeks and need a genuine reason to decide.

It backfires with S profiles, who feel pressured and pull back, so even a real deadline needs soft wording with an S ("there's no rush at all"). C profiles check the deadline, and if it doesn't hold up you lose their trust for good. With anyone at all, invented urgency backfires.

### The rule on urgency

Never make up a deadline. People remember being pressured into a decision they later regretted, and they tell their friends. MoneySense tells consumers they're never obliged to buy on the spot, and your close should say the same thing.

If none of the three real sources apply, use the assumptive or choice close instead. And if the client says no to a real deadline, that's their decision. They also get 14 days after receiving the policy to cancel for a refund, so nobody needs to be rushed into signing.

## Using several closes in one meeting

You can use different closes at different points in the same meeting. Here's a presentation to an S-profile parent:

1. Trial close during the presentation: "Does the 20-year term feel right, or would a shorter term suit you better?" (a choice trial)
2. Trial close after the recommendation: "If we did go ahead, would you want to submit the application this month or next?" (an assumptive trial)
3. Main close, using choice: "We've looked at Plan A at about $400 a month and Plan B at about $550. Which fits your situation better?"
4. Reinforcement after the yes, with the paper-flip close from Day 49

The main close is where they decide, and the trial closes built up to it.

## What the close is not

The close isn't a trick. Closing means asking for the decision the prospect has already been moving towards. If you're trying to convince someone at the close, the real work didn't get done earlier: the discovery questions ([Days 43](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/43) and 44), the hot buttons ([Days 37](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/37) to 39) and the trial closes (Day 49).

From the outside, a good close looks almost boring, because the prospect is ready and the close confirms it. If your closes keep turning into showdowns, you're leaving the decision too late.

The close is also where onboarding starts, and Week 10 picks up from here. Moving straight on to the next prospect after a signature is the Year 1 habit that kills referrals.

## Do this today

1. Take the prospects you'll present to this week and write each one's DISC profile.
2. Pick the main close for each from the table, and write the exact line you'll use.
3. For any urgency close, write down the fact behind it. If there isn't one, switch to assumptive or choice.

## Quiz

**Q1. The assumptive close fits best with:**
- A) Every prospect equally
- B) D profiles and later-stage prospects whose trial closes were clean (correct)
- C) S profiles
- D) Prospects who say *"let me think"*

**Why:** The assumptive close needs a prospect who has already been leaning forward, which is what clean trial closes tell you, and a D profile likes the decisive momentum. With an S or a C it feels like pressure and usually leads to them pulling back or re-analysing. Reaching for it by default is the most common Week 9 mistake.

**Q2. The rule on the urgency close is:**
- A) Use it on every close to speed things up
- B) Never make up a deadline. Only use urgency when the time limit is real (correct)
- C) Keep it for high-net-worth clients
- D) Avoid it completely

**Why:** Real urgency (an announced product change, the client's own life events, their health) is legitimate and useful to them. An invented deadline such as *"this offer ends tomorrow"*, when it doesn't, is a fast way to wreck your reputation, and prospects who felt pressured tell their friends. If there's no real deadline, use the assumptive or choice close.

**Q3. Using several closes in one meeting means:**
- A) Using the same close three times
- B) Using different closes at different points: trial closes during the meeting, a main close at the end, reinforcement afterwards (correct)
- C) Closing on several products at once
- D) Having backup closes ready

**Why:** A good meeting has several close moments: 3 to 5 trial closes, one main close and often a reinforcement like the paper-flip afterwards. Each close suits a different point in the meeting. An assumptive trial feels natural mid-meeting, a choice main close gives the prospect control, and social proof backs up the yes.

**Q4. What's the most options to put in a choice close?**
- A) 2 only
- B) 3 at most. More options often lead people to put the decision off (correct)
- C) 5 to 7, to be thorough
- D) No limit

**Why:** Two options suit S profiles and quick meetings. Three give a C the range they like. Past three, people tend to defer because there's too much to weigh up. The choice close works because the choice is small.

**Q5. Legitimate reasons for urgency include all of these EXCEPT:**
- A) A product change the insurer has announced, such as a dated premium revision
- B) The client's own timing, such as a baby, a wedding or a job change
- C) The client's health, where a delay could change underwriting
- D) *"This offer is only for people who decide today"*, with nothing real behind it (correct)

**Why:** Options A, B and C are real facts outside your control that make now better than later. Option D is invented scarcity. Prospects, and C profiles especially, spot it quickly, and trust goes when they do.

**Q6. The urgency close backfires most often on:**
- A) D profiles
- B) S profiles, because urgency feels like pressure and they withdraw (correct)
- C) Prospects who just closed their first case
- D) C profiles who asked for urgency

**Why:** A D respects a real deadline. An S hears urgency as pressure, which is exactly what they want to avoid. Even a real deadline needs softer wording with an S: *"There's no rush at all. The notice says the revision starts on the 1st, so if you do want to go ahead, let me know by the end of the month. If not, that's completely fine."* That keeps them in control.

**Q7. "A good close looks almost boring from the outside" means:**
- A) Good closers have no charisma
- B) A good close asks for the decision the prospect is already moving towards. If closes feel dramatic, you're leaving the decision too late (correct)
- C) The prospect should look bored
- D) Close language should be flat

**Why:** A dramatic close ("this is the moment, decide now!") means the hot buttons and trial closes didn't do their job earlier. In a well-run meeting the prospect is leaning yes well before the end, and the main close just confirms it. If every close feels like a showdown, you're trying to do at the end what should have happened during discovery and the trial closes.
