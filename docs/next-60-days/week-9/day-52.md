# Day 52: The 3 magic questions

> The one idea for today: most objections aren't what they sound like. Three questions find the real concern before you spend your answer on the wrong one.

By the end of today you'll be able to:

- use the 3 magic questions to find the real objection behind what the prospect says
- tell a hard no (accept it) from a soft no (work with it) from a hidden concern (bring it out)
- diagnose before you respond, so you don't answer the wrong objection

---

## Why objection handling goes wrong

The prospect says *"too expensive."* The new FC launches straight into justifying the value: *"but look at what you get for that premium..."*

*"Too expensive"* is often about something other than price. It might mean that:

- they can't justify it to their spouse
- they don't see the need clearly yet
- they're comparing it with something else entirely, like investing on their own
- the value hasn't landed, and they can't see what the premium buys them

If the real objection is *"I don't see the need,"* defending the premium won't help. You have to go back and rebuild the need.

The 3 magic questions are a diagnostic. They find the real objection before you put effort into the wrong one.

---

## The 3 magic questions

![The 3 magic questions: Q1, is there anything else? Q2, suppose that wasn't a concern. Q3, what would need to be true?](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-52-m0.webp)

### Magic question 1: *"Is there anything else?"*

When to use it: right after the prospect raises an objection.

How to say it:
> *"Got it, that's a fair concern. Before I answer it, is there anything else giving you pause, or is that the main thing?"*

What it does: it brings out objections they haven't said yet. The first objection is often a decoy, because it's easier to say out loud than the real one. Asking *"anything else?"* invites the bigger one.

What you might hear:

- *"No, that's the main thing."* Now answer that objection with confidence.
- *"Actually, there's also..."* Whatever follows is often the real objection, so deal with it first.

### Magic question 2: *"Suppose that wasn't a concern..."*

When to use it: once they've stated their objection, plus anything that came out of Q1.

How to say it:
> *"Suppose the premium wasn't an issue, and the monthly amount was exactly where you wanted it. Would you go ahead? Or is there something else underneath?"*

What it does: it tests whether the stated objection is the real one. If they say *"yes, if the premium worked, I'd go ahead,"* the objection is genuine, and you can look at adjusting the cover to fit their budget. If they say *"well, even then I'd want to think about..."*, you've found the deeper concern.

It works because it lets the prospect name the real block without losing face. They don't have to admit they were holding something back. They're only answering a hypothetical.

### Magic question 3: *"What would need to be true?"*

When to use it: when the prospect is hedging but won't give you a concrete concern.

How to say it:
> *"For you to feel confident saying yes, what would need to be true? What would you need to see from me to feel sure this is the right decision?"*

What it does: it gets them to spell out how they'll decide. You've been guessing at what they need, and now they tell you.

What you might hear:

- *"I'd want to see it side by side with the other quote I got."* Now you have something concrete to deliver. Put the two plans side by side on the same cover and term using the insurer's documents or compareFIRST, and describe the other plan accurately.
- *"My wife would need to agree."* That's your next meeting, so book it now.
- *"I don't know, I just need time."* That's an honest signal that you need the follow-up close from Day 41.

## The diagnostic sequence

When the objection is unclear, use all three in order.

### Step 1: magic question 1
> *"Is there anything else?"*

- Yes: bring out the deeper objection.
- No: go to step 2.

### Step 2: magic question 2
> *"Suppose that wasn't a concern. Would you go ahead?"*

- Yes: the stated objection is the real one, so deal with it.
- No: there's a deeper issue, so go to step 3.

### Step 3: magic question 3
> *"What would need to be true?"*

- You get their actual decision criteria, and now you know where to go.

Three questions take about 90 seconds. At the end you know the real objection and what it would take to resolve it. Skip them and you can spend ten minutes on the wrong concern.

## Hard no, soft no or hidden concern

After the three questions, you'll be in one of three places.

### Hard no: accept it

The signs: they're firm and specific, and the objection is about fit, not price or features. *"I don't want insurance at all,"* said after a full fact-find, is a real no.

What to do: accept it gracefully, leave the door open, and ask if you can check in later.

> *"Completely understand, and thanks for being straight with me. If things change in a year or two, or you have a question down the line, just message me. Would you mind if I checked in with you sometime next year?"*

If they say no to that too, respect it. Pushing past a second no is pressure, and it damages the relationship. Accepting a hard no well keeps the door open for Year 2 and beyond.

### Soft no: work with it

The signs: the objection is specific and something you can address, like price, the length of the term, a particular feature or the timing.

What to do: deal with that specific concern using ART (Day 40). Don't overdo it. Give it one or two turns, then close again.

### Hidden concern: bring it out

The signs: what they say doesn't match how they seem. They look unsettled but the words are bland, or Q1 and Q2 brought out something deeper.

What to do: address the hidden concern. Put your effort there, not into the words on the surface.

## The special case: *"let me think"*

*"Let me think about it"* is vague by definition, so it almost always needs all three questions.

### The full sequence

> *Prospect:* *"Let me think about it."*
>
> You, Q1 adapted: *"Totally fair, take the time you need. Before you do, is there something specific giving you pause, or is it more of a general feeling that you want to sit with it?"*
>
> - *"Something specific"*: deal with that concern.
> - *"More general"*: go to Q2.
>
> You, Q2 adapted: *"Let me ask it another way. If the plan were exactly right, would you go ahead today, or would you still want to sit with it?"*
>
> - *"I'd go ahead"*: there's a specific concern, so bring it out.
> - *"I'd still want to sit with it"*: go to Q3.
>
> You, Q3 adapted: *"Help me understand. What would need to be true a week from now, that isn't true today, for you to feel ready?"*
>
> This is usually where the real answer comes. Either they name something concrete (*"my spouse needs to see it"*), which becomes your next step, or it turns out the *"think"* was a way of putting the decision off. In that case, use the follow-up close from Day 41 and book a specific date.

---

## Don't skip the diagnosis

The temptation is to go straight to handling the objection. You've prepared a good answer on price, so when they say *"too expensive,"* you want to use it.

Hold off. Spend the 90 seconds on the three questions first. You'll often find the real objection is something else, and your prepared answer was aimed at the wrong target.

Those 90 seconds save you ten minutes of answering the wrong thing. They also protect you from the worst outcome: the prospect who said *"too expensive,"* got a speech about value, and switched off because price was never the problem.

---

## Keep a record of what you learn

After every meeting where you used the three questions, write down:

- what the prospect said first (the surface objection)
- what Q1 brought out (any hidden objection)
- what Q2 told you (real or decoy)
- what Q3 gave you (how they'll actually decide)

After 20 meetings you'll start to see patterns. *"Too expensive"* from your kind of prospect will usually mean one of a few things, and so will *"let me think"*. Knowing those patterns makes you faster in every meeting after that.

## Objection-handling library

The 3 magic questions tell you *which* objection is the real one. Once you know, these Academy scripts cover each family.

### Objection families at a glance

| Family | What it looks like | Script |
|---|---|---|
| Push-away or not interested | A reflex defence in the first 30 seconds | "Not interested in insurance"; Young Adults all-objections |
| "I already have someone" | An existing adviser or a family member in insurance | "Already have an advisor" objection script |
| Format or channel pushback | "Send me an email", or video off on Zoom | Video-Off (Zoom); Texting EQ 4-step |
| Cost of delay or hesitation | "Let me think", "bad timing" | Cost of Delay (Pre-Retirees) |
| Recruitment | Recruitment leads only | Recruitment objection handling |

## Quiz

**Q1. The 3 magic questions are designed to:**
- A) Handle every objection with the same script
- B) Find the real objection before you put effort into the wrong one ✓
- C) Close harder
- D) Buy time in the meeting

**Why:** Surface objections like *"too expensive"*, *"let me think"* or *"I need to discuss with my spouse"* often stand in for a deeper concern. The three questions (anything else, suppose that wasn't a concern, what would need to be true) find the real block in about 90 seconds. Without them you answer the wrong objection.

**Q2. Magic question 2 (*"Suppose that wasn't a concern, would you go ahead?"*) works because:**
- A) It corners the prospect
- B) It lets the prospect name the real block without losing face ✓
- C) It removes the objection completely
- D) It forces a yes

**Why:** A prospect hiding a deeper concern behind a price objection often can't say it directly, because it would feel like admitting they weren't being straight. Q2 is a hypothetical, so it gives them an easy way to answer. *"Well, even then I'd want to think about..."* is easy to say in reply to that question and hard to offer unprompted.

**Q3. After a full fact-find, a prospect firmly says *"I don't want insurance at all."* The right response is:**
- A) Run all 3 magic questions to find the real objection
- B) Push back with a rebuttal
- C) Accept the hard no gracefully, leave the door open and ask if you can check in later ✓
- D) Offer a discount

**Why:** A firm objection about fit, not price or features, is a hard no, and trying to overturn it damages the relationship. Accepting it well keeps the door open for later, because people's circumstances change and they come back to the adviser who respected their answer. Running the diagnostic on a hard no feels disrespectful, and a discount makes their no look like a bargaining tactic.

**Q4. In order, the 3 magic questions are:**
- A) "What's the problem?", then "Why can't you decide?", then "When?"
- B) "Is there anything else?", then "Suppose that wasn't a concern, would you go ahead?", then "What would need to be true?" ✓
- C) "Are you ready?", then "Are you sure?", then "Really?"
- D) "How much can you afford?", then "By when?", then "Why not today?"

**Why:** Each question has its own job. Q1 brings out hidden objections, Q2 tests whether the stated objection is the real one, and Q3 gets the prospect to spell out how they'll decide. The order matters: "anything else" goes first because first objections are often decoys, and Q3 comes last when the first two haven't settled it.

**Q5. Why does magic question 1 (*"Is there anything else giving you pause?"*) matter?**
- A) It fills time
- B) The first objection is often a decoy, because it's easier to say out loud than the real concern ✓
- C) It shows empathy
- D) The framework requires it

**Why:** Prospects often give an easy objection to cover a harder one. *"Too expensive"* is easier to say than *"I don't trust you yet"* or *"my spouse will be upset if I commit to this."* Asking *"anything else?"* invites the real concern without accusing them of hiding it. Skip it and you end up answering the decoy while the real block stays put.

**Q6. You've spent ten minutes on what you thought was the real objection and the prospect still hesitates. The likely problem is:**
- A) You need to push harder on the same objection
- B) You skipped the diagnosis and answered the wrong objection, so run magic question 2 now ✓
- C) The prospect is being irrational
- D) The product isn't right

**Why:** Ten minutes on an objection that won't clear is a sign you've been aiming at the wrong target. Magic question 2 (*"suppose [the objection] wasn't a concern, would you go ahead?"*) tests that quickly. If they'd still hesitate, the real block is somewhere else, so go back to Q1 or Q3 to find it.

**Q7. Why are the 90 seconds of diagnosis worth it?**
- A) They fill meeting time
- B) They save ten or more minutes of answering the wrong thing, and avoid the prospect switching off because you missed their real concern ✓
- C) They impress the prospect
- D) The framework requires them

**Why:** 90 seconds spent finding the right target beats ten minutes of answering the wrong one, followed by a failed close. The pull is to skip the diagnosis because you have a good answer ready. The habit to build is to diagnose first and answer second, however sure you are about the objection.
