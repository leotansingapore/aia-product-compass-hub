# Day 25: Why new FCs under-ask

> **Today in one line:** Staying quiet about referrals feels polite. In practice it costs you the head start your Year 2 pipeline needed.

**What to remember**

1. Asking in Year 1 builds Year 2: ten cases with 3 warm names each can mean about 12 referral cases before you do any new outreach.
2. You can answer each of the 4 blocks: you're offering the friend something, a delivered result is your proof, tone decides how it sounds, and the ask comes from you.
3. A quality referral comes with your client's personal recommendation, and three vouched-for friends are worth more than 20 numbers.
4. Treat the referral ask as seriously as the sale at every appointment, so your list refills as fast as you use it.

## The compound math

Here's an illustration with round numbers. They aren't a forecast, and your own will differ.

A new FC closes 10 cases in Year 1. She asks for referrals properly, and each case gives her about 3 warm names. That's 30 warm leads going into Year 2.

If 40% of those become clients, she starts Year 2 with 12 cases from referrals before she's done any new outreach. The same thing happens again in Year 3, on a bigger base.

Her colleague closes the same 10 cases and never asks. He starts Year 2 with nothing in the referral column. Every new case has to come from his own outbound hours, and those run out.

Asking is the only difference between them, and it starts in Year 1.

## Why new FCs don't ask

Four blocks get in the way, roughly in the order they show up.

### Block 1: "I'm asking for a favour."

The reflex is to think you're taking something from the client. That makes the ask feel extractive, so it comes out awkward, and the awkwardness kills it.

Turn it round. You're offering something to the client's friend. If your work genuinely helps people, a referral is how that friend gets access to it, and your client is the one who can make the introduction.

An advisor with 17 years in the business always delivered for his clients and never asked for referrals. He qualified for MDRT three times on outbound effort alone.

In year 17 he changed one habit. He started asking on every case, with a proper script and timing (the FACT Method from [Day 28](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/28)). He qualified for MDRT several years running after that, and he says the ask was the main thing he did differently.

He told his mentor the block broke when he saw the ask as a responsibility:

> "If I actually believe my work helps people, it's my responsibility to make sure the people around my clients get access to it. The ask isn't about me."

When the ask is about you, it feels like taking. When it's about the friend who hasn't been helped yet, you feel you owe it to them.

### Block 2: "I haven't earned it yet."

New FCs tell themselves they need more proof before they can ask: more time, more cases, more testimonials. They picture some future version of themselves who has earned the right.

The best moment to ask is right after you've delivered something real for this person. Six months later the feeling has faded and the ask gets awkward. The outcome in front of the client is all the proof you need.

You don't need a closed case to start, either. Anyone you've had a real conversation with, at a Fact-Find or a warm coffee, is fair to ask. This works with warm contacts who haven't bought anything:

> "I'm just getting started and building my practice. Do you happen to know anyone at X stage?"

### Block 3: "They'll think I'm desperate."

Desperation lives in how you say it. "Who else should I be talking to?" sounds desperate with a trailing voice, rushed pacing or an awkward pause before it.

Said with certainty tonality ([Day 5](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/5)) at a natural point in the meeting, the same line sounds professional. That's why the practice this week is about how you ask.

### Block 4: "I don't want to seem pushy."

In Singapore this one is real. People here tend to recommend indirectly. Recommending a financial adviser over dinner isn't something most people do unprompted, even when they're delighted with you.

So the passive flow many advisors hope for ("my work will speak for itself") mostly doesn't arrive. If you wait for the client to start the referral conversation, most referrals will never happen.

That's why the ask has to come from you. Taking charge of it is the responsible thing to do for people who would benefit from meeting you.

Advisors who build a regular referral routine, with Day 28's FACT script and Day 29's flywheel check, get out of the Year 1 grind much faster than advisors waiting for recommendations.

## Quality referrals vs cold leads in disguise

A common mistake: an FC collects 20 names from every client, calls all 20 cold and converts about one. He calls it a referral pipeline, but those were cold calls with a client's name attached.

| | Quality referral | Cold lead in disguise |
|---|---|---|
| What happened | The client personally recommended you, and the friend agreed to hear from you | The client handed over a name with no recommendation |
| Your opener | "Hi, Amir said you might be open to a chat..." brings the relationship with it | The contact treats it like a cold DM, and often blocks you |
| Rough rule of thumb | Many book a Fact-Find | Very few book a Fact-Find |
| Relationship risk | Low, because Amir made the intro openly | High, because it feels to the contact like Amir gave their number away |

Get the recommendation, even if it costs you 15 of the 20 names. There's a legal side too. Under the PDPA, a friend's contact details should reach you with that friend's consent, and the referrer should confirm it when they pass them on.

## What asking actually looks like

Days 26 to 28 teach the mechanics. The routine looks like this:

- When: during the admin paperwork at the end of a closed case, while the meeting is still warm.
- Setup: "Okay, I'll take 5 to 10 minutes to process this. While I do, let me tell you what a lot of people do with this quiet stretch..."
- Ask: a specific angle (an age group, a life stage, a type of person) instead of "anyone you know?"
- Follow-up: a date you set for checking back, agreed at the moment you ask.

## The self-reinforcing and self-destructive cycles

### The see-saw effect (self-destructive cycle)

Every appointment uses up a lead from your list. More appointments means more leads used, so more time spent finding new ones, which leaves less time for appointments. Push harder on one side and the other side drops.

![The see-saw effect (self-destructive cycle): Getting leads; Calling; Appointments](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-25-m0.webp)

Left alone, the list runs dry, often somewhere around month 6 to 9, and the calendar empties. The FC usually blames themselves or the market. What actually happened is that nothing was replacing the names they used up.

### The self-reinforcing cycle

Referrals fix the see-saw. When every appointment produces at least one warm referral, the list refills as fast as you draw it down.

![The self-reinforcing cycle: Referrals; Calling; Appointments](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-25-m1.webp)

Now it runs the other way. More appointments bring more referrals, which bring more calls and more appointments. Advisors who keep this going for a few years spend far less time on cold outreach.

### The shift in ask priority

This leads to a rule that sounds backwards at first: at every appointment, treat the referral ask as seriously as the sale. The sale is one case, while the referrals from that meeting can lead to several more.

New FCs put all their energy into closing. Top producers make sure they ask as well. You see the difference around month 9, when the new FC's pipeline is dry and the top producer's list is full of names that clients have vouched for.

In practice:

- Ask at every appointment, even the ones that felt only okay.
- Track referral asks as a pipeline KPI on your CAR scorecard from [Day 3](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/3).

## Quiz

**Q1. The reframe that breaks the *"asking is a favour"* block is:**
- A) *"The client owes me after what I did for them"*
- B) *"I'm offering something to the client's friend, and the client is the bridge"* (correct)
- C) *"Everyone does it, so I should too"*
- D) *"If I don't ask, I won't eat"*

**Why:** B is the honest version. The friend doesn't have access to your work yet, and your client can make the introduction, so you're saying *"your friend would benefit"* and never *"you owe me a name."* A treats the client as owing you. C leans on "everyone does it", and D is desperation that shows in your tone.

**Q2. Quality referrals differ from cold leads because:**
- A) Quality referrals only come from HNW clients
- B) Quality referrals carry a personal recommendation from the referring client, and cold leads don't (correct)
- C) Quality referrals close within a week, and cold leads take months
- D) Quality referrals pay higher commission

**Why:** The recommendation is the whole difference. A name handed over with no recommendation gets treated like a cold DM. Three friends your client has vouched for beat 20 numbers, and calling people who never agreed to hear from you can hurt your client's standing with them.

**Q3. Most Singapore clients don't refer on their own, even when they're delighted, because:**
- A) The work wasn't good enough
- B) Recommending service professionals unprompted isn't the local habit, so the passive referral flow many advisors hope for doesn't arrive by itself (correct)
- C) They're hiding wealth
- D) They don't have enough friends

**Why:** People here tend to recommend indirectly and rarely bring up their adviser at dinner unprompted. Advisors who rely on *"my work will speak for itself"* fall behind advisors who run a clear, structured ask. Owning the ask is realistic about how people here behave, and it isn't pushy.

**Q4. The compound math: you close 10 cases in Year 1, each gives about 3 warm referrals, and 40% of those convert. Before any new outreach, Year 2 starts with:**
- A) 0 cases, because referrals don't carry across years
- B) About 12 cases from referrals alone (correct)
- C) 30 cases
- D) The same as Year 1 without referrals

**Why:** 10 cases x 3 referrals x 40% = 12 Year 2 cases before you prospect a single cold lead. Ask zero times in Year 1 and Year 2 starts from zero, so you repeat Year 1's effort all over again. The numbers are an illustration, but the shape holds: the asks you make in Year 1 are what give you a head start later.

**Q5. "I haven't earned the right to ask yet" (Block 2). The reframe Day 25 offers is:**
- A) Ask anyway, because clients expect to be pushed
- B) Right after you've delivered a real outcome is the best moment to ask, well before the feeling fades (correct)
- C) Wait until you've closed 10 cases
- D) Only ask after the client says thank you

**Why:** New FCs convince themselves a future version of them has to earn the ask. That future never arrives, and the outcome in front of the client already counts as proof. Wait 6 months and the feeling fades and the ask gets awkward. Straight after you deliver, while they're still grateful, is the easiest moment you'll get.

**Q6. A new FC has closed 0 cases so far. Can they still ask for referrals this week?**
- A) No, you need closed cases first
- B) Yes, any Fact-Find, warm coffee or proper conversation is a fair moment to ask (correct)
- C) Only if the mentor is present
- D) Only after Week 8

**Why:** Waiting for closed cases puts a 6-month delay on referrals that a new FC can't afford. Anyone you've had a real conversation with is fair to ask. *"I'm just getting started and building my practice. Do you happen to know anyone at X stage?"* works with warm contacts who haven't bought anything. Every ask is practice, whether or not you've closed.

**Q7. The Singapore "pushy" block (Block 4) reframes as:**
- A) Stop asking, because it really is pushy
- B) Ask more softly
- C) Not pushy, responsible: if you wait for the client to start the referral, most referrals never happen (correct)
- D) Only ask clients from other countries

**Why:** Worrying about seeming pushy makes advisors wait for recommendations that mostly don't come. If you believe your work helps, you can't leave that help stuck behind someone else's reluctance to bring you up. Owning the ask fills the gap the culture leaves open.
