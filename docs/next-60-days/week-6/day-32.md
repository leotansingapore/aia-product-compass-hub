# Day 32: The D profile

> **The one idea for today:** With a D, get to the point in your first sentence. A long intro loses them before you reach the fact-find.

By the end of today you'll be able to spot a likely D within a few minutes from body language, dress, word choice and the questions they ask. You'll know the 4 behaviours that work with a D (confidence, recommendations, options and the full range of scenarios) and the 4 mistakes that lose one. And you'll have a D pitch that gives the 60-second version before any deep dive.

---

## What the D is looking for

A D sits down across from you asking two questions:

1. *"Does this person know what they're doing?"* (competence)
2. *"Are they going to waste my time?"* (efficiency)

Everything else comes second. Warmth is nice to have, and stories only help if they prove you know your stuff. A long build-up makes you look like you're hiding something or don't know what to recommend.

A D runs out of patience quickly. You have roughly 2 to 3 minutes to show competence and set the pace, or they mentally check out and the rest of the meeting is a formality.

---

## How to spot a D: the 5-signal scan

Within 5 minutes of sitting down, check:

| Signal | D tells |
|---|---|
| Body | Stands straight, firm steps, takes up space, direct eye contact, head up when thinking |
| Pace | Fast: walks fast, talks fast, wants things done fast |
| Questions | Bottom-line questions: *"What do I actually need to do?"* *"How much, how long, what do I get?"* |
| Words | *"Cut to the chase."* *"What's the return?"* *"Let's move."* *"Skip the details."* |
| Dressing | Sharp and clean-cut, expensive without being flashy (the weakest signal, so never rely on it alone) |

When 3 or more line up, you're probably with a D. Match their speed by cutting your intro, your pacing and your small talk roughly in half.

---

## The 4 behaviours that work

### 1. Be confident, efficient and resourceful
- Arrive 5 minutes early
- Have the fact-find agenda printed or ready on your iPad
- When they ask a question, answer it without hedging. If you don't know, say *"I'll find out and get back to you by [specific day and time]"*, then do it

A D will forgive you for not knowing something on the spot. What they won't forgive is a promise to find out that you never keep.

### 2. Recommend, and skip what they already know
A D wants your recommendation, and a 20-minute lesson on how insurance works gets in the way of it.

Weak: *"So there are three types of critical illness cover, let me walk you through each..."*
Better: *"Based on what you've told me, I'd go with A over B, and here's why in one sentence. Do you want the details, or are you happy to move on that?"*

### 3. Give options on the solution
A D needs to feel in control, so take that seriously.

Weak: *"The only way to solve this is X."* (This gets pushback even when X is right.)
Better: *"There are two sensible routes, A and B. Here's the trade-off. Which fits you better?"*

### 4. Show the range of outcomes
D's think in scenarios, so show them more than the middle case, taking every number from the benefit illustration and product summary:

- For a participating plan, the illustration shows projected values at two investment rates set under Life Insurance Association (LIA) guidelines, currently 3.00% and 4.25% a year. Show both, and say clearly that neither is guaranteed.
- For an investment-linked plan, walk through what a sharp market fall in the early years would do to the fund value, and what cover stays in place while it recovers.
- Then show the guaranteed values, which are what the plan pays even if the projections don't happen.

A D respects the downside figure most, because it shows you've thought about risk.

---

## The 4 mistakes that lose a D

### 1. Long intros and forced rapport
Warming up a D feels polite to you. To them it looks like stalling. Skip the weather chat, ask *"shall we get started?"* and start.

### 2. Hedging
*"Maybe,"* *"it depends,"* *"we could consider..."* all sound unsure to a D. Even when there are genuine trade-offs, give your recommendation first and add the caveat after.

Weak: *"We could consider A, or maybe B, it depends on your situation..."*
Better: *"I'd go with A. The main trade-off is [X], which matters if [Y] applies to you. Does it?"*

### 3. Story-led pitches
Stories that work well on an I fall flat with a D. *"Let me tell you about a client who..."* gets half their attention at best. Lead with numbers, your recommendation and the outcome. Keep stories for a specific objection where a real, relevant example helps.

### 4. Missing the moment they've decided
D's decide fast, especially on big-picture points. When they say *"okay, let's do it"*, they've decided. Don't re-pitch, re-explain or ask again if they're sure. Move to the next step: *"Great. Here's what I need from you to set it up. Would you like to pay by GIRO or credit card?"*

Re-pitching after a D has decided can talk them back out of it.

---

## D blind spots that can hurt the meeting

Four habits D's bring into the room:

1. They boss people around without realising it. Don't take it personally, it's just how they talk.
2. They hate being challenged head-on. If you disagree, say *"one thing to consider is..."* instead of *"actually, that's wrong."*
3. They don't understand why others aren't as quick as they are. If you need time to work something out, tell them why. D's respect a process once you name the reason.
4. When they blow up, it's loud. It's rarely about you, usually about whatever they're impatient with. Don't raise your voice to match. Stay calm and it passes.

---

## Common D objections and how to answer

A D's objections come sharper and faster than other profiles'. Three you'll hear:

### *"This is taking too long."*
Weak: apologise and carry on at the same pace.
Better: *"Got it. Let me cut to the three things that matter most for you: [X, Y, Z]. We can handle the rest over email."*

### *"What's the bottom line?"*
Weak: *"Well, there are several factors to consider..."*
Better: *"Bottom line: $500K of critical illness cover plus $500 a month into the savings plan. That covers your biggest risk and builds the buffer you asked for. Do you want to go ahead, or shall I explain why each piece is there?"*

### *"I don't make decisions on the spot."* (rare for a D, but it happens)
Weak: push harder.
Better: *"Understood, that makes sense. What would you need to see before Thursday to decide?"* This gives them control, which they need, and still sets a date.

---

## The D pitch: same plan, different framing

The recommendation stays the same all week: $500K CI cover plus a $500-a-month savings plan. For a D, you open with the bottom line.

### D opening (90 seconds)

> *"Based on what you've shared, two kids, you're the main earner and cash flow is a bit tight, I'd recommend $500K of critical illness cover plus a $500-a-month savings plan. That covers your biggest risk and builds the buffer you said you need. You've got three choices: go ahead, adjust the CI amount, or rethink the savings piece. Which one?"*

The recommendation comes first. The options give them control, which is their core need, and you finish with a direct question. No story, no warm-up.

### D close: assumptive

Once a D has clearly agreed to the recommendation, skip the *"so will you?"* step and move to the practical details:

> *"Since you have two girls, I'll set up plans for both of them, correct?"*
>
> *"Tuesday 7pm, same place, to sign?"*
>
> *"GIRO or credit card?"*

This fits because a D expects things to keep moving, and assumptive language treats the decision as made. Only use it after the fact-find, once they've actually said yes. The client still gets the product summary and benefit illustration, and still hears the key terms explained before signing. Being brisk with a D never means skipping those.

Avoid procedural closes with a D (too slow) and long reassurance (it sounds like you're hedging). Use a follow-up close only as a last resort, because a D who was pitched well usually decides in the meeting.

## Quiz

**Q1. With a D profile, the 5-minute scan should look for:**
- A) Warm smile, open body language, story-telling
- B) Direct eye contact, fast pace, bottom-line questions, efficient clean-cut dressing ✓
- C) Frowning when thinking, process-oriented questions, detailed note-taking
- D) Shy posture, soft voice, agreeable answers

**Why:** A describes an I, C describes a C and D describes an S. D signals are about speed, directness and results. If you're looking, you'll notice them almost at once: they walk in with momentum and ask *"so what do we need to decide today?"* in the first 2 minutes.

**Q2. The worst move you can make with a D after they've said *"okay, let's do it"* is:**
- A) Move to the next step immediately
- B) Re-pitch or re-explain why the decision is a good one ✓
- C) Thank them
- D) Send the paperwork that evening

**Why:** D's decide fast and expect you to respect it. Re-pitching sounds like *"are you sure?"*, which plants doubt that wasn't there, and it can talk them out of the decision. Move on to the next step (*"Great. Here's what I need from you..."*) so the decision stands and things keep moving.

**Q3. A D says *"this is taking too long."* The best response is:**
- A) Apologise and keep going at the same pace
- B) Cut to the 3 things that matter most and move the rest to email ✓
- C) Explain why thoroughness matters
- D) Offer to reschedule

**Why:** A ignores what they told you, C defends a process the D doesn't care about, and D stops the meeting cold. With B you take the feedback seriously, shrinking the rest of the agenda to what matters and offering email for everything else, which is the kind of pace change a D respects.

**Q4. A D runs out of patience quickly. In the first few minutes of a meeting, the D is judging:**
- A) How much you'd charge per hour
- B) *"Does this person know what they're doing?"* and *"Are they going to waste my time?"* ✓
- C) Whether you'd be a good friend
- D) Your family background

**Why:** A D's first filter is competence and efficiency. Warmth is optional, and stories only help if they prove you know your stuff. If the first 2 to 3 minutes don't show both, the D checks out and the rest of the meeting is a formality.

**Q5. Hedging fails with a D because:**
- A) D's dislike the word "maybe" specifically
- B) *"Maybe,"* *"it depends,"* *"we could consider..."* sounds unsure; even when there are real trade-offs, a D wants the recommendation first and the caveat after ✓
- C) D's take it personally
- D) It's considered bad manners

**Why:** A D wants your recommendation delivered with confidence, with the trade-offs attached. *"I'd go with A. The main trade-off is X, which matters if Y applies"* gives them a clear call and is still honest about the trade-offs. *"Maybe A or maybe B, depends on your situation"* sounds like you don't know, and trust drops fast.

**Q6. When you show a D the range of outcomes, you should include:**
- A) Just the middle case
- B) Both illustrated projection rates, the guaranteed values and the downside; the downside figure earns their respect ✓
- C) Only the higher projection
- D) A single number with no context

**Why:** D's think in scenarios, so showing only one case looks like you haven't thought it through. Showing the downside and the guaranteed values tells them you've already tested the plan. Take the figures from the benefit illustration, which uses the LIA illustration rates, and say plainly that projections aren't guaranteed.

**Q7. A D raises their voice mid-meeting about something frustrating. The best move is:**
- A) Match their volume to show you can stand your ground
- B) Stay calm and measured; it's rarely about you and usually passes ✓
- C) End the meeting
- D) Apologise profusely

**Why:** A D's outburst is usually about whatever they're impatient with, like a policy, a delay or some red tape, and matching their volume only makes it worse. Stay calm and it blows over, and the D often carries on as if nothing happened. Apologising for something you didn't cause (D) comes across as weak and costs you some of the trust you've built.
