# Day 35: C profile

> **Today in one line:** With a C, skip the story and bring numbers you can defend line by line. Every claim has to survive a close look.

**What to remember**

1. A C wants rigour: arrive on time, open with an agenda, and back every claim with a figure.
2. Start with the downside: the lower illustrated rate and the guaranteed values come first.
3. Help them compare, and correct any error the moment you notice it.
4. Book the follow-up 7 to 14 days out, and expect to close at the second or third meeting.

## What the C is looking for

A C comes in with three questions:

1. "Are the facts in this presentation correct?"
2. "Is this good value, or am I being oversold?"
3. "What could go wrong, and what happens if it does?"

A C wants rigour. They'll question every number, check it against their own research and take longer to decide than any other profile.

A C may well do more research after your meeting than you did before it. If they catch you in a factual error or an inconsistency, you lose their trust, possibly for good. The upside: a C you've convinced tends to stay, and refers other careful thinkers.

## How to spot a C: the 5-signal scan

| Signal | C tells |
|---|---|
| Body | Frowns when thinking. Straight face, even at your jokes. Long pause before answering. Can seem cold when focused. |
| Pace | Slow and deliberate. Stops to consider. Rarely interrupts. |
| Questions | Why and how questions about process: "Why this fund?" "Why this timeline?" "How can you be sure?" "What's the track record?" |
| Words | "Sounds too good to be true." "I don't make decisions on the spot." "Let's look at the track record." "Let's play safe." "I need to compare." |
| Dressing | Practical, clean and plain, nothing flashy (the weakest signal on its own) |

If 3 or more fit, assume a C. Slow down, drop the stories and expect the questions to take longer than the presentation.

## The 4 behaviours that work

### 1. Be professional and objective: on time, with an intent statement and a structure

A C notices whether you arrived on time, set out the meeting at the start and seem organised, and whether your recommendation came from a process or a hunch. The intent statement from [Day 7](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/7) matters more with a C than with anyone else, because it shows you have a process, and process is how a C thinks.

- Arrive 5 minutes early
- Open with a short, structured intent statement
- Name the agenda before you start
- Signpost as you go: "we're moving on to the proposal now"

### 2. Frame everything as value for money

A C cares less about feeling good about a plan and more about whether it's the best financial decision. Speak in those terms:

- Weak: "This plan gives you peace of mind."
- Better: "Here's what you pay each month, what the cover pays out and what's guaranteed versus projected. If you want to compare, compareFIRST, the comparison site run by the Life Insurance Association, lists term and whole life plans from different insurers side by side. We can look at it together."

Use real numbers from the benefit illustration and maths they can repeat. Never quote another insurer's figures from memory, or claim your plan beats the market without proof.

### 3. Start with the downside

A C looks for what could go wrong before what could go right, so get there first:

> You: "Let's start with the less favourable case. At the lower illustrated rate, here's what the policy would be worth in year 10, and here's what's guaranteed no matter what. For the investment-linked part, here's what a sharp market fall early on would do to the fund value, and here's what cover stays in place. Now let's look at the higher rate."

It shows you've already looked for the weak spots, which saves them the trouble of catching you out.

### 4. Give data, facts, figures and comparisons

An I tunes out after 2 minutes of data, but a C gets more interested. Give them:

- Detailed coverage tables
- Year-by-year values from the benefit illustration
- Past fund performance, with a clear reminder that it doesn't predict future returns
- A side-by-side comparison with similar plans, for example from compareFIRST
- Your assumptions stated plainly. For a participating plan, the illustration uses two rates set under LIA guidelines, currently 3.00% and 4.25% a year, and neither is guaranteed.

Bring printed documents, and offer to email the product summary and benefit illustration so they can check the maths themselves.

## The 4 mistakes that lose a C

### 1. Hedging or claims you can't support

Saying "this is the best plan" without evidence gets you cross-examined. "This has X benefit" with no figure behind it invites "how do you know?" An unsupported "best" claim is also something MAS fair dealing expectations tell you not to make.

Every claim you make to a C needs a table, a figure or a specific case behind it. If you don't have one, say "I don't have that with me, I'll email it by Monday" instead of improvising, and then send it.

### 2. Emotional pitches

Stories work on an I and feelings work on an S. To a C, both feel like an attempt at manipulation. The C thinks "this person is trying to get past my analysis with feelings", and their trust drops.

Keep the pitch factual even when you're tempted to add a story. The exception is a worked example with real numbers ("a 42-year-old with the same cover, here's how the claim would pay out"), which works because it's a data point.

### 3. Pressure to decide

A C doesn't decide on the spot. They need time to research, compare and think. Push a C to decide today and you get one of two results:

- A soft yes that falls apart when they go over the numbers at home
- Instant rejection: "this person is pushy, so the plan probably isn't good"

So never ask a C to commit in the first meeting. Book the follow-up 7 to 14 days out, let them research, and close at the next meeting. A C usually decides at the second or third meeting.

### 4. Getting a detail wrong and not catching it

One wrong fact costs you far more with a C than with anyone else, because they start wondering what else you got wrong. If you spot an error mid-meeting, say so straight away:

> You: "Sorry, a correction to what I said earlier. The figure is $1,240, not $1,340. Let me recheck the rest."

Correcting yourself on the spot wins back trust that staying quiet would lose.

## C blind spots

Seven habits a C brings:

1. They see one answer: they treat their own analysis as the answer, so you may need to offer other ways of looking at it.
2. They listen to reply: they're often planning their next question while you talk, so check they understood.
3. They need to be right, so if you contradict them, do it gently and with data.
4. They can use rigour as cover: a C can justify hesitating by calling it thoroughness. Sometimes you need to gently push back: "I hear that you want more data. What would be enough? Some things we'll only find out once we start."
5. They sound negative, but they raise downsides because they're seriously considering the plan, so don't read the tone as a no or take it personally.
6. They guard their own field. If they're an engineer, they know engineering maths, so respect that.
7. They miss feelings: they often don't pick up emotional signals, so don't assume they noticed your change in tone.

## Common C objections and how to answer

"Let me compare with the market." Expected, and sensible, so don't fight it.

- Weak: "I assure you this is the best option available."
- Better: "Absolutely, that's the responsible thing to do. Can I make it easier? compareFIRST lists term and whole life plans from different insurers side by side, and I can show you how to read it. If another plan suits you better, I'd honestly rather you knew. Shall we look at it together and meet again once you've compared?"

"What if [specific edge case]?" A C loves edge cases.

- Weak: "That's unlikely to happen."
- Better: "Good question. Let me walk you through exactly what happens in that case." Then do it precisely, with the policy document open. If you don't know, say "I don't know offhand, but I'll get you the exact clause by Monday", and send it by Monday.

"I'll think about it." Expected, because a C rarely decides in the meeting.

- Weak: pressure them to commit today.
- Better: "Completely understandable. A few things that might help: here's a one-page summary you can go through, I've emailed you the benefit illustration and product summary, and let's book a follow-up for next Thursday at 7pm. If you have questions by then we'll go through them, and if not we can go ahead. Does that work?" A week or more to digest it, plus a follow-up already booked, is how a C prefers to decide.

## The C pitch: same plan, different framing

It's still $500K CI cover plus a $500-a-month savings plan. For a C, you open with structure.

### C opening (90 seconds)

> You: "Here's the plan for the next 45 minutes. First, 10 minutes on my recommendation. Then 15 minutes on how I got there: the numbers, the assumptions and the other options I considered. The last 20 minutes are for your questions, and I've brought the compareFIRST comparison for similar plans so we can test it. My recommendation is $500K of critical illness cover plus a $500-a-month savings plan, and there are two assumptions I want to flag that may or may not hold for you. Shall we start?"

Naming the assumptions upfront and building in a comparison matters because a C values an organised process as much as the content.

### C close: procedural with a follow-up

> You: "It makes sense that you'd want to review this before deciding. I'll send you the one-page summary, the benefit illustration with the assumptions marked, and the compareFIRST comparison we looked at. Let's book a follow-up for next Thursday at 7pm. If you have questions by then we'll work through them, and if the numbers check out, we go ahead. Does that work?"

The procedural part gives them a process, and the follow-up sets a decision point 7 to 14 days out, so you're helping their analysis instead of fighting it.

Don't use an assumptive close with a C, where you go straight to the paperwork as if they've said yes. It feels pushy and sends them back to analysing from scratch, which can lose you the deal. Avoid urgency too ("sign today or lose the rate"), because a C reads it as sloppy.

## Quiz

**Q1. With a C profile, the best opener is:**
- A) A warm story about a client
- B) *"Let me start with the less favourable case, here's what the plan looks like at the lower illustrated rate and what's guaranteed..."* (correct)
- C) *"Trust me lah, this is the best plan out there"*
- D) *"Let me tell you why I love this product"*

**Why:** A C looks at the downside first, so starting there shows you've already thought about what could go wrong. Option A's story falls flat, option C damages trust with a claim you can't back up, and option D is about your feelings with nothing behind it.

**Q2. If you realise mid-meeting that you misstated a fact to a C, you should:**
- A) Hope they didn't notice and move on
- B) Correct it straight away: *"A correction to what I said earlier, the figure is X, not Y"* (correct)
- C) Wait until the end to clarify
- D) Email the correction later

**Why:** A C notices details and takes notes. Stay quiet and they'll either catch it themselves or start doubting everything else you said. Correcting it straight away shows the rigour and honesty a C values most.

**Q3. A C says *"I need to compare with the market."* The best response is:**
- A) *"I assure you this is the best option available"*
- B) *"Let me help. compareFIRST lists similar plans side by side, and I can show you how to read it"* (correct)
- C) *"We can talk about that next meeting"*
- D) Push them to decide today anyway

**Why:** A C will compare whether you help or not. Offering to help shows you're not hiding anything and puts you on their side of the analysis, which makes them more likely to come back to you.

**Q4. A C typically decides:**
- A) At the first meeting
- B) At the second or third meeting, after they've researched, compared and thought it over (correct)
- C) Within 20 minutes
- D) Only through referrals

**Why:** Push a C to decide in the meeting and you get soft yeses that fall apart at home, or a yes that gets cancelled later. Book the follow-up 7 to 14 days out, let them check and compare, and close at the next meeting.

**Q5. Emotional pitches and stories work well with an I and reasonably with an S. With a C, they:**
- A) Work the same as with an I
- B) Feel like manipulation; the C thinks *"this person is trying to get past my analysis with feelings"* and trust drops (correct)
- C) Are required for compliance
- D) Help close faster

**Why:** A C judges rigour first, so a story with no numbers behind it feels like a trick. The exception is a worked example with real numbers (*"a 42-year-old with the same cover, here's how the claim would pay out"*), which works because it's a data point told as a story. General feel-good stories cost you with a C.

**Q6. In a C meeting, every claim you make:**
- A) Should be delivered with confidence whether or not you have data
- B) Needs something behind it, such as a table, a figure or a specific case; if you don't have it, say so and send it by Monday (correct)
- C) Can be improvised for emphasis
- D) Should be memorised word for word

**Why:** A C checks claims, and improvising (option C) gets caught. The honest *"I don't have that with me, I'll email it by Monday"*, followed by actually sending it, builds more trust than any improvised answer.

**Q7. A C sounds pessimistic and negative. The right way to read that is:**
- A) They're not interested
- B) They aren't trying to be negative; it's how a C engages, and it's easy to mistake for disinterest, so don't take it personally (correct)
- C) They're testing you
- D) They'll never buy

**Why:** A C raises the downsides first. They wouldn't bother pointing out risks if they weren't seriously considering the plan. Treating the tone as a no (A, D) ends conversations that were heading somewhere, so keep supplying rigour.
