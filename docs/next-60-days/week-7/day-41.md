# Day 41: Pitch mechanics, confidence and closing

> **Today in one line:** People say yes far more often when you ask with confidence than when you hedge. What you need is a handful of verbal habits and a close that suits the profile.

**What to remember**

1. Ask directly. Hedged phrases ("maybe we could...") sound like doubt, so swap out the 3 you use most.
2. Give a real reason with "because" for anything bigger than a small favour.
3. Lift the prospect's certainty in the product, in you and in the firm to 9 or above, then use the transition line before you close.
4. Match the close to the profile: Assumptive for a D, Reassurance for an I, Procedural for an S or C. If they leave unsigned, book the follow-up before they go.

## The 70% rule

> "When salespeople demonstrate confidence and ask assertively for what they want, prospects say yes about 70% of the time."

That line is from sales trainer Jeb Blount's book Fanatical Prospecting (2015). The 70% is his figure from years of watching sales teams, not the result of a controlled study, so treat it as a rule of thumb. It rests on two things:

1. You believe the prospect will want what you're offering.
2. Your language matches that belief.

When the two line up, the prospect tends to pick up your certainty. When they don't (you believe in it but sound tentative, or the other way round), the prospect senses the mismatch and stalls. Your default frame should be "of course this is happening" instead of "I hope this happens", and it shows in the first few words of every sentence.

## Swapping hedged phrases for direct ones

Same content, different framing. Read both columns aloud and listen to the difference:

| Hedged (weak) | Direct (strong) |
|---|---|
| "Is this a good time?" | "The reason I'm calling is..." |
| "Would you maybe want to consider..." | "What we're doing next is..." |
| "What do you think?" | "Why don't we go ahead and set that up?" |
| "Maybe we could meet sometime next week..." | "Tuesday 7pm or Thursday 11am, which works?" |
| "I was just wondering if..." | "Quick question..." |
| "We could perhaps consider adding..." | "I'd add X here, because [Y]." |
| "I'm sorry to bother you..." | "[Name], I've got something worth 3 minutes of your time." |

Hedged phrases sneak an apology into the frame, and the prospect hears "I don't fully believe what I'm about to say." Direct phrases leave the apology out, and the prospect reads that as confidence.

This week's drill: pick the 3 hedged phrases you use most. Replace them with direct ones and rehearse until the direct version is what comes out under pressure.

Direct is about how you ask. What you claim still has to be accurate, and the prospect can still say no.

## The power of "because"

In a 1978 study, psychologist Ellen Langer and colleagues had someone ask to cut in line at a photocopier. For a small request (5 pages), 60% agreed when no reason was given. Adding "because I'm in a rush" took it to 94%. Even the empty "because I have to make copies" got 93%.

For a bigger request (20 pages), the empty reason did nothing (24%, the same as no reason), and only a real reason helped (42%). So "because" works on small asks almost by itself, but a bigger ask needs a real reason. Buying a policy is a big ask, so give real reasons.

| Without because | With because |
|---|---|
| "Can I have 15 minutes of your time?" | "Can I have 15 minutes of your time, because I'd like to understand your current setup?" |
| "Can we book Thursday?" | "Can we book Thursday, because I want to go through the comparison with you in person?" |
| "Would you sign here?" | "Would you sign here, because that gets your application to underwriting this week?" |

Cover normally starts once AIA accepts the application and the policy is in force. Signing the form doesn't start it, so never tell a client it does.

## The Assumptive Position: the frame under every close

The Assumptive Close below is one tactic. Underneath every tactic sits the **Assumptive Position**, a posture that sounds like this in your head:

> "What I'm asking for is going to happen. My job is to make it easy."

In practice:

| You expect | So you |
|---|---|
| The meeting to be booked | Offer 2 specific times instead of asking when they're free |
| The Fact-Find to happen | Arrive 5 minutes early with the agenda printed |
| The recommendation to be acted on | Bring the application form to the presentation |
| Referrals to come | Ask at every appointment |

Many sales trainers rank this posture above any single technique. It doesn't guarantee a yes, but it shapes every decision you make around the yes you expect, and most tactics land better from it than from "let's see what happens."

You can't keep up confident language on top of hopeful belief, because the doubt leaks through. Get the posture right and the phrases follow.

Assuming the yes is about how you prepare. It never means skipping the needs analysis, rushing the client past their questions, or treating a hesitant client as agreed.

## Building certainty before you close

From Day 40: the prospect needs to be near a 9 or 10 on all three certainties (product, you, firm) before any close works. Your pitch is a structured climb up those three scales.

### The certainty scale

Picture a line from 1 to 10. At 10 they'd buy today without being asked, and at 1 they wouldn't take it free. Every prospect starts the meeting somewhere on this line for each of the three, often around 3 or 4.

Your pitch has to lift all three to 9 or above before you ask for the order. If one is stuck at 5 or below, the close brings out the cover-up objections from Day 40, and looping is the only way back.

![The certainty continuum: Prospect at 3-4 on all three 10s; Raise Product certainty; Raise You certainty; Raise Firm certainty; Re-ask for the order](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-41-m0.webp)

### What builds each certainty

| Certainty | How you build it in the pitch |
|---|---|
| Product | Tie the recommendation to the hot button they shared in the Fact-Find, show the math ("this $X a month closes the $Y gap you mentioned"), and compare it fairly with the alternative they're considering. |
| You | Real listening during the Fact-Find. Your service commitment: "What you can expect from me is [X]. You'll hear from me [how often]." A client story only if it's genuinely yours, with no names or identifying details (and if you don't have one yet, don't invent one). |
| Firm | One fact about AIA's claims record or history, taken from AIA's own published materials. A claim you've handled yourself, if you have one. If you don't, you can tell one of your manager's with their permission, as your manager's case, with nothing that identifies the client. |

Every pitch should touch all three. Weak pitches only cover the product, and average ones cover product and you. Strong ones cover all three, so they need less looping.

### The transition line

Once you've covered all three and you're about to recommend, one line tells the prospect a recommendation is coming:

> "Based on everything you've shared with me, this is a good fit for you. Let me tell you why."

Or shorter:

> "Given what you told me earlier, here's what I'd do in your position."

It does three things at once:

1. It ties the recommendation to their words, which lifts product certainty a notch.
2. It positions you as an adviser rather than a salesperson, which lifts certainty in you.
3. It signals the plan was built for them, which is what justifies the premium that follows.

Then walk them through the recommendation, including the benefits, the risks, the costs and what happens if they stop paying or surrender early, and move into one of the four closes. Don't ask for the order without the transition. Even when certainty is high, a close without it feels abrupt.

## The 4 closes

Four closes to keep ready, each suited to different profiles and moments. Every one of them ends in a question ("correct?", "credit card or GIRO?", "shall we proceed?", "are we good to go ahead?"). The close doesn't state that they're buying. It asks a question that assumes they are. [Day 43](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/43) and [Day 44](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/44) go deeper on building those questions, and [First 60 Days, Day 47](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/47) has the 3-point checklist for testing one.

| Profile | Works well | Avoid or use with care |
|---|---|---|
| D | Assumptive (best), plus a short Reassurance, or Procedural if they trust the process | Follow-up is rarely needed |
| I | Reassurance (best), or Assumptive after a warm yes | Procedural is too dry, and a Follow-up only works if it's within days, before interest fades |
| S | Procedural, Reassurance, Follow-up | Assumptive feels like pressure |
| C | Procedural (best) and Follow-up (the default for C), with Reassurance only when it comes with numbers | Assumptive feels pushy |

The mismatch that kills the most deals is the Assumptive close on an S or a C. The S either agrees to keep the peace and quietly backs out within 48 hours, or refuses outright. The C goes back to analysing from scratch and often rules you out.

### Close 1: Assumptive

Skip the "will you?" and go straight to the logistics.

> You: "Since you want both girls covered, I'll set up a plan for each of them, correct?"
>
> You: "Tuesday 7pm, same place, to sign?"
>
> You: "Credit card or GIRO?"

Suits D profiles, who expect decisive momentum, and sometimes I profiles once they've already said yes. Don't use it on an S (it feels like pressure) or a C (it feels pushy and sends them back into analysis).

### Close 2: Procedural

Walk them through the next steps in order, with no ambiguity.

> You: "Here's how it works. First we complete the application together, then AIA's underwriters review it, and once it's approved the policy is issued to you. For the first premium you can pay by GIRO, card or a cheque made payable to AIA Singapore Private Limited. Once those steps are done, the plan is in place."

Payment always goes to AIA directly, never to you personally. AIA doesn't allow its representatives to collect cash on its behalf.

Suits C profiles, who respect the structure, and S profiles, who get a clear process with no pressure in it. Don't use it on an I, who finds it dry and tunes out.

### Close 3: Reassurance

Deal with their specific hesitation, reassure them, and move forward.

> You: "Alan, of everything we looked at, this is the one that fits what you told me you wanted: [their reason]. And you get 14 days after you receive the policy to review it and cancel for a refund if it isn't right. Let's take the first step today."

A longer version that deals with an objection:

> You: "Jenny, I understand the hesitation. Your job situation is uncertain, and that's real. So let's check this properly before anything else. You told me your savings cover about six months of expenses, and this premium is $150 a month. If you'd rather not commit that right now, we can start with a smaller amount of cover and add more once work settles. What matters is closing the biggest gap without stretching you. Which feels right?"

Suits I and S, who both decide with emotional confirmation. Don't use it on a D (too long), and with a C only when the reassurance comes with numbers, such as the figures in the benefit illustration.

Check affordability properly. A plan the client can't keep paying fails them, and MAS expects your recommendation to fit their finances. Don't claim a plan is the best, the most popular or a bestseller unless you have published figures to back it.

### Close 4: Follow-up

This is the last resort, for when closes 1 to 3 didn't land in the meeting. It suits a C, who needs research time, and an S, who needs processing time. Never make it your default close, because a follow-up without a fixed date is where decisions go to die.

Before they leave:

1. Ask an Iceberg question: "If there's one thing that might be holding you back, what would that be?"
2. Book the follow-up there and then, a named day and time: "Thursday 7pm, same place." Never an open-ended "I'll text you."
3. Put it in their calendar while you're together, so it's real.

For the follow-up itself:

1. Hold it within 3 days. Any longer and the interest fades.
2. Send a reminder 24 hours before: "Looking forward to Thursday. Quick recap of what we talked about..." That reminds them why they were interested.
3. Open with the decision: "Over the past few days you've had a chance to look through the recommendation. Are we good to go ahead?" Don't open with "Do you have any questions?", which invites a fresh round of objections. If they do have questions, answer every one properly. They can always ask; you just don't open the meeting by inviting it.

2. Pick your three pitches for this week's KPI. For each, note the prospect's DISC profile, the close you'll use, and the close you'll avoid.
3. Write your transition line and say it out loud until it comes out naturally.

## Quiz

**Q1. The 70% rule says:**
- A) 70% of prospects will close regardless of language
- B) When salespeople show confidence and ask assertively, prospects say yes about 70% of the time, far more often than when the ask is hedged (correct)
- C) 70% is the highest close rate possible
- D) 70% of objections come from price

**Why:** The 70% is Jeb Blount's rule of thumb for what happens when belief and language line up. Direct framing (*"what we're doing next is..."*) lets the prospect pick up your certainty. Hedged framing (*"maybe we could perhaps consider..."*) signals doubt, and the prospect mirrors it. How you phrase the ask is one of the easiest things a new FC can change.

**Q2. The 4 closing techniques are:**
- A) Trial, Alternative, Urgency, Assumptive
- B) Assumptive, Procedural, Reassurance, Follow-up (correct)
- C) Hard, Soft, Indirect, Direct
- D) Open, Close, Reveal, Commit

**Why:** Assumptive skips the permission step and goes to logistics. Procedural walks them through the steps in order. Reassurance deals with their specific hesitation. Follow-up is the last resort when the others didn't land, and it anchors the decision to a second meeting so the prospect doesn't drift.

**Q3. The biggest deal-destroyer across profiles is:**
- A) Too much data
- B) Using Assumptive Close on an S or C (correct)
- C) Offering too many options
- D) Not being confident enough

**Why:** The Assumptive close (*"credit card or GIRO?"*) suits a D's decisiveness. To an S it feels like pressure, so they agree to keep the peace and back out within 48 hours. To a C it feels pushy, so they re-analyse from zero and often rule you out. Matching the close to the profile matters more than any other choice you make at this stage.

**Q4. A C-profile prospect says *"let me think about it."* Your best close is:**
- A) Assumptive, push for the decision now
- B) Reassurance, tell them everyone chooses this plan
- C) Procedural + Follow-up: walk through the process and book a specific follow-up meeting to close (correct)
- D) Urgency, tell them the offer expires Friday

**Why:** C's genuinely need time to research, and pushing breaks their process and loses the deal. The best close for a C supports their analysis (Procedural, with the data) and books a follow-up within 3 days, so they get their time and you have a fixed date. Invented urgency (D) is the fastest way to lose a C, and it's a pressure tactic you shouldn't use on anyone.

**Q5. The Assumptive Position differs from the Assumptive Close because:**
- A) They're the same thing
- B) The Assumptive Position is the *posture* (*"what I'm asking for is going to happen; my job is to make it easy"*), while the Assumptive Close is one specific tactic inside that posture (correct)
- C) The Position is for beginners; the Close is for seniors
- D) The Position is about price

**Why:** The close is a technique for the last 30 seconds of a meeting. The position is the expected-yes frame that shapes how you arrive, how you offer options and how you prepare the paperwork. Tactics land better from it because your language reflects what you believe, and confident words can't sit on top of hopeful belief without the doubt showing.

**Q6. Reassurance Close (address the hesitation, reassure, move forward) lands best on:**
- A) D and C
- B) I and S (correct)
- C) Only I
- D) Only S

**Why:** I's respond to warmth and confirmation, and S's respond to gentle reassurance about their specific worry. Both decide with emotional confirmation, which is what this close gives them. D's find it too long. C's reject it unless it comes with real figures, such as the numbers in the benefit illustration.

**Q7. Follow-up Close: when you re-connect within 3 days, you should NOT open with:**
- A) *"Ready to proceed?"*
- B) *"Here's a recap of what we discussed"*
- C) *"Do you have any questions?"* (correct)
- D) *"I believe you've reviewed the recommendation. Are we good to move forward?"*

**Why:** *"Any questions?"* as an opener invites a new round of objections that weren't there 3 days ago. A better opener assumes they used the time to review and moves to the decision, with a recap (B) and a direct ask (A, D). If they do raise questions, answer them fully.

**Q8. Before any close technique can land reliably, the prospect must sit near a 9 or 10 on the certainty scale for:**
- A) The Product only
- B) The Product and You
- C) The Product, You, and the Firm, all three at once (correct)
- D) The Product, the price, and the timing

**Why:** All three need to be high together. A strong product pitch with weak rapport still gets *"let me think."* A great relationship with no confidence in the firm gets *"I want to compare providers."* Weakness in any one brings out the cover-up objections that only looping fixes.

**Q9. The transition line (*"Based on everything you've shared with me, this is a good fit for you. Let me tell you why."*) is designed to:**
- A) Buy you a few seconds while you think
- B) Tie the recommendation to the prospect's own words, position you as an adviser, and signal the plan was built for them, which lifts certainty in the product and in you at the same time (correct)
- C) Apologize for what's coming
- D) Give the prospect an out

**Why:** The line ties the recommendation to their words (product certainty), shows you listened (certainty in you), and signals the plan is specific to them, which justifies the premium. Skip it and the close feels abrupt, because the prospect was waiting for a "here's what I recommend" cue that never came.
