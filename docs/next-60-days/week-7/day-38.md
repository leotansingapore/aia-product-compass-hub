# Day 38: Hot buttons II

> **Today in one line:** You can only use a hot button the prospect has actually told you about, so today is about the questions that bring them out.

**What to remember**

1. Ask questions that make the prospect picture something specific. "What are your financial goals?" only gets a rehearsed list.
2. Start from the hot button they raised, then map it to the one part of the plan that deals with it.
3. Write each hot button down the moment it comes up, then quote it back in the pitch with their own numbers.
4. Press the hot buttons your plan actually fixes, and build the recommendation on real needs.

## Why surfacing is most of the work

You can only use a hot button that has come up. New FCs often stall here, because they ask "what are your financial goals?" and accept the flat answer.

That question is too direct. People give a rehearsed list ("save for retirement, protect my family, grow my money") that sounds like goals and has no feeling behind it. You can pitch to those answers all day without touching a real hot button.

Ask questions that can't be answered generically. A question that makes them recall a specific memory or picture brings the emotion up with it.

This is the right-question habit from [First 60 Days, Day 47](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/47#the-10-jobs-a-right-question--can-do). Check each question below against that lesson's three points: it leads to your objective, it's specific enough to force a specific answer, and it's logical and indisputable. "What are your financial goals?" fails the second one.

## 9 social hot-button questions

These fit anywhere: a warm-up coffee, the Fact-Find, a casual chat. They come across as interest in the person, and each one can bring up a hot button.

1. "What's one thing you couldn't live without?"
2. "What's the single biggest fear you have in life?"
3. "What's something that keeps you up at night?"
4. "What ongoing thing in your life frustrates you the most?"
5. "What are the top few things you look for when you pick a travel destination?"
6. "What are the top 3 things you look for in a partner?"
7. "If you could relive one experience from the past, what would it be?"
8. "If you have a bucket list, what's the one thing on it that's non-negotiable?"
9. "What are some personal values or beliefs you'd never compromise on?"

Use 2 or 3 per meeting, spread out, never all 9. Ask the frustration question early to find current pain, and save the bucket-list or values question for later in the meeting.

## 6 sales hot-button questions

These are about financial planning. Use them in the Fact-Find itself, late, once you've built rapport over the first 30 minutes.

1. "Suppose we never got you started on financial planning. What are the worst implications you can imagine for your future self?"
2. "If we manage to achieve all the financial goals we've set, how would you picture your dream retirement?"
3. "What are the top 3 things that matter most to you when choosing who to work with as an adviser?"
4. "Who would you be most worried about if you were gone early one day?"
5. "What's the worst experience you've had with financial planning or a financial adviser?"
6. "What do you think is the most important part of any good financial plan?"

Question 4 is usually the strongest. It goes straight to the loved-ones hot button, and most people have never actually pictured the answer.

After you ask it, stay quiet and let them think for as long as it takes. If you jump in with another question or rephrase it, you pull them out of the picture they're building. The silence feels uncomfortable, but that's when the hot button fires.

If question 5 turns up a bad experience with another adviser, listen and don't pile on. Criticising another adviser makes you look small, and it's a conduct problem too.

## Mapping hot buttons to product benefits

Once a hot button has come up, match it to a specific part of your recommendation.

| Hot button they raised | What it maps to |
|---|---|
| "My dad's stroke recovery is eating everything they saved" | Critical illness cover: a lump sum paid once the condition meets the policy definition, which the family can spend on income and caregiving that hospital insurance doesn't pay for |
| "I don't want my kids struggling like I did as a kid" | Term protection plus education savings, so they're covered if something happens and funded if nothing does |
| "I never want to rely on my kids financially when I'm older" | A retirement income plan on top of CPF LIFE, so income keeps coming in |
| "I work too hard to keep losing money to old policies I don't understand" | A review of what they already hold. That's your value as an adviser, before any product |
| "What if I get a critical illness and can't work? My whole family depends on my income" | Disability income cover alongside critical illness cover |

The payout conditions come from the policy wording. Under the LIA's 2024 definitions, for example, a severe-stage stroke claim needs a permanent neurological deficit confirmed by a neurologist at least 6 weeks after the event. Quote from the actual policy, never from memory.

Always start from the hot button. "Here's my CI plan, let me find a reason for you to want it" is the wrong way round. Say "You said X matters, and here's the part of the plan that deals with X", and the prospect feels heard instead of sold to.

## The callback technique

During the pitch, refer back to the hot button out loud:

> You: "When I was putting this together, I kept coming back to what you said earlier: 'I never want my kids struggling like I did.' That's why I'm recommending $500K of critical illness cover rather than the $250K I first pencilled in. You spend about $80K a year, so $250K covers roughly 3 years of your family's current life. $500K covers about 6. That gap is the difference between your kids having time to adjust and your kids having to give things up straight away."

A good callback has three parts:

1. Quote them directly: "what you said earlier, 'I never want...'"
2. Connect the product to the hot button using their own numbers.
3. Show the concrete difference. "3 years versus 6" lands where "more coverage is better" doesn't.

The numbers must come from their Fact-Find (their spending, their savings) and your firm's approved illustration. Never round up the gap to make a bigger case.

## Capture discipline: the notes habit

You can't call back a hot button you didn't write down.

- When one comes up, type 3 words into your notes straight away.
- Mark it with `**` or `[HB]`, for hot button, so you can find it later.
- Go back to those marks when you build the recommendation between the Fact-Find and the presentation.

Example notes from a Fact-Find:

```
Amir, 38, 2 kids (6 and 3)
- Spouse Ruth, works part-time
- Cashflow: $8K/mo, savings thin
[HB] Dad had stroke at 62, watching it eat their retirement
[HB] "I don't want Ruth going through what Mum is going through"
- Current coverage: 1 term, 2 ILPs (messy), CI $100K
[HB] Said "I grew up with not enough" (value-level)
```

At pitch time, those notes give you 3 specific hot buttons to refer back to. Keep notes like these on your firm's approved device or system, because they're a client's personal data.

## Hot-button hygiene

You bring hot buttons out to understand the prospect. Using them to work on the prospect is something else.

- Surfacing means you ask real questions, listen to the answers and build the recommendation on real needs. That's legitimate.
- Manufacturing means creating fear where there's no real gap. That's manipulation.

Only press on a hot button where the product deals with a real concern. If someone already has enough cover and a sound retirement plan, don't invent disaster scenarios to trigger loss aversion. Tell them they're in good shape and move on. MAS expects any recommendation to have a reasonable basis in the client's own needs, and fear you created doesn't count as one.

## Do this today

1. Pick 3 of the 9 social questions and 2 of the 6 sales questions you'd be comfortable asking. Say each one out loud until it sounds like you.
2. Set up a notes template with an [HB] marker for your next Fact-Find.
3. After that meeting, map each hot button to one part of the plan using the table above, and write the callback line you'll use in the pitch.

## Quiz

**Q1. The most powerful sales hot-button question from Section 3 is often:**
- A) *"What are your financial goals?"*
- B) *"Who would you be most worried about if you were prematurely gone one day?"* (correct)
- C) *"What's your budget?"*
- D) *"Do you have an existing advisor?"*

**Why:** "Section 3" is the list of 6 sales hot-button questions. B goes straight to the loved-ones hot button, and most prospects have never pictured the answer, so the silence after you ask it is the hot button firing. A gets rehearsed answers. B forces a specific picture, and the feeling comes up with it.

**Q2. Mapping a hot button to a product benefit means:**
- A) Finding the product you want to sell and inventing a reason they should want it
- B) Starting from a hot button they surfaced and naming the specific product element that addresses it (correct)
- C) Selling them the most expensive option
- D) Comparing your product to competitors

**Why:** The mapping runs from hot button to product benefit. Start from a product and backfill a reason, and the prospect feels sold to. Start from a concern they raised and show them the part of the plan that deals with it, and they feel heard. It can be the same product either way.

**Q3. The "callback" technique during pitch involves:**
- A) Calling the prospect back on the phone after the meeting
- B) Quoting them directly during the pitch and connecting the product to the specific hot button they surfaced (correct)
- C) Referring back to your own sales collateral
- D) Returning a referred lead's phone call

**Why:** In a pitch, a callback means repeating what the prospect said earlier: *"when I was putting this together, I kept coming back to what you said about [X]..."*. The direct quote, the link to a specific part of the plan and the concrete difference (*"3 years versus 6"*) make it personal. Most new FCs hear the hot button and forget it by pitch time, which is why you write it down.

**Q4. Why is *"what are your financial goals?"* a weak question for surfacing hot buttons?**
- A) It's too long
- B) Prospects give rehearsed answers (*"save for retirement, protect family, grow wealth"*) that sound like goals but carry no emotional heat (correct)
- C) It's too personal
- D) It's not financial-planning specific enough

**Why:** Ask about goals directly and people give the answer they think they should give. Questions that call up a specific memory or picture (*"what keeps you up at night?"*, *"worst experience with an adviser?"*) get past that rehearsed list to something real.

**Q5. After asking a strong hot-button question (like *"who would you be most worried about if you were prematurely gone?"*), the correct next move is:**
- A) Follow up with another question immediately
- B) Stay silent and let the prospect think it through, however long it takes. The silence is the hot button firing (correct)
- C) Pivot to product
- D) Rephrase the question

**Why:** The question does the work and the silence lets it land. Jumping in (A, D) interrupts them while they're picturing it and pulls them back into small talk. Pivoting to product (C) wastes the moment. The silence feels uncomfortable, but it's where the link between the hot button and the decision forms.

**Q6. Mapping a hot button to a product benefit requires:**
- A) Selling the biggest plan
- B) Starting from the hot button the prospect surfaced and naming the specific product element with specific numbers that addresses it (correct)
- C) Explaining every product feature
- D) Waiting until after the close

**Why:** *"More coverage is better"* doesn't land. *"You said you don't want Ruth going through what your mum is going through. $500K of critical illness cover pays for about 6 years of your family's current spending, and $250K pays for about 3. That gap is what you were talking about."* Their words, their numbers, a concrete difference. The prospect sees themselves in the plan because it was built from what they said.

**Q7. The "hot-button hygiene" ethical line is:**
- A) Use every hot button you find for maximum conversion
- B) Only activate hot buttons where the product actually addresses a real concern. Manufacturing fear where there's no gap is manipulation (correct)
- C) Avoid hot buttons entirely
- D) Only activate positive hot buttons

**Why:** If a prospect already has enough cover, painting critical-illness disaster scenarios creates emotion that serves you, not them, and gives your recommendation no reasonable basis. Tie every hot button you press to a real gap and a part of the plan that actually closes it. Avoiding them entirely (C) throws away the most honest part of advice: helping people see what they'd otherwise keep putting off.
