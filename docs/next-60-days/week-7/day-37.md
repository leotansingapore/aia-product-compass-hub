# Day 37: Hot buttons I

> **The one idea for today:** People decide with emotion and justify with logic. Find the hot button first, then translate the plan into it.

By the end of today you'll be able to define a hot button and tell a positive one from a negative one. You'll know the 7 categories (loved ones, dreams and goals, pride/hobby/skill, faith, needs versus reality, values and beliefs, bad experiences and fear). And you'll recognise the 4 signals that tell you one has just been hit: gaze, emotion, body language, and tone or pace.

---

## What a hot button actually is

A **hot button** is something that matters deeply to a person and is often the real reason behind their decision.

It isn't what they say matters. It's the topic their energy changes around.

Say a prospect tells you *"retirement planning is important to me."* It's polite and reasonable, and completely flat. Their voice doesn't move and their eyes don't change. That's a statement.

Thirty minutes later you mention their father's recent stroke. Their voice slows, their eyes narrow, and they look away for a moment before saying *"yeah... I don't want my kids going through what I'm going through right now."*

That's a hot button. The energy changed and the real motivation showed itself.

Almost every real buying decision hangs on one. Your job is to find it.

---

## Emotion drives decisions, logic justifies them

Decision researchers have studied this for decades, and a 2015 review in the *Annual Review of Psychology* (Lerner and colleagues) described emotions as potent, pervasive and predictable drivers of decision-making. For you, it boils down to this:

> People buy with emotion. They justify with logic.

When someone buys a plan, the decision usually comes from an emotional connection: fear for their family, pride in a goal, relief at finally dealing with a worry they've carried for years. What they tell themselves afterwards is the rational version: the coverage amount made sense, the premium was fine.

So if you lead with logic, you're arguing with the part of them that justifies while the part that decides sits untouched, and the close stalls for that reason.

Lead with the hot button instead, and bring the logic in second. Once the emotion is engaged and the decision starts to form, your numbers become the reasons they use to confirm it.

---

## Positive and negative hot buttons

Hot buttons can pull in either direction, and both kinds work.

| Type | Example | What it paints |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | Dream of early retirement, pride in providing for family, a legacy for the kids | What they want more of |
| Negative | Fear of a parent's illness repeating, shame of running out of money, regret over past money decisions | What they want to avoid |

Negative ones usually carry more weight. Kahneman and Tversky's work on loss aversion found people feel a loss roughly twice as strongly as an equal gain, and a 2024 meta-analysis of the research put the average at about 1.96. People differ on this, and some I-profile prospects respond more to a positive picture, and some C-profiles respond more to avoiding the worst case.

What lights up one person can leave the next one cold. Roller coasters, hiking, a big family: a hot button for some people, nothing for others. Don't assume. Ask, and watch.

---

## The 7 categories of hot buttons

Most hot buttons fall into one of 7 categories. Knowing them helps you spot one when it comes up.

| Category | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Loved ones | Spouse, kids, parents, siblings. The most common one. *"I don't want my kids to struggle the way I did."* |
| Dreams and goals | Early retirement, a lifestyle, travel, a house, a business. *"By 50 I want to be done working."* |
| Pride, hobby, skill | Who they see themselves as: the responsible planner, the golfer, the parent, the successful professional. |
| Faith and religion | Values from their belief system: duty to family, tithing, stewardship. |
| Needs versus reality | The gap between what they should have and what they actually have, and the discomfort that comes with it. |
| Values and beliefs | Integrity, independence, freedom. Things they'd never compromise on. |
| Bad experiences and fear | A past financial disaster, a parent's bankruptcy, a friend who was under-insured. *"Never again."* |

Most prospects have 2 or 3 live hot buttons at any one time. You don't need to catalogue all 7. Find the 2 or 3 that are live right now and build the pitch around those.

---

## The 4 signals that a hot button has fired

When you hit one, you'll usually see 2 or 3 of these change at once.

### 1. Gaze

They look away or down, or they hold your eye harder than before. This is the most reliable of the four. An *"uh..."* plus a shift in the eyes means you've touched something.

### 2. Emotion and energy

They slow down or speed up, their voice catches, or their smile changes. Any shift away from how they were a minute ago counts.

### 3. Body language

They lean in or sit back, cross their arms, touch their face. The posture change doesn't match what the conversation is about on the surface.

### 4. Tone and pace

Their pitch drops, they slow down, or they say *"mm..."* instead of answering. Compare it with how they've sounded for the last 15 minutes.

All four depend on knowing their baseline. If you don't know how they sound, look and move on neutral topics, you won't notice when that changes. Use the first 5 to 10 minutes of every meeting to learn it.

---

## The biggest mistake with hot buttons

Most new FCs do notice the signal. Where they go wrong is carrying on as if nothing happened.

The temptation is to stick to the agenda: *"Okay, noted. So let's look at your current coverage..."* That files the hot button away as a fact and skips the moment.

Slow down instead, and ask one clarifying question:

> *"That thing you just said about your dad's stroke. Can you tell me more about what that was like?"*

You aren't fishing for sympathy. You're giving them room to stay with something they care about. The 20 seconds they spend telling you more is where the link between that hot button and your eventual recommendation gets made.

Treat a hot button as an opening in the conversation and go through it.

---

## Capture it and use it later

When a hot button comes up, note it, in your head or in your notes if you're writing. When you present the recommendation later, you want to be able to say:

> *"Remember when you mentioned [the specific thing]? Here's how this part of the plan deals with that."*

That callback is what makes a pitch feel personal. The prospect thinks: this person listened, and this plan is actually for me.

New FCs often hear the hot button, nod, and have forgotten it by minute 50, so the callback never happens and the pitch sounds like one they could give anyone. Write it down.

## Quiz

**Q1. A hot button is:**
- A) A phrase the prospect repeats often
- B) Something that matters deeply to a person and is often the real motivation behind their decision, which you can spot through visible changes in their energy ✓
- C) Any objection the prospect raises
- D) A demographic category

**Why:** A hot button is an emotional motivator, which is different from a stated preference. Prospects can say something matters without any heat behind it. The tell is the visible change in energy, so if you only listen to the words you'll miss a lot of them. Watch gaze, emotion, body and tone as well.

**Q2. The correct order is:**
- A) Logic first, then emotion
- B) Emotion (hot button) first, logic (justification) second ✓
- C) Logic only, since emotion is manipulation
- D) Emotion only, since logic bores people

**Why:** The decision is emotional and the justification is logical. Lead with logic and you're trying to persuade the justifying part before anything has moved the deciding part, so the close stalls. Lead with the hot button and your numbers become the reasons the prospect uses to confirm what they've already decided.

**Q3. When a hot button fires mid-meeting, the correct move is:**
- A) Note it and move on with the agenda
- B) Slow down, ask one clarifying question, and give the prospect room to say more ✓
- C) Immediately pitch the relevant product
- D) Change topic to avoid making them uncomfortable

**Why:** Moving on (A) treats the moment as a fact to log. The 20 seconds the prospect spends saying more is where the link to your eventual recommendation forms. Pitching straight away (C) feels manipulative, and changing topic (D) wastes the opening. One clarifying question is enough.

**Q4. Positive vs negative hot buttons. Which tends to be more powerful for most people?**
- A) Positive, because people buy aspirations
- B) Negative, because loss aversion makes a loss feel roughly twice as strong as an equal gain for most people ✓
- C) Neither, they're equal
- D) Only positive is ethical to use

**Why:** Kahneman and Tversky found that losing $100 feels about twice as bad as gaining $100 feels good, and a 2024 meta-analysis put the average ratio at about 1.96. Negative hot buttons (a parent's illness repeating, running out of money) move more people than positive ones (legacy, a dream retirement). That doesn't mean always leading with fear. Some I-profiles respond better to a positive picture.

**Q5. The 7 categories of hot buttons include all of these EXCEPT:**
- A) Loved ones
- B) Dreams / Goals
- C) Faith / Religion
- D) Product features ✓

**Why:** The 7 are loved ones, dreams and goals, pride/hobby/skill, faith, needs versus reality, values and beliefs, and bad experiences and fear. Product features describe the solution you later map to a hot button. Treat a feature as a hot button and you get a flat pitch.

**Q6. "What a prospect *says* matters vs what a hot button is". The distinction is:**
- A) A hot button is just anything they mention
- B) A hot button is what their *energy changes around*, while stated preferences often have no emotional heat behind them ✓
- C) Hot buttons can only be identified by DISC profile
- D) Every stated preference is a hot button

**Why:** Most prospects can list the usual important things (retirement, protecting the family) in a flat, polite voice, and those are just statements. A real hot button comes with a change in voice, eyes, posture or tone. The skill is noticing that change, and parsing the words alone won't get you there.

**Q7. Calibrating a prospect's "baseline" in the first 5-10 minutes matters because:**
- A) You need to build rapport
- B) You can't notice an energy change (the signal that a hot button fired) if you don't know what their neutral state looks like ✓
- C) It fills time
- D) It's required for the Fact-Find form

**Why:** The baseline is your reference point. If their neutral voice is already slow and soft, as it often is with an S, you won't mistake that for a hot button. If it's fast and animated, as with many I's, the change might be them slowing down. Neutral small talk (the commute, the weekend, family) at the start gives you that reference.
