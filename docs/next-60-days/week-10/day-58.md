# Day 58: How a top producer runs a week

> **Today in one line:** What separates top producers from everyone else is rarely talent or luck. It's the weekly system they keep running whether they feel like it or not.

**What to remember**

1. A top producer works about 45 productive hours a week: roughly half new business, a third relationship and referral work, and the rest systems.
2. Protect the fixed blocks: Monday calling, the meeting windows, the Friday review and 30 minutes of Sunday prep.
3. Keep systems work under 20% of the week. When it grows past that, it's usually hiding you from calls and closes.
4. Read for 15 minutes every day. That's 62.5 hours a year, and it compounds.

## Why study the weekly rhythm

Every top producer who started as a new FC made the same shift. They went from "I work when there's something to do" to "I work the same week every week, whether I feel like it or not."

Motivation came and went, and structure made the shift. Their weekly system keeps prospecting going however many cases close, stops client work getting squeezed out, keeps admin from eating the productive hours, and makes rest and family time actually happen.

The exact hours differ from person to person, but the shape of the week is strikingly similar across the guest speakers who've shared theirs with the team.

## A representative week: the three-way split

A typical top-producer week runs to about 45 to 50 productive hours, split three ways:

![A representative week, the 3-category split: New business (prospecting + appts + close) ~50%; Relationship / referral (client touchpoints + reviews) ~35%; Systems (admin + review + CE) ~15%](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-58-m0.webp)

| Category | Hours a week | What goes in it |
|---|---|---|
| New business | 22 to 25 | Prospecting calls, Fact-Finds, pitches, closes |
| Relationship and referral work | 16 to 18 | Client reviews, onboarding, after-sales touchpoints, referral asks |
| Systems | 6 to 8 | Admin, the weekly review, continuing education (CE), mentor time and reading time |

New business brings in this year's income, and relationship work brings in future income through retention and referrals. New FCs tend to spend too long on systems (lots of planning, not enough doing) and too little on relationships, which leaves their book thin by Year 2.

## A sample weekly schedule

Here's one top-producer week in time blocks.

### Monday: calling day

- 8:30 to 9:00: weekly review, pipeline scan, finalise the week's target list
- 9:00 to 12:00: prospecting calls (Market Survey, warm outreach, referral follow-ups) with notifications off
- 12:00 to 13:00: lunch and email
- 13:00 to 17:00: more prospecting, appointment setting, text and DM follow-ups
- 17:00 to 18:00: admin buffer

Before any cold call or text to a number that hasn't given you consent, check it against the Do Not Call Registry, as the Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) requires.

### Tuesday to Thursday: meetings

- 9:00 to 11:00: prospecting or client calls (flexible)
- 11:00 to 12:30: meeting 1 (Fact-Find, pitch or review)
- 12:30 to 14:00: lunch and travel buffer
- 14:00 to 15:30: meeting 2
- 15:30 to 17:00: meeting 3, or buffer and debrief
- 17:30 to 19:00: meeting 4 (evening slot for people who work office hours)

### Friday: review and relationships

- 9:00 to 10:00: weekly scorecard review (calls, appointments and referrals, the CAR numbers from [Day 3](/learning-track/next-60-days/day/3))
- 10:00 to 12:00: onboarding touchpoints (Day-7, Day-30 and Day-90 messages and calls)
- 12:00 to 13:00: lunch and a peer or mentor call
- 13:00 to 15:00: client review meetings (A-tier check-ins)
- 15:00 to 17:00: systems work: CE, reading, content, learning

### Saturday: half day or flex

- 9:00 to 12:00: client meetings that didn't fit in the week, content, or prospecting if the pipeline is light
- From 12:00: off

### Sunday: off, plus 30 minutes of prep

- 30 minutes in the evening to prepare the week ahead: update the target list, confirm calendar blocks
- The rest of the day is rest

That's about 45 productive hours, which you can sustain for years.

## The non-negotiables

A few habits show up in almost every top-producer schedule.

- Monday morning is for calls only, with no meetings, admin or email. Those three hours set up the week's pipeline, and if something else eats them, the whole week suffers.
- Certain blocks are kept for prospect meetings, and prospects fit into them. Admin and personal errands fit around the meeting grid, never the other way round.
- The week ends on relationship work (Friday's review and onboarding touches), because the new-business momentum from Monday to Thursday carries into the next week anyway.
- Saturday is flex, neither full rest nor a full workday, so overflow doesn't push the weekday blocks around.
- Rest goes in the calendar like everything else, and half an hour of Sunday prep makes Monday's first calls much easier to start.

## What top producers don't do

- They don't check email during calling blocks. Three focused hours of calls achieve more than six broken-up ones.
- They don't go to every networking event or optional meeting. They pick one or two recurring ones that pay off and skip the rest.
- They don't relearn the same lessons. They turn what they learn into checklists, templates and CRM steps instead of relying on memory.
- They don't take every prospect. They qualify early and walk away from poor fits, instead of spending three meetings on someone who won't buy or would be a difficult client.

## Where new FCs' weeks break down

These are the three most common ways the rhythm falls apart in Year 1.

### Failure 1: calling drift

You meant to call for three hours on Monday morning. Instead you checked email, answered a client and sorted some admin, and by the time you picked up the phone it was 10:30. This is the most common Year-1 failure.

The fix: protect the calling block. Turn notifications off, close every browser tab except the CRM, and stay in the chair for the full three hours apart from water and toilet breaks.

### Failure 2: saying yes to every reschedule

A prospect asks to move Tuesday at 2pm to Thursday at 4pm, and you agree. After six weeks of that your grid is gone. You can't find calling time and the Friday review keeps getting bumped.

The fix: offer two alternatives that fit your grid, such as "Tuesday at 2 or Thursday at 11 both work for me, which suits you better?" Most people will simply pick one. Someone with a genuine clash will tell you.

### Failure 3: systems work spreads

You block Friday 3 to 5pm for CE and reading. It becomes Friday 1 to 5, then 11 to 5, then half of Tuesday too. Before long you're spending 15 hours a week on systems and 20 on work that actually earns.

The fix: keep systems under 20% of the week. When it creeps above that, it usually means you're hiding from calls and closes behind planning and learning, because those feel safer. "I need to learn more first" and "I need to sort out my CRM" are the usual stories.

## Adapt it to your life

Treat the schedule above as a model. Your week will look different:

- If you're a morning person, you might call from 6:30 to 9:30am.
- If your prospects are working professionals or business owners, you may need more slots from 6 to 9pm.
- Family commitments may decide when your focused work happens.
- Local patterns matter too, such as Singapore office hours or adjusting meeting times during Ramadan.

Keep the structure and change the details. The calling block, protected meeting windows, weekly review, Day-7/30/90 touches and Sunday prep stay put, while the exact hours can move.

## Staying hungry past Year 5

Some advisers keep running this system for 5, 10 or 20 years, while people who started the same week quit in Year 2, and the difference is mostly motivation.

In Year 1 the hunger is easy to find. The numbers are small, progress shows up every week, the stakes feel high, and you want to prove you can do this.

By Year 5 it feels different. Your book brings in renewal income, the early fear of "can I survive this?" has been answered, and calls no longer feel life-or-death the way they did in Month 2. That's where a lot of multi-year plateaus begin.

What gets an adviser through Year 1 is usually fear of failure, and that fear fades once you've survived. Advisers who keep growing after Year 5 tend to find something else to run on, usually one of three things:

| Year-5+ motivator | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| Mastery | "I want to get as good at this job as anyone can. I'll still be working on objection handling in Year 20." |
| Impact | "Every client I look after properly is a family that's better off. I don't want to stop at 50 clients when I could serve 200." |
| Legacy | "I want the next FCs on my team to point at me as the standard." |

These line up with what psychologists call intrinsic motivation. Richard Ryan and Edward Deci's self-determination theory links long-term drive to feeling competent, having autonomy and being connected to others, much more than to outside rewards. Competing on rankings tends to wear thin, because there's always someone ahead.

In Year 1 you can't honestly pick your Year-5 motivator yet, because you haven't lived through the shift. You can notice which of the three pulls at you most as you read them.

## Out-learn to out-earn: the 4 pillars

The Year-5 motivators explain why top producers keep going. The four pillars are what they run on, the physical and mental base that makes five years of steady growth possible.

| Pillar | What it means | What it looks like day to day |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Desire | Three questions: what do you want, how will you get it, and how badly do you want it? | Your Year-5 sentence from today's exercise. If you can't answer it, discipline alone won't carry you. |
| 2. Mental resilience | Sometimes you slow down to speed up: reading, CE, roleplays. | 15 minutes of reading a day over 250 working days adds up to 62.5 hours a year. |
| 3. Physical resilience | Exercise, sleep and eating well. Your mental energy is capped by your physical energy. | Cutting sleep to fit in two more calls tonight usually costs you more in tomorrow's focus and judgement. |
| 4. Discipline | The Monday block, the Friday review and Sunday prep, whatever your mood. | The weekly system above. It's what keeps the week running when motivation has run out. |

### The out-learn rule

> "Out-learn your peers and you'll out-earn them."

Fifteen minutes of reading every day does more than three hours once a month. Learning research supports this. A 2006 review by Cepeda and colleagues, covering 317 experiments, found that spreading study over time leads to much better retention than cramming it into one session.

What 62.5 hours a year of professional reading gives you:

- clearer thinking under pressure
- more than one way of looking at a client's problem
- more to offer clients, so you become the person they call with the odd question
- better vocabulary, writing and conversation
- a reputation for knowing your stuff, which brings in referrals

## Do this today

1. Put your own week next to the sample schedule. Find the one or two blocks you're missing (usually the Monday calling block or the Friday review) and put them in your calendar from next week.
2. Fix a 15-minute reading slot at the same time every day: with morning coffee (the business pages and a chapter of a book on advising or selling), on your commute (a podcast or audiobook), or before sleep (a book, not a screen). "I'll read when I can" doesn't work.
3. On the back of your Day 3 scorecard, which you'll fill in tomorrow, write one sentence: "Five years from now, what will keep me caring on a Wednesday in Month 59?" If the answer is "I don't know yet," keep the question and come back to it at Month 12. Plenty of advisers who leave in Years 3 to 5 never found an answer.

## Quiz

**Q1. In a representative top-producer week, the 3 activity categories split roughly:**
- A) 90% new business, 5% relationships, 5% systems
- B) ~50% new business, ~35% relationship / referral, ~15% systems (correct)
- C) Equal thirds
- D) 25% new business, 50% relationships, 25% systems

**Why:** New business brings in this year's income, relationship work brings in future income through retention and referrals, and systems keep the practice running. The rough 50/35/15 split covers all three. New FCs tend to overdo systems and neglect relationships. A is the Year-1 all-out sprint that usually burns out by Year 2.

**Q2. Monday-as-calling-day is a near-universal top-producer pattern because:**
- A) Prospects are fresh on Monday
- B) The week's pipeline is set in those 3 hours, and if Monday morning gets eaten by anything else, the rest of the week suffers (correct)
- C) Admin tasks accumulate over the weekend
- D) It's a tradition

**Why:** How full your pipeline is later in the week shows whether Monday's calling block really happened. Top producers guard it with no email, meetings or admin. New FCs let Monday drift and then spend the week catching up on prospecting.

**Q3. Systems work (admin, review, CE) expanding to consume 30%+ of the week is:**
- A) A sign of deep investment in long-term growth
- B) Usually a signal that the advisor is avoiding uncomfortable revenue-work (calling, closing) by doing comfortable planning-work (correct)
- C) Required for regulatory compliance
- D) Fine as long as new business still hits targets

**Why:** Systems should stay under 20% of the week. When it grows past that, avoidance is usually behind it: planning and learning feel comfortable, while calling and closing don't. New FCs tell themselves *"I need to learn more first"* or *"I need to sort out my CRM."* If new-business numbers are flat while systems time grows, the systems work is hiding the real problem.

**Q4. Monday morning calling block should be:**
- A) Flexible based on how motivated you feel
- B) 3 hours, protected: no email, no meetings, no admin, phone notifications off, browser closed except CRM (correct)
- C) 30 minutes max to ease into the week
- D) Spread across the day

**Why:** Three focused hours achieve more than six broken-up ones. The protection is the point. Let email, meetings or admin in and the block breaks into pieces. Those three hours set up the week's pipeline, and a structure you've set in advance holds up better than willpower on the day.

**Q5. A prospect asks to move a Tuesday 2pm meeting to a random Thursday 4pm slot. The correct response is:**
- A) Say yes, because client flexibility matters
- B) Offer 2 alternative times that fit *your* grid: *"Tuesday 2pm or Thursday 11am both work. Which suits you better?"* (correct)
- C) Refuse any rescheduling
- D) Move all your other meetings to accommodate

**Why:** If every prospect sets your calendar, the grid is gone within six weeks, along with your calling, review and onboarding blocks. Two alternatives respect their needs and protect your system, and most people pick one of them. Someone with a genuine clash will tell you. Someone who keeps asking for odd slots is telling you something too.

**Q6. Year-5+ hunger is usually driven by one of three things. Which is NOT one of them?**
- A) Mastery: getting as good at the job as possible
- B) Impact scale: wanting to serve more people
- C) Legacy identity: wanting to be the standard for the next generation
- D) Competition: beating peers in rankings (correct)

**Why:** Mastery, impact and legacy keep long advisory careers going. Competing on rankings tends to wear thin, because there's always someone ahead and the comparison turns against you. The three that hold up come from inside (mastery), point outward (impact) or rest on who you want to be (legacy), which fits self-determination research on intrinsic motivation. In Year 1 you can't pick yours yet. The question is something to come back to.

**Q7. The Year-5 hunger question *"what keeps me still caring on a Wednesday in Month 59?"* works because:**
- A) It tests discipline
- B) It anchors the learner to a specific future moment where their current motivator (fear of failure / novelty) will have faded, so they have to find a more durable one (correct)
- C) It's philosophically interesting
- D) It predicts income

**Why:** Year-1 motivators (fear of not surviving, the novelty of being new, the urge to prove yourself) fade once you know you'll survive. The Month-59 question puts you in that later state and asks what will replace them. Noticing your answer, or that you don't have one yet, tells you what to look for over the next five years.
