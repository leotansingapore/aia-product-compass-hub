# Day 2: The activity math

> **Today in one line:** Your FYC is three numbers multiplied together. Two of them improve with reps. The third, appointments, you control today.

**What to remember**

1. FYC = appointments x close rate x case size. There's no fourth lever.
2. Appointments is the lever you can move this week, because it's behaviour. Close rate and case size grow over months.
3. At starter numbers, $30,000 FYC in 6 months needs about 5 appointments a week. Aim for 6 to 7.
4. Prospecting pays off over the next 90 days and every meeting uses up a lead, so keep calling and keep adding names.

## The formula

Every dollar of first-year commission comes from the same equation:

```
FYC = Appointments × Close rate × Case size
```

Appointments is how many qualified first meetings you sit in a week. These are Educational Fact-Finds, and a social coffee doesn't count. Close rate is the percentage of those meetings that become paying clients. Case size is your average first-year commission per closed case.

If your FYC isn't where you want it, one of those three is the reason. There isn't a fourth.

## Why appointments is the only lever you control today

The three levers move at different speeds.

![Why appointments is the only lever you control today: appointments, controllable today; close rate, reps over weeks; case size, experience over months; FYC](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-02-m0.webp)

Close rate depends on skill: the questions you ask, how well you read a prospect's DISC profile, how you handle objections and whether you close with conviction. None of that gets much better in a week. The team plans on a new FC closing 20 to 40% of qualified meetings, and maybe 30 to 50% by month 3.

Case size depends on who's across the table and what you uncover. Bigger cases come from wealthier prospects, better fact-finds and the nerve to recommend a complete plan. Expect your first 10 cases to land at $500 to $1,000 FYC each. That grows with your network, and you can't force the timeline.

Appointments is behaviour: you pick up the phone, send the text and book the meeting. Nothing in your skill level on Day 2 stops you booking 5 to 7 meetings this week. Ask the senior advisors what held them back early. Most will say they didn't pick up the phone enough.

## Honest starting numbers

These are the team's planning figures, not an industry survey. Tomorrow you'll put your own on the scorecard.

| Lever | Typical new-FC starting range | Where it ends up in Years 2 to 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Close rate | 20 to 40% of qualified Fact-Finds | 40 to 60% |
| Case size | $500 to $1,000 FYC | $1,000 to $1,500 FYC |
| Appointments per week | 5 to 7 | 7 to 10 |

Take a 30% close rate and a $750 case. At 5 appointments a week, 5 x 30% x $750 = $1,125 FYC a week, or about $58,500 a year. At 7 a week, the same math gives $1,575 a week and about $81,900 a year.

The close rate and case size haven't changed. Two more meetings a week add about $23,400 FYC over a year, with no new skill.

### Working backward from your target

Say you want $30,000 FYC in your first 6 months. That's $30,000 ÷ 26 weeks, or about $1,154 FYC a week. At starter numbers, $1,154 ÷ ($750 x 30%) = about 5.1 appointments a week.

That's the floor. Some meetings will fall away to cancellations, ghosting and slow weeks, so aim for 6 to 7 a week. This module is built around that activity floor.

You can move any lever for a bigger number. Tighter fact-finds in Week 8 raise case size, and objection-handling reps in Week 9 raise close rate. More meetings is the one you can start on this afternoon.

## What this changes about your week

1. Work on the right lever. New FCs tend to optimise the wrong thing: the deck, closes rehearsed in the mirror, sales psychology books. That's slow-lever work, and polish only pays off once you have reps. Pick up the phone first.
2. Count the right unit. Messages sent and people who said maybe don't count. The formula only uses a qualified first meeting booked with a time, a place and a Fact-Find agenda.
3. Keep the number where you can see it: a whiteboard, your phone wallpaper or a sticky note on your desk. You'll move the number you look at every day.

The advisors who get through Year 1 usually aren't the smoothest talkers. They're the ones who kept picking up the phone on days they didn't feel like it.

## The 30-day rule and the law of replacement

Two rules from Jeb Blount's *Fanatical Prospecting* (Wiley, 2015) tell you when the activity pays off and what skipping it costs.

The 30-Day Rule: the prospecting you do in any 30-day window pays off over the next 90 days. A skipped day of dials probably won't hurt this week. A skipped week shows up in your commission two months later, and a skipped month brings a slump in 90 days that you can't explain.

The delay is what catches new FCs out. "I skipped Monday calls but still closed 2 cases this week, so it's fine." Those closes came from pipeline built a month ago, and the skipped Monday shows up later as a thin month. So your calling block doesn't bend to how busy this week feels.

The Law of Replacement: every appointment uses up a lead, whether it closes or not, so you have to add new prospects faster than you use them up.

At a 30% close rate, 10 leads give you 3 clients and 7 passes. Add fewer than 10 new leads for every 10 you work through and the pipeline empties, usually several months into Year 1. Plenty of new FCs take it as proof the career isn't for them, when really they stopped replacing leads months earlier.

### How a slump happens

When a Year 1 FC says "I'm in a slump", the chain almost always runs like this:

1. They stopped prospecting (broke the 30-Day Rule).
2. The pipeline stalled (broke the Law of Replacement).
3. Deals stopped closing because the pipeline ran out.
4. Confidence dropped, energy followed and desperation set in.

The way out is to start prospecting hard again straight away, without dwelling on last month. One calling block, then the next.

## Do this today

1. Work out your weekly floor: your 6-month FYC target ÷ 26 ÷ ($750 x 30%). Round up, then add one or two for meetings that fall away.
2. Write that number where you'll see it every day.
3. Block a calling slot today and book this week's first Fact-Finds, each with a time, a place and an agenda.
4. Before Friday, watch the EPS walkthrough and find your month-by-month target in the Lark doc.

## Quiz

**Q1. Your FYC equation has three variables. In your first 60 days, which one is most under your direct control?**
- A) Close rate, because it's about how polished your pitch is
- B) Case size, because it's about finding wealthier prospects
- C) Appointments, because it's a behavioural number you can move today (correct)
- D) All three are equally hard to move

**Why:** Close rate and case size improve with reps and experience over months or years. Appointments is behaviour. Whether you picked up the phone today is a yes or no, and it's up to you whatever your skill level.

**Q2. A new advisor has a 30% close rate, $750 average case size, and books 5 appointments per week. What's their weekly FYC pace?**
- A) $750
- B) $1,125 (correct)
- C) $2,250
- D) $3,750

**Why:** 5 x 30% x $750 = $1,125 FYC a week. Over a year that's about $58,500, and it grows as close rate and case size improve with experience.

**Q3. Two advisors both have a 30% close rate and $750 case size. Advisor A books 5 appointments a week. Advisor B books 7. What's the FYC gap over a year?**
- A) ~$23,000 (correct)
- B) ~$50,000
- C) ~$10,000
- D) Depends on product mix

**Why:** A: 5 x 30% x $750 x 52 = about $58,500. B: 7 x 30% x $750 x 52 = about $81,900. The gap is about $23,400 with the same skill and the same products, from two more meetings a week. More meetings also mean more practice, so over several years the gap tends to widen.

**Q4. Your close rate is 20-40% as a new FC. Why can't you "just get better at closing" this week?**
- A) Because only certified advisors can close
- B) Because close rate is a skill that improves with reps across weeks, and a single week isn't enough (correct)
- C) Because AIA policies prevent fast close-rate growth
- D) Because close rate is purely about product knowledge

**Why:** Close rate improves with more meetings, better DISC reads and sharper objection handling, and you learn those over weeks and months. A weekend of rehearsing closes in the mirror won't change Monday's live close rate much. Skill comes from volume.

**Q5. Day 2 argues: "stop optimizing the wrong thing." What's the "wrong thing" new FCs tend to over-optimize?**
- A) The pitch deck, the script and the memorised objection answers: all close-rate and case-size work, done before they have volume (correct)
- B) Their prospect list, which is always too long
- C) Their commission splits, which they should negotiate up
- D) Their product knowledge, which is never enough

**Why:** Close rate and case size need work eventually, but they move slowly. Obsessing over them before you've booked enough meetings to test them is procrastination that looks like preparation. Get the volume first and polish after.

**Q6. "Count the right unit": what does Day 2 define as the unit that feeds your FYC formula?**
- A) DMs sent
- B) Coffees scheduled
- C) Qualified first-meetings booked with a time, a place, and a Fact-Find agenda (correct)
- D) LinkedIn connections accepted

**Why:** A DM isn't a meeting, and neither is "let me check my calendar". A coffee with no agenda is socialising. The only unit that goes into the formula is a confirmed first meeting where the prospect has agreed to a Fact-Find. Count anything looser and you inflate your number and hide what you're actually doing.

**Q7. Day 2 says the advisors who get through Year 1 are the ones who kept picking up the phone when they didn't feel like it. What does that imply about the most common way new FCs fail?**
- A) Failure is rarely about talent or product. It's about whether you keep calling through low-motivation stretches (correct)
- B) Failure is always about product knowledge
- C) Only extroverts make it in financial advisory
- D) The smoothest talkers become the best advisors

**Why:** The FCs who quit in Year 1 rarely lacked product knowledge. They stopped dialling after a run of rejections. Year 1 rewards steady discipline more than talent.
