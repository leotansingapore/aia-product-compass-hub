# Day 43: The power question (right questions, part 1)

> **The one idea for today:** A hundred statements won't do what one right question does.

> Where you've met this before: First 60 Days [Day 47 - Right Questions](../../first-60-days/week-8/day-47.md) introduced the principle, the 3-point checklist and SPIN as the grammar. Day 44 used a right question as the Q step in Acknowledge, Question, Reframe for objections, and Day 38 used one to surface hot buttons. Today goes deeper with the 6 golden rules and field examples, so the questions come out sounding like you. Tomorrow is part 2, on silence.

By the end of today you'll be able to use the 6 golden rules (echo the keyword, offer possible answers, use contrast, give two clear options, use non-committal words, bring in a neutral source), test any question against the 3-point checklist, and swap your 3 most common statements for questions that let the prospect reach the conclusion on their own.

---

## Why statements lose to questions

Here's a statement:
> *"Insurance is important because it protects your family."*

The prospect hears "here's what you should believe." If they already agree, you've wasted the line. If they don't, you now have to argue them out of their position, and that almost never works.

Here's the same idea as a question:
> *"If something happened to you tomorrow, who would be affected financially, and by how much?"*

Now they have to think. They picture a real scenario and land on their own answer, so you didn't have to tell them anything.

People defend statements that other people make, and they own conclusions they reach themselves. A good question gets them to their own conclusion.

---

## What a right question can do

A well-built question can do any of these jobs:

1. Open the mind and get attention, because it breaks the script they expected
2. Find out what their priorities actually are
3. Get underneath the objection they first voiced
4. Work around an objection
5. Show what happens if they do nothing
6. Build agreement one step at a time
7. Warm up the conversation
8. Move attention to what matters
9. Get them to reflect, so their own situation becomes the evidence
10. Test readiness (a trial close) without asking for commitment

A statement usually does one or two of these. It's no accident that Neil Rackham's *SPIN Selling* (1988), built on 12 years of research into 35,000 sales calls, is at heart a sequence of four kinds of question.

## The 6 golden rules

### Rule 1: Pick up their keyword and use it in your next question

The prospect just said something. Put their word back into your next question.

> *Prospect:* *"Bro, honestly I don't believe in insurance. I think it's a scam."*
> *You:* *"If you don't mind me asking, why don't you believe in insurance?"* or *"Thanks for telling me straight. May I know the reason?"*

Using their word (*believe*, *scam*) shows you listened. It also makes them explain their own position, and strong positions tend to soften once someone has to spell them out.

### Rule 2: Offer possible answers

If the prospect is hedging, give them a short menu.

> *Prospect:* *"Let me think about it."*
> *You:* *"Sure. May I ask which part you'd like to think over? Is it the monthly commitment, or something else?"*

Without the menu you get "just the whole thing", which tells you nothing. With it, they usually pick one, and now you know what to address.

### Rule 3: Use contrast and words with some weight

Two versions of the same question:

Weak: *"Wouldn't you agree the earlier you start, the better it is?"*

Stronger: *"Wouldn't you agree we don't have many years we can afford to waste? Since your time matters, why not start early and see the results of your effort sooner?"*

The first one is flat. The second puts wasted years against time that matters, and that gets a reaction. The content is the same.

### Rule 4: Give two clear, contrasting options

Frame the choice as two options, where the sensible one is easy to see.

> *"If you had to choose, would you rather pay a large hospital bill out of your own savings, or pass that risk to an insurer for a much smaller regular premium?"*

They pick, and because they picked, they own the choice. You didn't tell them what to think.

Then check it, without flattery: *"Which of those sits better with you?"* Let them say it in their own words. If they pick the first option, that's a real answer too. Find out why.

### Rule 5: Use non-committal words

Use *may*, *might*, *possibility*, *could*, *is there a chance*, *perhaps*. Avoid *must*, *will*, *should* and *definitely*.

Too heavy: *"Do you think I'm the right person for the job?"* This feels like pressure, so they hedge.

Lighter: *"Do you think there's a possibility I might be the right person for the job?"* They can say yes without feeling committed, and that yes is often the start of a real one.

New FCs tend to think being direct means being strong. In practice, light wording gets more honest yeses than pressure does.

### Rule 6: Bring in a neutral, respected source

When a prospect holds a firm view, don't argue with it. Ask them to weigh it against a source they'd accept as neutral. The source has to be real and you have to represent it accurately.

> *Prospect:* *"I don't want to invest with insurance companies. ILPs are not good."*
> *You:* *"Why do you think ILPs aren't good?"* (Rule 1)
> *Then:* *"Would you agree MoneySense is a fairly neutral place to check? It's the government's financial education site, not an insurer."* (agreement on the source)
> *Then:* *"Their guide says that if protection is all you want, an ILP may not be the best fit, and term insurance may give you more cover for less. An ILP is for people who want cover and investment in one plan and are fine with the charges. Which of those sounds more like you?"* (the source plus two options)

Now they aren't arguing with you. They're checking their view against a source they already accepted, and whatever they answer tells you what to recommend. Sometimes the honest answer is that an ILP isn't right for them. Only recommend an ILP if you hold CMFAS M9 and M9A.

## The 3-point checklist: is this a good question?

Before you ask a question, test it:

1. Does it lead to my objective? Is there a clear path from this question to where the conversation needs to go?
2. Is it specific? Does it call for a specific answer, or does it leave room for a hedge?
3. Is it logical and hard to dispute? Could the prospect disagree without sounding unreasonable?

If all 3 pass, ask it. If one fails, rewrite it first.

> The pairing rule: the checklist tests the question, and the cushion tests how you deliver it. You need both. A good question without a cushion still makes the prospect feel argued with, and a cushion in front of a sloppy question wastes the moment. See [Day 40](../week-7/day-40.md#cushioning-the-move-under-all-three-tools) for cushioning, and [First 60 Days Day 47](../../first-60-days/week-8/day-47.md) for how the checklist applies across SPIN's four phases.

## Worked example: "why should I work with you?"

A prospect asks:
> *"Why should I work with you? How do I know you're the right advisor?"*

A weak answer is a list of credentials. It sounds like self-promotion, and as a new FC you don't have a long track record to list anyway. Never borrow one. Inventing client numbers or a retention record is misrepresentation.

A better answer is honest and ends in a question:
> *"Fair question. I'm new, so I can't point you to a long list of clients. What I can do is show you how I work. Everything I recommend goes in writing, with the guaranteed and non-guaranteed parts shown separately, and you can check any plan against others on compareFIRST before you decide. What would you need to see from me to feel comfortable?"*

Their answer tells you what trust looks like to them, and now you can deliver exactly that.

## Your 3 most common statements: convert them

Take the 3 statements you make most often and turn each into a question.

Examples:

| Statement | Question |
|---|---|
| *"You should have CI coverage."* | *"If you were diagnosed with cancer tomorrow, what would that do to your savings and income over the next 3 years?"* |
| *"Starting early is better."* | *"Wouldn't you agree time is the one thing we can't get back? Which years would you rather have working for you?"* |
| *"This is the best plan for you."* | *"Of the options we've looked at, which one gives you the trade-off you want between cover, cost and flexibility? Why that one?"* |

That last row matters for compliance too. "Best plan" is a claim you can't back up. A question lets the prospect weigh the options themselves.

## Quiz

**Q1. The main reason questions do better than statements is:**
- A) Questions are shorter
- B) People own conclusions they reach themselves and push back on conclusions other people hand them ✓
- C) Questions fill silence
- D) Questions sound more humble

**Why:** A statement sets up a yes-or-no reaction to what you said. A question gets the prospect to work out the answer, so the conclusion is theirs, and people hold on to their own conclusions much more firmly than to yours.

**Q2. Non-committal wording (*"may," "might," "possibility"*) works better than committal wording (*"must," "will," "should"*) because:**
- A) It sounds more polite
- B) It lowers the pressure, so the prospect can say yes without feeling locked in, which leads to more honest yeses ✓
- C) It's less confident
- D) It's more British

**Why:** Committal wording makes people feel boxed in, and boxed-in people hedge. Lighter wording lets them say yes to exploring, and that often turns into a real yes later. What counts is the result, not how firm you sound.

**Q3. The 3-point checklist for a right question is:**
- A) Short · friendly · warm
- B) Leads to your objective · specific · logical and indisputable ✓
- C) Confident · direct · assumptive
- D) Creative · unusual · memorable

**Why:** A question that doesn't lead to your objective wastes the moment. One that allows a hedge gets you no information, and one the prospect can reasonably dispute opens an argument you didn't want. Fix whichever point fails before you ask.

**Q4. Rule 1 says to pick up the prospect's keyword and use it in your next question. Why?**
- A) To save time
- B) Using their word shows you heard them, and it makes them explain their own position, which usually softens it ✓
- C) To confuse them
- D) For legal reasons

**Why:** When a prospect says *"insurance is a scam"* and you ask *"why do you think insurance is a scam?"*, you show you listened and you get them explaining their view. Strong opinions often soften once someone has to explain them out loud.

**Q5. Rule 4 gives two clear, contrasting options. Why does this work?**
- A) Prospects like simple choices
- B) The prospect picks the option that makes sense to them, and because they chose it, they own it ✓
- C) Binary choices are less confusing
- D) It avoids legal issues

**Why:** *"Would you rather pay a large hospital bill from your savings, or pass that risk on for a smaller regular premium?"* lets the prospect state the conclusion themselves. It still has to be a fair choice. If they pick the other option, ask why and listen.

**Q6. On the question *"am I the right person for the job?"*, why does adding *"is there a possibility I might be..."* help?**
- A) It sounds humble
- B) It makes a yes feel like exploring rather than committing, and that leads to more real yeses than pressure does ✓
- C) It shortens the question
- D) It avoids compliance issues

**Why:** The plain version feels like a commitment, so prospects hedge. The softer version lets them agree without feeling boxed in, and that first yes gives you something to build on.

**Q7. Rule 6 (bring in a neutral, respected source) works by:**
- A) Citing any authority that sounds impressive
- B) Getting agreement that a source is neutral, then asking the prospect to weigh their view against what that source actually says ✓
- C) Making the FC sound smart
- D) Dropping names

**Why:** You aren't saying "an expert says so, so believe it." You agree on a neutral source first (MoneySense, not an insurer), then quote it accurately and ask which case fits them. They rethink without losing face, and the source has to be real, because a made-up authority is misrepresentation.
