# Day 44: Silence as a tool (right questions, part 2)

> **The one idea for today:** The real answer comes in the pause after a good question. If you fill the pause, you lose it.

> Where this fits: yesterday's [Day 43](day-43.md) covered the 6 golden rules for building a right question. Today is about delivery. A good question is wasted if you talk over the 5 to 10 seconds that follow it. For background from First 60 Days, see [Day 47](../../first-60-days/week-8/day-47.md) for the principle, [Day 44](../../first-60-days/week-8/day-44.md) for the Q step in objection handling, and the [right-question database](../../first-60-days/_source-scripts/right-question-database.md) for a bank of questions.

By the end of today you'll be able to hold 5 to 10 seconds of silence after a loaded question, tell a prospect who is thinking from one who has checked out and respond to each, and chain questions so each answer takes you deeper. That chain is the discovery funnel, and it's what turns a form-filling session into a real fact-find.

---

## Why new FCs miss this

A new FC asks a strong question: *"If something happened to you tomorrow, who'd be most affected financially?"*

The prospect pauses, looks down and starts thinking.

The FC can't stand the quiet and fills it:
- *"I mean, your wife probably, and your kids..."*
- *"Obviously nobody can predict these things but..."*
- *"Maybe we can come back to that later if it's not relevant..."*

The prospect was about to picture a specific scenario, and that picture is what makes cover feel real to them. The FC talked over it to ease their own discomfort.

Asking good questions is half the skill. The other half is keeping quiet long enough for the question to work.

> Three things happen in this order: the cushion ([Day 40](../week-7/day-40.md#cushioning-the-move-under-all-three-tools)), a right question that passes the 3-point checklist ([Day 43](day-43.md#the-3-point-checklist-is-this-a-good-question)), and then silence. Leave out the cushion and the prospect feels argued with. A weak question surfaces the wrong thing. If you skip the silence, their own conclusion never gets a chance to form, so practise all three as one move.

---

## What happens in 10 seconds of silence

Treat this as a rough coaching model. Nobody has timed these stages in a lab, but the order matches what you'll see across the table.

![A rough model of 10 seconds of silence: 0-2 sec register the question; 2-5 sec search for an answer; 5-8 sec a specific image forms; 8-10 sec the feeling lands](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-44-m0.webp)

- 0 to 2 seconds: they register what you asked
- 2 to 5 seconds: they search for an answer
- 5 to 8 seconds: a concrete picture forms (their wife, their kids, a specific scenario)
- 8 to 10 seconds: the feeling that goes with it lands

Cut in early and you usually get the surface answer, which you can't build a pitch on. Wait, and you get the answer with feeling attached.

The waiting part has research behind it. Mary Budd Rowe studied classroom "wait time" for years (her 1986 review is in the *Journal of Teacher Education*), and when teachers waited 3 seconds or more after a question, students gave longer, more considered answers.

---

## Thinking silence and dead silence

Silence comes in two kinds, and you can tell them apart by watching.

### Thinking silence
- Their eyes move: up, to the side, down
- Small facial movement: a frown, a tilt, a slight nod
- Their breathing slows or deepens
- They stay still or lean in slightly

What to do: hold. Don't speak. Give them longer than feels comfortable.

### Dead silence
- Their eyes glaze over or go to their phone
- They look closed off or bored
- They've pulled back physically (leaned away, arms crossed)
- Their face is flat, with no small movements

What to do: give them a way out. *"Am I going too deep? Happy to come back to that after we cover some basics."* That releases them without forcing an answer.

The quick check: watch their eyes for 3 seconds. Moving eyes usually mean thinking. Glazed eyes mean you've lost them.

## The discovery funnel

One question rarely finds the real hot button. A chain of questions, each going a level deeper, usually does. The chain runs generic, then specific, then emotional, then actionable.

Here's how a chain on retirement might run with a prospect in their fifties:

1. Start generic, and you ask *"What does retirement look like for you?"* and get *"Oh, travel, spend time with the grandkids, the usual."*
2. Get specific with *"When you picture the travel, what is it? Anywhere in particular?"* This time the answer has detail: *"Actually, I've always wanted to do three months around Europe with my wife. We talked about it 5 years ago."*
3. Go to the feeling with *"What's kept that from happening?"* Now you hear the real block: *"Honestly? Money. We keep saying next year."*
4. Make it actionable with *"If the numbers worked, and you knew enough was coming in each month whether or not you were working, how soon would you go?"* The reply you build on: *"Next year. We're not getting any younger."*

The fourth answer is the one you build the pitch around. The first answer ("travel, spend time with the grandkids") would have given you a generic retirement pitch. The fourth gives you a specific one: how much income they need, and by when, to take that trip.

Don't stop at the generic answer. Keep going until you reach something specific, felt and actionable.

---

## The "tell me more" family

Four neutral follow-ups that keep a chain going without steering the prospect:

| Prompt | When to use it |
|---|---|
| *"Tell me more about that."* | The answer feels thin and you sense there's more |
| *"What do you mean by [their word]?"* | They used a word worth unpacking |
| *"How come?"* | They stated a preference and you want the reason |
| *"And what else?"* | They answered but might have more to add |

These are open prompts. They don't push toward the answer you'd like, they just give the prospect room to keep talking.

*"How come?"* is the one to get comfortable with. It asks the same thing as *"why?"*, but *"why?"* can sound like an interrogation, especially twice in a row. *"How come?"* sounds curious.

## Noticing what they didn't say

Part of good listening is noticing what's missing from an answer, and the gaps are often where the useful questions are hiding. For example:

- A retirement picture with no spouse in it may mean the plan isn't a shared one yet.
- Financial priorities that never mention the kids may mean the kids aren't the hot button you assumed.
- If they talk about cover and skip critical illness entirely, it could be fear, or they may simply not know about it.
- When money isn't on their list at all, a pitch about growing wealth probably won't move them.

Each of these is a guess you still have to check. Ask gently, and make it easy to decline:

> *"I noticed we haven't talked about [X]. Is that just not on your mind right now, or would you rather leave it for another time? Either is completely fine."*

Asked like that, most people answer honestly, and the ones who don't want to go there can say so without awkwardness.

---

## Drill: the 10-second hold

You build this through reps:

1. Ask a loaded question (from Day 40's list or the funnel above).
2. Count silently in your head: one thousand, two thousand...
3. Hold until 8 or 10.
4. When they speak first, you held long enough.
5. No answer by 10? Check which kind of silence it is. Hold longer for thinking, and offer a way out for checked-out.

Expect to crack at around 4 the first few times. For the next 2 weeks, count on every loaded question you ask.

## Quiz

**Q1. The most useful thing to do in the 10 seconds after a loaded question is:**
- A) Add context so they understand the question
- B) Nothing. Hold the silence until they speak ✓
- C) Ask a clarifying sub-question
- D) Make a joke to ease the tension

**Why:** In that window the prospect is forming a specific picture, and the feeling that goes with it arrives last. Talk over it and you get a generic answer. Most new FCs fill the silence after 2 or 3 seconds, which is why their fact-finds stay on the surface.

**Q2. You can tell thinking silence from dead silence by:**
- A) How long it lasts
- B) Eye movement, posture and small facial movements. Thinking silence has movement in it, and dead silence is glazed and flat ✓
- C) The prospect's DISC profile
- D) Whether you asked a good question

**Why:** A long silence can be either kind. Ten seconds of deep thought and ten seconds of checking a phone take the same time. Look for movement in the stillness: moving eyes, small expressions, a change in breathing. Reading it correctly lets you hold one kind longer and step in sooner on the other.

**Q3. In a discovery funnel, the generic answer (*"travel, spend time with family"*) is:**
- A) Good enough, so move on to the next topic
- B) The starting point for a chain of deeper questions that reach something specific, felt and actionable ✓
- C) A sign the prospect is evasive
- D) All prospects ever give

**Why:** Generic answers are the ones people have ready. Build a pitch on one and the pitch comes out generic too. Keep chaining (*"what specifically?"*, then *"what's kept that from happening?"*, then *"if the numbers worked, how soon?"*) until you reach the answer you can build on.

**Q4. In the rough 10-second model, when does the feeling behind the answer land?**
- A) Second 1
- B) Second 3
- C) Seconds 8 to 10 ✓
- D) Second 30

**Why:** In the model, they register the question first (0 to 2), then search (2 to 5), then a picture forms (5 to 8), then the feeling lands (8 to 10). The timings are approximate, but if you cut in early you tend to get the surface answer.

**Q5. *"How come?"* often works better than *"why?"* because:**
- A) It's shorter
- B) It's softer. It asks the same question without the edge that can make people defensive ✓
- C) It's more formal
- D) It forces the prospect to repeat their answer

**Why:** *"Why?"* can feel like cross-examination, especially when you ask it back to back. *"How come?"* sounds like curiosity. Over a fact-find with dozens of follow-ups, the tone adds up.

**Q6. Noticing what a prospect *didn't* say matters because:**
- A) It's gossip
- B) Gaps can point to something worth asking about gently, like a spouse who never comes up in their retirement plans ✓
- C) It fills notes
- D) It's never reliable

**Why:** What people leave out can tell you as much as what they stress. Treat a gap as a guess, not a conclusion, and ask in a way that lets them decline: *"Is that just not on your mind right now, or would you rather leave it for another time?"*

**Q7. The drill for building up your tolerance for silence is:**
- A) Memorise longer questions
- B) Count silently after each loaded question and hold until 8 or 10 before you speak ✓
- C) Avoid loaded questions
- D) Ask shorter questions

**Why:** Counting turns a vague feeling ("it felt long") into a number you can track ("I held for 6"). Most people crack around 4 at first. Two weeks of counting on every loaded question builds the habit.
