## 1. CPF Contribution Caps

### Monthly Ordinary Wage Ceiling: $6,800
- Only the first **$6,800** of monthly income is subject to CPF contributions
- Any income above $6,800 → no CPF contribution required on the excess
- Example: Earning $10,000/month → CPF applies to $6,800 only; the remaining $3,200 is taken home in full (no CPF deduction)
- Even someone earning $50,000/month only contributes CPF on the first $6,800

### Annual CPF Contribution Cap: $102,000
- Formula: **17 months × $6,000** = $102,000 (based on the $6,000 ceiling from 2022; maintained at $102,000 even after monthly ceiling increased to $6,800)
- This cap includes salary **and** bonuses
- Anything above $102,000 in total wages for the year → no CPF contribution on the excess

### Maximum CPF Contribution Per Year (Per Job): $37,740
- $102,000 × 37% = **$37,740**
- This is the absolute maximum that can flow into CPF from one job in a year, regardless of how high the salary or bonus

---

## 2. Worked Examples

### Employee A – Earns $2,000/month
- $2,000 < $6,800 → full salary subject to CPF
- Annual salary: $24,000
- Take-home: 80% (employee contributes 20%)
- Employer contributes 17%
- Total CPF: $8,880/year

### Employee B – Earns $10,000/month
- CPF applies only to $6,800/month → $6,800 × 12 = $81,600 subject to CPF
- Monthly CPF contribution: $6,800 × 37% = $2,516; annually = ~$30,192 (capped figures apply)
- Excess $3,200/month → 100% take-home
- Even at $15,000/month, the monthly CPF amount stays capped at $3,192

### Bonus Calculations
- Bonus CPF cap = $102,000 − salary already contributed
- Employee A (salary $24,000): bonus cap = $78,000 before CPF maxes out
- If bonus = $36,000 → still under $78,000 → full bonus subject to CPF (take-home = 80%)
- If bonus = $48,000 → exceeds $78,000 → CPF maxes at $37,740 total; excess taken home in full
- Employee B (salary used: $81,600): bonus cap = $20,400
- Even an 18-month bonus of $180,000 → only $20,400 subject to CPF; max contribution still $37,740

## 4. CPF Interest Rates

### Base Rates
- **OA (Ordinary Account):** 2.5%
- **SA/MA/RA (Special, MediSave, Retirement):** 4%

### SMRA Interest Rate (Special/MediSave/Retirement Account)
- Currently ~**4.08%**
- Since 2008, CPF no longer guarantees a flat 4% for SMRA
- Actual formula: **10-Year Singapore Government Securities (SGS) yield + 1%**
- When the formula yielded less than 4%, the government extended a **4% floor** year by year (announced each September)
- Recent quarters (4.04%–4.08%) suggest the formula now naturally exceeds 4% → the government may eventually remove the annual floor extension
- If the floor is removed and rates drop: you'd get the formula rate, with a minimum floor of **2.5%**

---

## 5. Additional CPF Interest (Extra Interest)

### Below Age 55
- **Extra 1%** on the first **$60,000** of combined CPF balances
- Maximum extra interest: **$600/year**
- Credited to **Special Account (SA)**

### Above Age 55
- **Extra 2%** on the first **$30,000** + **extra 1%** on the next **$30,000**
- Maximum extra interest: **$900/year**
- Credited to **Retirement Account (RA)**

### Hierarchy for the $60,000 Calculation
**RA → OA (capped at $20,000) → SA (no cap) → MA**

#### Example 1: OA $100K / SA $100K / MA $60K (below 55, no RA)
- First $20,000 from OA → earns extra 1% = $200 (credited to SA)
- Next $40,000 from SA → earns extra 1% = $400
- **Total extra: $600**

#### Example 2: OA $0 (used for property) / SA $100K / MA $60K
- Full $60,000 drawn from SA (no cap) → extra 1% = $600
- **Still gets full $600**

#### Example 3: OA $0 / SA $40K (rest invested) / MA $60K
- $40,000 from SA → $400
- $20,000 from MA → $200
- **Still gets $600**
- If MA hits Basic Retirement Sum (BRS), the $200 overflows to SA

## 6. Why CPF LIFE Payouts Are Higher Than Commercial Annuities

Comparing CPF LIFE vs. a commercial annuity, both purchased at age 55 with $205,800 (FRS):

- Commercial annuity premium = $205,800 (that's it)
- CPF LIFE effective premium = $205,800 **+ additional interest of $900/year** from age 55–65 and beyond (as long as RA balance > $60,000)
- The actuary factors in this extra interest when calculating the monthly payout → CPF LIFE pays **more per month**

---

## 7. Why BRS/FRS/ERS Payouts Are NOT Proportional

### The Numbers
- **BRS** = 1× (~$102,900) → payout ~$900
- **FRS** = 2× (~$205,800) → payout should be ~$1,800 but is less
- **ERS** = 3× (~$308,700) → payout should be ~$2,700 but is less

### CPF's Official Explanation
Different interest rates across balance tiers (first $30K at 6%, next $30K at 5%, balance at 4%) cause non-proportional growth.

### Simpler Explanation
All three groups (BRS, FRS, ERS) receive the **same $900/year** in additional interest (since all have >$60K in RA). That $900 compounded over 10 years ≈ ~$10,000 at age 65.

- $10,000 added to a small BRS base = **significant** boost (percentage-wise)
- $10,000 added to a large ERS base = **less significant**

**Analogy:** Three children get $5, $10, $15 allowance. Each gets an extra $5. The child with $5 sees a 100% increase; the child with $15 sees only a 33% increase.

### Key Takeaway
- BRS is NOT inherently "the best value" — all plans are good
- The non-proportionality is simply a result of a fixed additional interest amount being spread across different base sizes
- **Do not conclude clients should choose BRS for better "returns"**

---

## 8. CPF LIFE Plan Selection – Advisory Approach

### General Practice
- Before age 64–65: always **assume CPF LIFE Standard** for planning purposes
- Only discuss plan choice when client is **turning 64/approaching 65**

### Decision Framework (at age 65)
- **Default is FRS** at age 55
- To upgrade to ERS: go online after 55, top up via cash or CPF (simple QR code process)

### When to recommend **Standard** (higher monthly payout, lower bequest):
- Client has limited savings and needs maximum monthly income
- Legacy/bequest is not a priority

### When to recommend **Basic** (lower monthly payout, higher bequest):
- Client has significant liabilities and needs legacy planning
- Bequest value matters due to limited other assets

### Best Practice
- Don't calculate IRR or push one plan over another
- Let client visit **CPF Board** for a consultation first
- Listen to what CPF Board advised and how the client feels
- Only offer your opinion if the client's choice contradicts their actual situation
- **Let the client decide**

> *"CPF/retirement planning is selfish planning — you must make provision for yourself first."*
> Most clients choose **CPF LIFE Standard** because they prioritize their own retirement income over leaving a larger bequest.

## Purpose of the Retirement Cash Flow Spreadsheet

The spreadsheet enables advisors to **factor CPF into retirement planning** for clients. It projects CPF balances from the current date to age 55 and beyond (to the client's desired retirement age), based on assumptions about the client's circumstances and CPF constraints.

### Key CPF Constraints Built Into the Spreadsheet

- OW (Ordinary Wage) ceiling
- AW (Additional Wage) ceiling
- BHS (Basic Healthcare Sum) limit
- CPF contribution rates by age band
- CPF interest rates by account
- Deductions (housing, insurance, investments)

### Important Caveat

The projection numbers are **not accurate to the decimal place** — they are estimations to facilitate planning. This must be kept in mind when presenting to clients.

---

## The Five-Stage Planning Framework

The spreadsheet supports all five stages of retirement planning (covered in detail in subsequent modules):

| Stage | Purpose |
|-------|---------|
| Stage 1 | How much and in what form does CPF return your money |
| Stage 2 | Is it enough for your desired lifestyle |
| Stage 3 | Identify other financial assets for retirement |
| Stage 4 | Merge everything with CPF and reassess |
| Stage 5 | Fine-tune and bridge any remaining gap |

---

## CPF Contribution Rate Changes for Age 55+

### Background

A key recommendation made in 2019 was to **increase CPF contribution rates for workers aged 55 and above** to boost retirement adequacy. All increments from these changes are allocated to the **Special Account (SA)** only.

### The Long-Term Targets

| Age Band | Pre-2022 Rate | Long-Term Target |
|----------|--------------|-----------------|
| Above 55 | 26% | **37%** |
| Above 60 | 16.5% | **26%** |
| Above 65 | 12.5% | **16.5%** |

### Progressive Implementation Timeline

| Year | Above 55 | Above 60 | Above 65 |
|------|----------|----------|----------|
| Pre-2022 | 26% | 16.5% | 12.5% |
| Jan 2022 | 28% (+2%) | 18.5% (+2%) | 14% (+1.5%) |
| Jan 2023 | 29.5% | 20.5% | 15.5% |
| Jan 2024 | 31% | 22% | 16.5% ✓ (target met) |
| By 2030 | 37% ✓ | 26% ✓ | — |

The above-65 age band reached its target in 2024. The other two bands will continue increasing progressively through to 2030.

---

## Two Spreadsheet Versions Explained

Because changes are being phased in over multiple years, two versions of the spreadsheet exist to project more accurately for different client age groups.

### Version Selection Rule

| Client Birth Year | Version | Rationale |
|-------------------|---------|-----------|
| **Born 1975 onwards** | V1a (e.g., 23 V1a) | These clients won't turn 55 before 2030. By then, all rate changes will be fully implemented. Uses the 2030 target rates for projection. |
| **Born before 1975** | V1 (e.g., 23 V1) | These clients will turn 55 before 2030 and are affected by the progressive annual changes. Uses the current/near-term contribution rates. |

### Practical Note

The V1 version factors in the **next calendar year's** contribution rates (e.g., version 23 V1 uses 2024 rates rather than 2023 rates, since the new rates take effect within a few months). This provides a slightly more accurate projection without requiring an immediate update when the new year begins.

## What Is Stage 1 Planning?

Stage 1 is the first of five stages in the retirement planning process. Its purpose is to **find out what basic retirement scheme CPF provides**, answering the first two of three key questions:

1. **How much** can I look forward to get back from CPF when I retire?
2. **In what form** can I expect to receive it?

(The third question — "Is it enough?" — is answered in Stage 2.)

### What Stage 1 Produces

A **one-page report** (the "Report at 55" tab) showing:

- **Monthly income from 65:** Projected CPF LIFE payout per month, for life
- **Lump sum at 55:** The excess CPF money above the Full Retirement Sum, withdrawable anytime

---

## Essential Fact-Find Information for Stage 1

| Field | Details |
|-------|---------|
| Name | Client's name |
| Date of birth | Key in as DD-MMM-YY format (e.g., "20 Aug 88") to avoid computer date format errors |
| Gender | Male or Female — affects CPF LIFE payout projection |
| Monthly income | As accurate as possible — drives the CPF contribution projection |
| Bonus (months) | Use contractual bonus only (be conservative, not optimistic) |
| Annual salary increment | Default 3%. Put 0% if client has reached a salary ceiling |
| Statement date | The date the CPF balances were extracted — must correspond to the three balances |
| Three CPF balances | OA, SA, MA as of the statement date |
| Housing loan | Monthly amount and end age (if financed by CPF OA) |

**Statement date is critical:** The report is only accurate as of that date. The balances and the date must match — they represent a snapshot in time.

---

## Data Entry: Step by Step

### Basic Information (Rows 4–38 of Input Tab)

1. Key in name → Tab to next field
2. Date of birth → use **DD-MMM-YY** format (e.g., "20 Aug 88") to avoid format errors across different computer settings
3. Gender → default is Male; click to change to Female
4. Monthly income → enter exact figure
5. Bonus months → enter contractual months only
6. Annual salary increment → default 3%; change to 0% if salary has hit a ceiling
7. Statement date → DD-MMM-YY format; the "Age as of statement date" auto-calculates once both DOB and statement date are entered
8. Three CPF balances → OA, SA, MA
9. RA balance → leave blank (client hasn't turned 55)

### Reset Before Each New Case

Before entering a new client's data, always click the **Reset button** at the bottom of the Input tab. This clears previous client data while preserving essential defaults (like the D65 retirement age setting).

There is also a **Clear button** that removes everything including defaults — not recommended unless you want to start completely from scratch.

---

## Deduction Fields & How They Appear in Projections

### Housing Loan

- Enter monthly amount and end age
- Leave start date blank for current loans (deductions begin immediately)
- In the CPF Projection tab: the amount appears as a monthly deduction from OA until the specified end age

### CareShield Life (CSL) / ElderShield (ES)

| Setting | Behaviour in Projection |
|---------|------------------------|
| **CSL, no rider** | Premium deducted in July each year, increasing at 2% annually |
| **CSL, with rider** | Premium + $600 rider, deducted in July, increasing at 2% |
| **ES, no rider** | Premium deducted in July each year, flat (no increase) |
| **ES, with rider** | Premium + $600, deducted in July, flat |

- The projection tab header changes to reflect the selection (e.g., "CSL/Rider" or "ES with Rider")
- Premium amount must be extracted from the client's CPF transactional history

### Integrated Shield Plan (IP)

| Setting | Behaviour |
|---------|-----------|
| **Yes** (has IP/rider) | MSL premium + $600 AWL cap deducted from MediSave. AWL increases by age band ($600 → $600 → $900) |
| **No** (MSL only) | MSL premium only (at 9% GST), no rider amount added |

- Default is **Yes** (most clients have an IP)
- MSL premium is calculated based on the applicable MediShield Life premium schedule with 9% GST

### CPFIS Investments (OA and SA)

- If client has regular annual CPFIS deductions, enter the annual amount and the end age
- OA investment: deducted every January from OA
- SA investment: deducted every January from SA
- These appear in the CPF Projection tab as January deductions each year until the specified end age

---

## Reading the One-Page Report (Report at 55)

After entering all data, click the **Report at 55** tab. The report shows:

### Projected Balances at 55
- OA: projected amount
- SA: projected amount
- MA: projected amount

### Full Retirement Sum (FRS)
- The projected FRS the client must set aside in the RA at age 55
- CPF takes from SA first; if SA is insufficient, the balance comes from OA
- The report confirms whether the client can meet the FRS requirement

### Two Forms of CPF Return

| Form | Details |
|------|---------|
| **Monthly income (from 65)** | The FRS grows with interest to 65 → used to buy CPF LIFE (annuity) → projected monthly payout for life |
| **Lump sum (at 55)** | Excess = (OA + SA) − FRS → withdrawable anytime, many times, via PayNow ($50K per transaction) |

---

## Presenting the One-Page Report

### Presentation Script

> "Mr Young Man, this report answers the first two of three questions about your CPF. How much are you going to get back, and in what form."

> "We projected your current CPF balances to age 55. Your projected OA is about $804,000, SA about $487,000, and MediSave about $169,000."

> "At 55, CPF creates a Retirement Account and sets aside the Full Retirement Sum — projected at $395,600 for your case. Can you meet it? Yes — your SA alone has more than enough."

> "What happens with that $395,600? CPF grows it to age 65, then uses it to buy CPF LIFE — an annuity that pays you a projected **$3,140/month for the rest of your life** starting at 65."

> "The second form: after setting aside the FRS, your remaining OA + SA balance is your **excess money — projected at $895,000**. This can be withdrawn anytime, many times, after age 55."

> "In summary: CPF returns your money as a **lump sum of $895,000 at 55**, and **monthly income of $3,140 from 65 for life**."

### The Closing Question

> **"How do you like this basic retirement plan that you have by CPF?"**

Then pause. Let the client respond. Echo whatever they say — don't add commentary. This transitions naturally into Stage 2.

### Disclaimer Reminder

Always note that the report is accurate **as of the statement date**. The client's actual CPF balances may have changed since that date.

## Key Takeaways

- Stage 1 helps the client **appreciate** their CPF retirement savings — what they have and what form it takes
- The entire process requires minimal information: name, DOB, gender, income, bonus, statement date, three balances, and housing loan details
- Data entry takes **under 5 minutes**
- The one-page report answers **how much** and **in what form**
- Always present with the closing question to naturally lead into Stage 2
- Use the **Reset button** before every new case to avoid data errors from previous entries

## Assumptions for Property Purchase

- **Key collection:** June 2026 (approximately 3 years from statement date)
- **Down payment:** From CPF OA (wipe out OA, keep $20,000)

## Step-by-Step: Simulating the Purchase

### Step 1: Find the Projected OA Balance at Purchase Date

Go to **Input tab → CPF Option section** → enter the down payment date as **one month before** key collection (conservative approach).

- Key collection: June 2026 → Down payment: **May 2026**
- Enter May 2026 in the date field → the system shows the projected OA balance (e.g., $221,177)

**Why one month before?** CPF contributions don't arrive on the 1st of the month — they come mid-month. By using the previous month's balance, you exclude the uncertain May contribution. This is the conservative, safe approach.

**Verification:** Go to CPF Projection tab → find April 2026 → the OA balance matches what's shown in the Input tab.

### Step 2: Enter the Down Payment (Housing Single Payment)

- The prompt says: "Amount should not be greater than projected OA in cell G62"
- Client decides to keep $20,000 behind → pays $200,000 (out of $221,177 available)
- Enter **$200,000** in the Housing Single Payment field

**In the CPF Projection tab:** The $200,000 deduction appears in May 2026. OA balance drops to ~$22,000 after payment.

### Step 3: Calculate the Loan Amount

- Property price: $500,000
- Down payment from CPF OA: $200,000
- **Loan amount: $300,000**

### Step 4: Calculate Monthly Installment

Go to **Calculator Quick Link** tab → click the **Financial Calculator** link → select **Loan Calculator**.

Enter three values:
- Loan amount: **$300,000**
- Interest rate: **2.6%** (HDB loan rate)
- Tenure: **20 years**, 0 months

Click Calculate → Monthly installment = **~$1,643**

Client and spouse share equally → **$800 each** (rounded up slightly)

### Step 5: Enter the Monthly Housing Loan

Go back to **Input tab → Deduction section → Housing Loan:**

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Monthly housing loan | $800 |
| Start date | **June 2026** (one month after down payment) |
| Liable until age | = 37.78 + 20 = **57.78** years |

**How to find the start age (37.78):** Go to CPF Projection tab → find June 2026 → read the age column.

**Tip:** In the "liable until" field, enter the formula **=37.78+20** so you can see how the number was derived. This helps during future reviews.

### Current Loan vs. Future Loan — The Start Date Rule

| Scenario | Start Date Field | Behaviour |
|----------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Future loan** (not yet started) | Enter the start month/year (e.g., June 2026) | Deductions begin from that date |
| **Current loan** (already paying) | **Leave blank** | Deductions begin immediately from the statement date |

If you accidentally leave the start date blank for a future loan, the system treats it as a current loan and starts deducting from the first month — this will corrupt your projection.

### Step 6: Verify in CPF Projection Tab

After entering all housing data:
- The $200,000 lump sum deduction appears in May 2026
- The $800 monthly deduction starts in June 2026
- Monthly deductions continue for exactly **240 months** (20 years × 12)
- The last deduction falls at age 57.78
- All deductions come from OA

### Planning for above 55

https://youtu.be/5aDonZG9TB4
# Lecture Notes

## Why Above 55 Is Easier Than Below 55

For clients below 55, you project CPF balances forward using assumptions (salary, contributions, interest). For clients above 55, **there's no projection needed** — the balances are current, real numbers.

### Two Things CPF Returns

Always remember: CPF returns money in two forms — **lump sum** and **income**.

| Form | For Above 55 |
|------|-------------|
| **Lump sum** | OA + SA (next year, just OA after SA closure). No calculation — it's the current balance, withdrawable now. |
| **Monthly income** | CPF LIFE payout or RSS payout. You need to find this number and key it into the spreadsheet manually. |

### The One Extra Field

The only difference between below-55 and above-55 input is **one additional item: the RA balance**. Everything else (name, DOB, gender, statement date, balances, deductions) is the same.

---

## The Three Groups (Albert's Classification)

**Do NOT go to CPF Board and ask about "Group 1, 2, 3" — this is Albert's own categorization for teaching purposes.**

| Group | Born | CPF LIFE Status | Selection Age |
|-------|------|----------------|---------------|
| **Group 1** | Before 1 Jan 1958 | Optional (default is RSS) | Can opt in before age 80 |
| **Group 2** | 1 Jan 1958 – 30 Jun 1960 | Compulsory (pioneers of CPF LIFE) | Selected at age 55 |
| **Group 3** | After 30 Jun 1960 | Compulsory | Select at age 65, defer to 70 |

### How to Identify the Group

- If born **before 1960 or after 1960** → year alone tells you the group
- If born **in 1960** → you must know the **month**: first half (Jan–Jun) = Group 2, second half (Jul–Dec) = Group 3

---

## Group 3: Using CPF LIFE Estimator

Group 3 clients **haven't bought CPF LIFE yet** — they can still choose between Standard, Basic, or Escalating. Use the CPF LIFE Estimator to generate a projected payout.

### How to Access CPF LIFE Estimator

1. Go to the **Calculator Quick Link** in the spreadsheet → click CPF LIFE Estimator link
2. **Must log in with SingPass** (changed from previous open access)
3. After login, go to Calculators → **Monthly Payout Estimator**
4. Select gender, birth year, enter RA balance
5. For planning purposes, always select **Standard Plan** (highest payout among the three)
6. Click Next → projected monthly payout appears

### What to Do with the Number

- The CPF LIFE Estimator result does **not** auto-populate into the spreadsheet
- You must manually key it into cell **H46** (the CPF LIFE monthly income field)
- Also set the CPF LIFE start age (default 65, can change to 70 for deferment)

### Critical: CPF LIFE Amount and Start Age Must Match

If you key in a deferred amount (e.g., $1,670 at 70) but forget to change the start age from 65 to 70:
- The report will show the wrong start age
- The cash flow will incorrectly show payouts starting at 65
- **Both fields must be updated together**

### Critical: Use Current RA Balance, Not Outdated Values

The CPF LIFE Estimator treats whatever RA balance you enter as **today's value**. If you use a 2021 RA balance in 2024:
- The system doesn't know it's outdated
- It calculates as if the client has that amount today
- The projection will be **lower than reality** (because the RA has been growing at 4% annually)

> **Always use the most current RA balance** from this year's statement or the client's live CPF account.

---

## Group 2: The Pioneers of CPF LIFE

Group 2 clients are **policyholders** — they already selected and bought CPF LIFE at age 55. You cannot generate a new quotation for them. You must find their existing payout information.

### Three Ways to Find Group 2 Payout (in order of preference)

| Method | What You Get |
|--------|-------------|
| **1. YS OA (Annual Statement)** | Plan selected, projected payout range, premium paid — **best and most reliable method** |
| **2. Policy Certificate** | Plan, premium, projected payout — extremely rare, most clients don't have it |
| **3. CPF Hotline** | Ask three questions: What plan? How much paid? What's the projected monthly payout? — most clients won't call |

### How to Extract from YS OA

- Find the CPF LIFE section in the annual statement
- Note the projected monthly payout (usually shown as a range)
- **Take the average of the range** → key into the spreadsheet
- Note the premium paid (confirms it's Group 2)

### WARNING: Do NOT Use CPF LIFE Estimator for Group 2

Group 2 clients' RA balances appear **abnormally low** (roughly BRS level) because half was taken as the first premium installment. If you naively enter this low RA into the CPF LIFE Estimator:

- You'll get a projected payout of perhaps $590 instead of the actual $1,300+
- The client (who reads their own statements) will know you're wrong
- **You will lose all credibility** — the client will not buy anything from you

> "You just made a fool of yourself. Very scary."

### Why Group 2 RA Looks Low

**Example:** Client turned 55 in 2015. FRS was $155,000. At 55, $155,000 set aside in RA. Immediately, half ($77,500) deducted as first CPF LIFE premium installment. RA shows only $77,500 (or its grown value, ~$99,000 by 2021).

The remaining RA balance grows with interest until 65, when it's taken as the second installment. After that, RA = $0.

### Group 2 Disadvantages (Why They Changed to Group 3)

1. **Selected too early (at 55):** Could choose the wrong plan, cannot change afterward
2. **Lost interest on premium paid:** The $77,500 paid at 55 earns no interest for the policyholder. If the client dies before 65, the family gets back only $77,500 (the premium) — not $99,000+ (the premium with interest). They lose ~$20,000+ in interest.
3. **Same payout as Group 3:** Despite paying earlier and losing interest, Group 2 receives the exact same monthly payout as Group 3

## Group 1: Born Before 1958 (RSS by Default)

### Key Characteristics

- Default scheme is **Retirement Sum Scheme (RSS)** — not CPF LIFE
- RSS pays a fixed monthly amount until the RA is **fully depleted** (not for life)
- They have the **option** to switch to CPF LIFE before age 80
- Most are already 66+ years old — majority have started collecting

### How to Determine If They've Opted for CPF LIFE

**Always check the YS OA.** It will clearly state either:
- RSS: "You will receive a monthly payout of $X until you fully withdraw your CPF savings"
- CPF LIFE: Shows the CPF LIFE plan selected and projected payout

### Scenario A: Already Collecting RSS (Most Common)

- The YS OA states the monthly payout amount directly
- **Just key that number into the spreadsheet** — no calculation needed

**Example:** Client born 1956. YS OA 2021 states: "You will receive a monthly payout of $2,180 until you fully withdrawn your CPF savings."
→ Key in **$2,180**. Done.

### Scenario B: Deferred, Not Yet Collecting (Rare)

- The YS OA says: "You may instruct us to start your monthly payout anytime from 65"
- You need to calculate the projected payout
- The **RSS Payout Calculator** exists but is only accessible via the **member's own SingPass** (advisors cannot access it)

**Workaround:** Use the **CPF LIFE Estimator** with the **Standard Plan** to estimate. Standard gives the highest payout among the three CPF LIFE plans, which is closest to the RSS payout (since RSS also has higher payouts than CPF LIFE).

**Example:** Client born 1955, RA = $93,000. Used CPF LIFE Estimator Standard → $547/month estimate. Client then logged in herself with SingPass to the actual RSS calculator → actual answer was **$572/month**. Difference: ~$25 (close enough for initial planning). Update the spreadsheet once the actual number is confirmed.

**RSS duration:** The calculator also shows how many years the payout lasts (e.g., 23 years from age 66 = until age 89). RSS is not for life — once RA is depleted, payouts stop.

### Scenario C: Group 1 Who Opted for CPF LIFE

Some Group 1 clients voluntarily chose CPF LIFE. The YS OA will show the CPF LIFE plan selected.

**Example:** Client born before 1958, RA = $235,000. Looks like RSS (high RA). But YS OA shows: "CPF LIFE Basic selected. Premium paid to date: $18,500."

Why only $18,500 on a $235,000 RA? Because **CPF LIFE Basic only takes 10% of RA as premium** (not 50%). For Group 1/2 who opted for Basic, the first installment is half of 10% = **5%**. The second installment (at 65) is another 5%. Total: 10%. The remaining 90% stays in RA and draws down to age 90.

---

## CPF LIFE Basic: The 10% vs 20% Rule

### Why the Percentage Changes

Think of it like an insurance product:

| Start Age | Premium % | Accumulation Period to 90 | Logic |
|-----------|-----------|--------------------------|-------|
| 65 | **10%** of RA | 25 years | More time to grow → lower premium needed |
| 70 | **20%** of RA | 20 years | Less time to grow → higher premium needed |
| 65–70 | **Interpolated** | — | Proportional between 10% and 20% |

The payout from 90 onward is the same regardless of when you start. But starting later means less accumulation time, so a larger premium is required.

### How Basic Works for Each Group

| Group | At 55 | At 65 | RA Remaining |
|-------|-------|-------|-------------|
| **Group 3** | N/A | 10% taken as premium | 90% in RA (draws down to 90) |
| **Group 1 & 2** (opted for Basic) | 5% taken (half of 10%) | Another 5% taken | 90% net in RA |

This explains why Group 1/2 clients who chose CPF LIFE Basic still show very high RA balances — 90% of their money is still there.

## Handling Retirees: Key in Age Next Birthday

### The Rule

For clients who are **already retired**, do not key in:
- Their actual retirement age (historical — the system cannot plan for the past)
- Their current exact age (may be a partial year)

**Always key in the age at their next birthday.** This is the first full future year the system can plan for.

### Example

| Client Detail | Value |
|---------------|-------|
| Current age | 57.12 years |
| Actually retired at | 55 |
| Income | $0 (retired) |

| Retirement Age Entered | Result |
|------------------------|--------|
| 55 | ❌ Error — historical, cannot plan |
| 57 | ❌ Error — partial year, cannot plan |
| **58** | ✅ Correct — first full future year |

> "My program is a planning tool. We plan for the future, we don't plan for history. We learn from history, we plan for future."

Even if a client is 57.9 years old, you must enter **58** — the system needs a full year to begin planning.

### CPF Option Lump Sum Withdrawal

https://youtu.be/zAePhfEZmdM
# Lecture Notes

## Purpose

This module teaches how to simulate a client withdrawing money from the **excess fund (above FRS)** between age 55 and their desired retirement age — for expenses, children's education, or any other reason — and shows the trade-off on retirement readiness.

---

## The Scenario (Miss Singer)

| Detail | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Stage 1A lifestyle (CPF alone) | $2,100/month (lasts to 85) |
| CPF LIFE payout (from 65) | $1,660/month for life |
| Excess at 55 | $352,791 (withdrawable anytime after 55) |
| Stock balance at 60 (no withdrawals) | ~$552,000 |
| Desired retirement age | 60 |

**Default assumption:** The client doesn't touch the excess fund between 55 and retirement. It continues to grow with 5 more years of CPF contributions (from employment), interest, and the original excess — resulting in ~$552,000 at age 60.

**But what if the client needs some of that money before 60?**

---

## How to Simulate the Withdrawal

### Step 1: Identify When the Client Turns 55

Go to **CPF Projection tab** → scroll to find the month the client crosses age 55.

**Important:** In that exact month, the RA account is created and the FRS (~$205,800) is deducted from SA/OA. You'll see a large drop in the OA balance (e.g., from $267,000 to $61,000). **Do not simulate a withdrawal in that month** — it may cause calculation errors.

**Always use the month AFTER the RA account creation** to avoid crashes.

### Step 2: Enter the Withdrawal in the Input Tab

Scroll down to **CPF Option → Housing Single Payment using CPF OA** (Row 63).

This field was designed for housing down payments, but it can be **repurposed** to simulate any lump sum withdrawal from the OA excess after 55.

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Date | One month after RA creation (e.g., May 2024) |
| Amount | Whatever the client plans to withdraw (e.g., $100,000) |

**Check first:** The projected OA balance at that date (shown in the input tab) tells you the maximum withdrawable. For Miss Singer: ~$291,000 available → she withdraws $100,000.

### Step 3: Verify in CPF Projection Tab

After entering the withdrawal:
- The $100,000 deduction appears in the specified month
- OA balance drops by $100,000 immediately
- All subsequent months show lower OA balances (less compounding from that point)

### Step 4: Check the Impact on Retirement

| Metric | Without Withdrawal | With $100K Withdrawal |
|--------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Stock balance at 60 | ~$552,000 | ~$440,000 |
| Difference | — | −$112,000 (the $100K + lost interest) |

The trade-off is clear: the client withdraws $100,000 but loses more than $100,000 at retirement due to foregone interest accumulation.

---

## What This Withdrawal Could Be Used For

- Children's education expenses
- Medical needs
- Property-related costs
- Any personal reason the client may have
- The spreadsheet doesn't judge — it shows the impact

---

## Critical Assumption: OA Withdrawal Bypass

**CPF policy requires you to withdraw from SA first before touching OA** for those above 55.

This simulation assumes the client has already met their FRS obligation and is able to withdraw from OA **bypassing SA**, using the **four-step strategy** taught in the workshop.

If the client hasn't met FRS or doesn't follow the bypass strategy, withdrawing from OA directly may not be possible in practice.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The **Housing Single Payment field** doubles as a post-55 lump sum withdrawal simulator — you're "borrowing" this feature for a different purpose
- Always simulate the withdrawal **one month after the RA creation month** (not in the same month)
- Every dollar withdrawn early has a compounding cost — $100K withdrawn at 55 costs more than $100K at retirement
- This feature lets you show clients the **exact trade-off** in dollars, helping them make informed decisions about accessing their excess fund early
- The simulation is only valid if the client can execute the OA bypass strategy (FRS must be met)

### CPF Options VHR RSTU

https://youtu.be/UaMiBKU8b3I
# Lecture Notes

## Overview

Two CPF optimization options available in the spreadsheet:

1. **VHR (Voluntary Housing Refund)** — Return cash to CPF OA to boost retirement accumulation
2. **RSU (Retirement Sum Topping Up)** — Top up SA with cash (below 55 only) to boost retirement and enjoy tax relief

---

## Option 1: VHR (Voluntary Housing Refund)

### What Is VHR?

VHR allows a client to **voluntarily return money to their CPF OA** using cash. The amount returned is limited to the **P+I (Principal + Accrued Interest)** — the total amount previously withdrawn from CPF to finance the current property.

### When Is VHR Attractive?

- When interest rates are low (OA earns 2.5% — may beat bank rates in a low-rate environment)
- When the client has surplus cash and wants to lock it into CPF for retirement
- When the client wants to boost OA accumulation significantly

**Note:** In a high interest rate environment, returning money to OA at 2.5% may not be attractive. Timing matters.

### How to Find the P+I Amount

- Log into the client's **CPF account** → the P+I figure is unique to each individual
- It represents the total principal withdrawn for housing + accrued interest on that amount
- The VHR amount **cannot exceed** the P+I figure

### How to Simulate in the Spreadsheet

1. Go to **Input tab → CPF Option section → Voluntary Housing Refund using cash**
2. The prompt states: "Amount must be equal or lesser than P+I"
3. Enter the VHR amount (e.g., $100,000)
4. Enter the date the client plans to make the return (e.g., January 2024 — perhaps using year-end bonus)
5. **Date format:** Use YYYY (four digits for year) to avoid errors

### Verifying in CPF Projection Tab

After entering the VHR:
- Go to **CPF Projection tab** → find the specified month
- Column H shows "Voluntary Housing Refund" — the $100,000 appears as a credit
- Column Q (OA balance) jumps accordingly (e.g., from $289,000 to $389,000)
- This provides a significant boost to CPF accumulation going forward

---

## Option 2: RSU (Retirement Sum Topping Up Scheme)

### What Is RSU?

RSU allows a client to **top up their SA with cash** to boost retirement savings and receive **tax relief** (up to $8,000/year for topping up own account, plus $8,000 for topping up a loved one's account).

### Eligibility

- Client must be **below 55 years old**
- Client's **SA balance must be below the current year's FRS** — there must be room to top up
- If the SA already exceeds the projected FRS, RSU is not possible

### How to Check Eligibility in the Spreadsheet

1. Go to **Input tab → CPF Option → RSU section**
2. The prompt directs you to **CPF Projection tab, Column I**
3. **Column J** shows the maximum RSU allowed for each month
4. If Column J is **empty/zero** → the client's SA already exceeds FRS → **RSU not possible**

### Miss Singer's Original Case: RSU Not Possible

Miss Singer (born 1969) has SA of ~$198,000+ which already exceeds the current FRS → Column J shows no value → she cannot do RSU.

### Modified Case: RSU Possible

To demonstrate, Miss Singer is made 3 years younger (born 1972) with a reduced SA of $150,000:

| Month | Column J (Max RSU) | RSU Entered | Remaining |
|-------|-------------------|-------------|-----------|
| Feb 2023 | $48,110 | $8,000 | ~$40,000 |
| Feb 2024 | ~$21,000+ | $8,000 | ~$13,000 |
| Feb 2025 | $0 | Cannot do | SA has grown past projected FRS |

**Result:** The client could only do RSU **twice** ($8,000 each year) before SA naturally grew past the FRS threshold.

### Important Rules

- Each cell in Column I must be **individually keyed in** — no copy and paste (formulas will break)
- RSU amounts go directly to SA, boosting the 4% compounding
- The $8,000/year limit is for tax relief optimization — you can top up more than $8,000 but only get tax relief on $8,000

---

## Addressing the Advisor's Fear: "If They Do RSU, They Won't Have Budget for My Products"

The spreadsheet demonstrates that RSU eligibility is **self-limiting**:

- A client doing $8,000/year in RSU accelerates their SA toward FRS
- Within 1–3 years, SA exceeds FRS and RSU is no longer possible
- After that, the client has **no more CPF optimization options** — the natural next step is building retirement through insurance and investment products

> "Don't be worried. Share CPF optimization schemes with your client — let them feel good about it. After they've maxed out what CPF can do, the next best thing is our products."

This builds trust and positions the advisor as someone who puts the client's interest first, leading naturally to product conversations.

---

## Key Takeaways

| Scheme | Who | Where | Limit | Tax Relief |
|--------|-----|-------|-------|------------|
| **VHR** | Anyone with a property financed by CPF | Cash → OA | Up to P+I amount | No |
| **RSU** | Below 55, SA below FRS | Cash → SA | Up to FRS cap | Yes ($8,000 self + $8,000 loved one) |

- VHR boosts OA accumulation — best in low interest rate environments
- RSU boosts SA accumulation — self-limiting within 1–3 years
- Both can be simulated in the spreadsheet to show clients the exact impact on their retirement projection
- Both build goodwill and trust with clients, naturally leading to product conversations once CPF optimization is exhausted

### Stage 4 Analysis/Assessment

# Lecture Notes

## Purpose of Stage 4 Assessment

After merging all the client's assets with CPF (Stage 3 & 4 integration), Stage 4 Assessment answers **one key question:**

> **How much has the client provided for themselves — outside CPF — for their retirement years?**

This is the moment of truth: the client sees their total retirement readiness as a percentage of their target lifestyle.

---

## How to Perform the Assessment

### Step 1: Fill the Two Yellow Boxes (Data Extraction File)

There are two yellow boxes to complete:

**Yellow Box 1 — Row 29 (Stage 2 Equivalent):**
- With all assets merged, how long can the client's desired lifestyle ($6,000) last?
- Go to Cash Flow → Column L → find the last positive year
- Example: CPF alone lasted to 65; with all assets, it now lasts to **81**

**Yellow Box 2 — Row 7 (Stage 1A Equivalent):**
- With all assets merged, what lifestyle can everything support from retirement age to 85?
- Perform the same try-and-error as Stage 1A on cell D50
- Reduce the lifestyle amount, check Cash Flow Column L until it reaches 85
- Example: CPF alone = $2,050; with all assets = **$5,300**

### Step 2: Read the Derived Numbers

Once both yellow boxes are filled, the extraction file automatically calculates:

| Metric | Formula | Example |
|--------|---------|---------|
| Client's own provision | Total lifestyle − CPF alone | $5,300 − $2,050 = **$3,250** |
| Achievement percentage | Total ÷ Target | $5,300 ÷ $6,000 = **88%** |
| Remaining gap | Target − Total | $6,000 − $5,300 = **$700 (12%)** |

---

## Four Client Scenarios & Presentation Scripts

### Scenario 1: Nearly There (88%) — Miss Singer, Age 53

> "Miss Singer, thank you for sharing your data. After merging everything with CPF, I'd like to congratulate you. On your own, you've provided yourself **$3,250/month** in today's dollars from age 60 to 85. Very well done."

> "Together with CPF's $2,050, your total is **$5,300** — that's **88%** of your $6,000 target. Remarkable."

> "I strongly encourage you to take up just **one more** savings or investment plan to bridge the gap, and your retirement plan is done. What do you say?"

**Alternative close (if client resists):**

> "If you insist on a $6,000 lifestyle without adding anything, your current readiness only lasts to **age 81**. But if you're willing to live on $5,300, you don't have to do anything — it lasts to 85."

**Why "just one more plan":** Miss Singer is 53 with only ~7 working years left. Not enough runway for multiple small plans — she needs one decisive action to close the gap.

---

### Scenario 2: Halfway There (50%) — Mr Singapore, Middle-Aged, Underperforming

> "Mr Singapore, after merging your assets with CPF, here's what we found. On your own, you've only provided **$950/month** for your retirement years from 60 to 85."

> "Together with CPF's $2,050, your total is **$3,000** — that's **50%** of your $6,000 target. You are still quite far away from your goal."

> "You can't afford not to do anything now. What do you say?"

**Typical response:** "I think $6,000 is too high. Maybe $5,000 is enough."

**Even at $5,000:** The client is still far behind → you can still build a case and close 1–2 plans. The reduced target makes the gap more manageable but doesn't eliminate the need for action.

---

### Scenario 3: Just Starting (50%) — Mr Young Man, Young Professional

> "Mr Young Man, after merging your financial assets with CPF, we found that you've **started** providing for your retirement. On your own, you've done **$950/month** from 60 to 85."

> "Together with CPF's $2,050, you've achieved **$3,000** — that's **50%** of your $6,000 target. You've started well."

> "It's important for you to **continue** taking up savings and investment plans from now to your retirement age to bring this from 50% to 100%. I'd like to propose a **two-agenda meeting** to keep you on track."

**Key difference from Scenario 2:** No urgency to close one big case. The young client has a long runway — the strategy is ongoing follow-up, annual reviews, and progressive plan additions over many years.

---

### Scenario 4: Making Progress (67%) — Mr Middle-Age Man

> "Mr Middle-Age Man, after merging your assets with CPF, here's what we found. On your own, you've provided **$1,950/month** from 60 to 85."

> "Together with CPF's $2,050, your total is **$4,000** — that's **67%** of your $6,000 target. You've been doing the right thing, and you should continue doing the right thing."

> "I strongly encourage you to stay on track so that by retirement, this 67% becomes 100%. I'd like to propose a **two-agenda meeting** to make sure we get there together."

## Presentation Principles Across All Scenarios

### Always Say "On Your Own"

The phrase **"on your own, you have provided yourself..."** is powerful because:
- It separates the client's personal effort from CPF (which is compulsory)
- It highlights personal responsibility and achievement
- It connects to the principle: "The best person to provide for your retirement is the person who loves you the most — and that person is you"

### Always Use Percentage

Stating the achievement as a **percentage of the target** (88%, 67%, 50%) makes the gap tangible and motivating. Most advisors in the industry cannot tell a client their retirement readiness percentage — this is a competitive advantage.

### Adapt Tone to the Situation

| Scenario | Tone | Action |
|----------|------|--------|
| Nearly there (80%+) | Congratulatory, encouraging | Close one plan now |
| Middle ground (60–79%) | Supportive, motivating | Propose two-agenda meeting, continue building |
| Just starting (40–59%) | Encouraging but direct | Propose two-agenda meeting, long-term follow-up |
| Far behind (<40%) | Urgent, matter-of-fact | "You can't afford not to act now" |

### Always End with a Call to Action

- **For near-ready clients:** "Take up one more plan and you're done. What do you say?"
- **For mid-range clients:** "I'd like to propose a two-agenda meeting to keep you on track."
- **For young clients:** "Continue to pick up plans from now to retirement. Let's meet regularly."
- **For resistant clients:** "If you don't act, your readiness only lasts to age X. Is that acceptable to you?"

### Planning for Self Employed

https://youtu.be/2AVyblR7NDc
# Lecture Notes

## Overview

The spreadsheet is built for **employees**, but it can be used as a good estimation tool for self-employed persons, foreigners, and anyone without CPF — with some workarounds and limitations.

---

## Three Types of Self-Employed

### Type 1: MediSave-Only Contributors (e.g., Insurance Agents)

These are Singaporeans/PRs who are self-employed and contribute only to MediSave (MA). Their OA and SA may be zero (or have small balances from previous employment or government grants).

**The MediSave Contribution Cap:**

Self-employed MA contributions are subject to a cap based on age band and capped at a maximum chargeable income:

| Chargeable Income | MA Liability (e.g., age band 10%) |
|-------------------|----------------------------------|
| $50,000 | $5,000 |
| $100,000 | **$7,200** (capped) |
| $1,000,000 | **$7,200** (same cap) |

No matter how much a self-employed person earns, MA contribution maxes out at roughly $7,200/year (varies by age band — younger bands are 8–9%, older bands are 10%).

**Impact on retirement:** At ~$7,000/year, it takes about 10 years just to fill the BHS (Basic Healthcare Sum). After BHS is met, overflow to OA/SA is only a couple of thousand dollars per year — negligible for retirement planning.

**How to input:** Key in everything as normal, but **set salary to zero**. The MA contribution is too small to meaningfully affect retirement projections. Treat the same as a non-CPF contributor for planning purposes.

---

### Type 2: Zero CPF Contributors (Gig Economy / Grab Drivers / Foreigners)

People who earn income but contribute nothing to CPF at all — gig workers, EP holders, work permit holders, foreigners, non-working spouses.

**How to input:**

1. Key in **date of birth** and **statement date** (today's date) — these two fields are mandatory for the spreadsheet to function
2. Set **salary to zero**
3. All CPF balances = zero
4. Report shows nothing; cash flow shows nothing from CPF
5. **Skip Stages 1 and 1A entirely** (no CPF to assess)
6. Go directly to **Stage 2:** Ask retirement age, desired lifestyle, and inflation assumption
7. The cash flow shows the client's full expenditure needs with zero CPF funding
8. Proceed to **Stages 3 & 4:** Capture whatever financial assets the client has outside CPF
9. The gap = 100% of the desired lifestyle (CPF provides nothing)

**Key reminder:** Even with zero CPF, you must still fill in **date of birth** and **statement date** — the system needs these to calculate the client's age for all planning functions.

---

### Type 3: Fixed Annual Voluntary Contributors

Self-employed persons who voluntarily contribute a fixed amount to CPF each year (e.g., $20,000, $30,000, or the maximum $37,740).

**The challenge:** The spreadsheet expects a monthly salary input. You must **reverse-engineer** the salary figure to produce the correct annual CPF contribution.

---

## Reverse-Engineering Salary for Voluntary Contributors

### Method: Work Backward from Annual Contribution

**Step 1:** Divide the annual contribution by 37% (the employee contribution rate for below 55)

**Step 2:** Check if the resulting monthly salary exceeds the OW ceiling ($6,000). If yes, redistribute using the bonus field.

### Example 1: $20,000 Annual Contribution

| Step | Calculation |
|------|-------------|
| Annual salary equivalent | $20,000 ÷ 0.37 = **$54,054** |
| Monthly salary | $54,054 ÷ 12 = **$4,504.50** |
| Check vs ceiling | $4,504 < $6,000 ✓ Accepted |

**Input:** Salary = $4,504.50, Bonus = 0, Increment = 0%

**Verification:** Go to CPF Projection → add up 12 months of contributions = $20,000 ✓

### Example 2: $30,000 Annual Contribution

| Step | Calculation |
|------|-------------|
| Annual salary equivalent | $30,000 ÷ 0.37 = **$81,081** |
| Monthly salary | $81,081 ÷ 12 = **$6,757** |
| Check vs ceiling | $6,757 > $6,000 ✗ **Rejected** |

The system caps contributions at $6,000/month. Keying in $6,757 would only produce ~$26,640 in annual CPF (not $30,000).

**Redistribute using bonus:**

| Step | Calculation |
|------|-------------|
| Months of income | $81,081 ÷ $6,000 = **13.51 months** |
| Input | Salary = $6,000, Bonus = **1.51 months**, Increment = 0% |

**Verification:** CPF Projection → 12 months + 1.51 bonus months of $6,000 × 37% = $30,000 ✓

### Example 3: $37,740 Annual Contribution (Maximum)

| Step | Calculation |
|------|-------------|
| Annual salary equivalent | $37,740 ÷ 0.37 = **$102,000** |
| Monthly salary | $102,000 ÷ 12 = **$8,500** |
| Check vs ceiling | $8,500 > $6,000 ✗ **Rejected** |

**Redistribute:**

| Step | Calculation |
|------|-------------|
| Months of income | $102,000 ÷ $6,000 = **17 months** |
| Input | Salary = $6,000, Bonus = **5 months**, Increment = 0% |

**Verification:** CPF Projection → 12 + 5 = 17 months × $6,000 × 37% = $37,740 ✓

## Critical Rules for Self-Employed Input

### Always Set Increment to 0%

Any salary increment would change the contribution amount year-on-year, breaking the fixed annual contribution assumption.

### Always Set Bonus to 0 Unless Needed

Only use the bonus field when the calculated monthly salary exceeds the $6,000 OW ceiling.

### Must Track Annually

Unlike employees whose contributions are mandatory, self-employed contributions are voluntary. The client says they'll contribute, but might not.

> "You have to meet me regularly to track whether I actually do it or not. If I don't, you have to update the CPF balances. Every time you meet, make sure the numbers are correct."

This is a strong reason for annual reviews with self-employed clients.

## Known Limitation: Above 55 Self-Employed

The spreadsheet uses 37% as the contribution rate for all calculations. For clients **above 55**, the actual rate drops to 28% (or lower for older age bands).

| Scenario | Actual Contribution on $102,000 | Spreadsheet Shows |
|----------|---------------------------------|-------------------|
| Below 55 (37%) | $37,740 | $37,740 ✓ |
| Above 55 (28%) | $28,560 | $28,560 (if salary correctly entered) |

**The issue:** If an above-55 self-employed person contributes the full $37,740 voluntarily, the spreadsheet cannot capture the full amount because it applies the lower age-based contribution rate to the salary figure. The software will show less than the actual contribution.

> "My software is generally for employees but can be used as a good estimation for self-employed. If they are above 55, there is a little bit of shortfall I cannot capture."

This is an acknowledged limitation — no workaround is available without over-complicating the input.

## The Problem: Assets That Don't Fit Tables 1–4

Certain financial products cannot be entered directly into any of the four input tables. This module covers two common scenarios and how to handle them using the **Calculator Quick Link** tab.

---

## Scenario 1: ILP Regular Premium (ILP RP)

### Why It Doesn't Fit

An ILP RP (Investment-Linked Plan with Regular Premium) cannot go into:

- **Table 1** (single output, no investment risk) — ILP has investment risk
- **Table 2** (single output, with investment risk) — Table 2 expects a current value that grows by return alone. But with ILP RP, the value increases each year partly because **premiums are still being paid**, not just from investment returns. Entering the current value into Table 2 would misrepresent growth.
- **Tables 3 & 4** — not applicable (no rental income or regular payout)

### How to Calculate

Go to **Calculator Quick Link** tab → use the **ILP RP Calculator** section.

**Example (Miss Singer's ILP RP):**

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Premium start age | 45 |
| Premium payable for | 10 years (to age 55) |
| Annual premium | $18,000 |
| Projected portfolio return | 7% |
| Assumed surrender age | 60 (keep invested 5 years after last premium for growth) |

**Why not surrender at 55?** The last premium installment at 55 has had no time to grow. Better to let everything compound for a few more years. Also, surrendering at 55 means you need to immediately find somewhere else to park the money.

**Result:** The calculator projects the accumulated value at age 60 (e.g., $373,225).

### How to Input the Result

1. **Copy** the projected value from the calculator (it's a formula — use Paste Values)
2. Go to **Input Current Portfolio → Table 1**
3. **Paste as Values** (not regular paste — avoids carrying over formulas)
4. Enter the assumed surrender age (e.g., 60)
5. Add a description: *"ILP RP — assumed surrender at 60, projected at 7% return"*

> Table 1 is the only place this can go, even though ILP RP has investment risk. It's the best available approximation.

## Scenario 2: Cash or Policy Maturing Before Age 55

### Why It Doesn't Fit

Table 1 only accepts ages **55 to 89**. If a client has:
- Cash earmarked for retirement available now (e.g., at age 53)
- A policy maturing before 55 (e.g., at age 54)

Entering age 53 or 54 into Table 1 will produce an **"out of range" error**.

### How to Handle

Use the **Calculator Quick Link** tab → use the **Pre-55 Maturity Calculator** section.

**Step 1: Establish the facts**

| Question | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| How much is available / what is the maturity value? | The present value or lump sum amount |
| When is it available? | Current age or maturity age |
| Is it meant for retirement? | Confirms it should be projected forward |

**Step 2: Ask where the client plans to park the money**

> "Where do you intend to park this $200,000? Any idea?"

**Do not sell immediately.** The client may already have a plan. If they do, evaluate it first before proposing an alternative. If they don't:

| Client's Preference | Assumed Rate | Notes |
|--------------------|-------------|-------|
| Leave in bank | 1% | Very conservative |
| T-bills | ~3.3% | Short-term, must reinvest every 6 months for years (14 cycles to retirement) — impractical long-term |
| Single premium endowment | ~2.8% | Minimum 10-year lock-in — adjust surrender age accordingly |
| VHR (return to CPF OA) | 2.5% | An option, but don't suggest it if you want the case |
| Client asks for your suggestion | Your product | Now you can propose |

**Step 3: Project forward**

Enter into the calculator:
- Present value: $200,000
- Assumed rate: whatever the client agrees to (e.g., 2.8%)
- Project to: retirement age or policy maturity (e.g., age 63 for a 10-year endowment)

**Step 4: Transfer to Table 1**

1. Copy the projected value from the calculator
2. Paste as Values into **Table 1**
3. Enter the projected maturity age (e.g., 63)
4. Add a description: *"Maturity at 54, reinvested at 2.8% to 63"*

### The Potential Case

If the client buys what you recommend (e.g., single premium endowment), you close a case **next year** when the money becomes available. Make a note to follow up.

## 1. The 4W Framework (Borrowed from CFP Curriculum)

CPF is Singapore's **basic financial plan** — it covers all 4 pillars of financial planning:

### W1: Wealth Accumulation
- CPF forces savings of **37%** of income (20% employee + 17% employer)
- Allocated across OA (liquid), SA (retirement), MA (medical)
- **Key message to clients:** "No matter how much you earn, if you don't save from what you earn, you will never be financially sound."
- *Sales opportunity:* Open a savings/endowment case

### W2: Wealth Preservation
- CPF provides risk-free interest: **2.5% (OA)** and **4% (SA/MA/RA)**
- For those wanting higher returns → CPF Investment Scheme (CPFIS)
- **Key message:** "Even if you save, you won't be financially sound unless you invest/preserve what you've accumulated."
- *Sales opportunity:* Single premium cases, investment products

### W3: Wealth Protection
- CPF offers: **DPS** (Dependants' Protection Scheme), **MediShield Life**, **ElderShield/CareShield Life**, and **CPF LIFE** (annuity)
- **Gap identified:** CPF does NOT cover **Critical Illness (CI)** — this is the missing class of insurance
- CI is critical because MediShield/IP plans have claim limits; CI lump sum can cover excess bills
- *Sales opportunity:* CI policies to supplement CPF protection

### W4: Wealth Distribution
- Transfer money to loved ones via **RSTU (Retirement Sum Topping-Up Scheme)** — OA → loved one's SA/RA
- After death: **CPF Nomination** distributes bequest — cannot be contested
- *Sales opportunity:* Legacy planning conversations

## 7. MRSS — Matched Retirement Savings Scheme

### Current Scheme (2021–2025)
- Dollar-for-dollar matching into RA
- Cap: **$600/year**, max **$3,000 over 5 years**
- Only for Singapore Citizens **above 55, below 70**
- Must meet criteria: RA below BRS, monthly income < $4,000, property annual value ≤ $21,000 (i.e., HDB only)

### New/Amended Scheme (from 2025)
| Feature | Old | New |
|---|---|---|
| Annual matching cap | $600 | **$2,000** |
| Lifetime cap | $3,000 | **$20,000** |
| Age cap | 70 years old | **No cap** (55+) |

### Important Tax Implication
- **Old scheme (2024):** Top up $8,000 to parent's RA → first $600 qualifies for MRSS, full $8,000 gets tax relief
- **New scheme (2025):** Top up $8,000 → first $2,000 qualifies for MRSS but gets **NO tax relief** → net tax relief = only $6,000
- To get full $8,000 tax relief under new scheme → must top up **$10,000** total ($2,000 MRSS + $8,000 RSTU)

### Matching Timeline
- Regardless of when you contribute (Jan or Dec), the matching grant is credited **early the following year** (Jan/Feb)
- Still beneficial to contribute early → money starts earning **4% interest** immediately

### Application — How to Use MRSS for Client Engagement
1. Identify clients with parents/grandparents above 55 (especially above 70 for new scheme)
2. Help clients log into parent's CPF via SingPass → Retirement Dashboard → check MRSS eligibility
3. **Note:** 2024 eligibility check still uses old scheme (cap at 70) — recheck in Jan 2025 for the new scheme
4. While helping them check, you can view their CPF balances and retirement dashboard → natural opening to discuss retirement planning

## 8. What Happens at Age 55

### RA Account Creation
- **RA (Retirement Account)** is automatically created at age 55
- CPF automatically transfers **FRS amount** (default) from OA + SA → RA
- They do **not ask** your preference — FRS is taken by default
- Balance after FRS is set aside remains in OA (from 2025 onwards, no more SA — everything is OA)

### Retirement Sum Tiers (2024 figures)

| Tier | Multiple | Amount | Est. Monthly Payout (at 65) |
|---|---|---|---|
| BRS | 1× | $102,900 | ~$900 |
| FRS | 2× | $205,800 | ~$1,670 |
| ERS (current, 3×) | 3× | $308,700 | ~$2,450 |
| ERS (2025, 4×) | 4× | $426,000 | ~$3,300 |

### Going Up (ERS) or Down (BRS)
- **Only possible AFTER RA is created** (i.e., after turning 55)
- **Top up to ERS:** Use cash or transfer from OA → irreversible once done (like OA→SA transfer)
- **Downsize to BRS:** Must pledge property → reduces RA balance → buys smaller CPF LIFE plan

### Property Pledging Formula
- **Maximum pledgeable amount = X − BRS** (where X = amount set aside in RA, max FRS)
- If RA < BRS → cannot pledge
- If RA ≥ FRS → max pledge = FRS − BRS = $102,900
- Quick answer on death: If you die without selling the property → pledge dies with you, nothing happens to property. If you sell during lifetime → must honor the pledge.

## 9. CPF LIFE — Key Concepts

### From 55 to 65
- RA money grows at **4%** interest
- If you die during this period → interest belongs to you (your estate)

### At Age 65 (or defer up to 70)
- Buy CPF LIFE plan using RA money → RA becomes **zero** (general assumption)
- Monthly payout begins — for life
- Can defer to 70 (RA continues earning 4%) — by 70, you **must** buy

### Death Benefit
- CPF LIFE guarantees return of **premium minus total collected**
- Example: Paid $300K premium, collected $150K → death benefit = $150K
- Under CPF LIFE Standard: you typically collect back your full premium by **age 80–81**
- After that: death benefit = **$0** (you've already gotten your premium back)

### Interest After Buying CPF LIFE
- Premium continues to earn 4% — but interest goes to the **common pool**, not to you
- Common pool funds payouts for those who live long
- This is how all annuities work (commercial ones too)

> **Sales angle:** Show clients this structure → suggest diversifying: put 2–3× in CPF LIFE for guaranteed baseline, complement with **dividend income plan** for flexibility and legacy

## 10. The "$1,000 Rule" — Present Value of CPF LIFE Payouts

### Key Insight
FRS increases at **3.5% annually** (government benchmark for lifestyle inflation). When you discount future CPF LIFE payouts back to today's dollars:

- **Women: ~$1,100/month** in today's money
- **Men: ~$1,200/month** in today's money

(Women get lower monthly payouts because they live longer → more installments expected)

### Proof
- Sister A (born 1966): turns 55 in 2021, sets aside $186K → gets $1,370/month from 2031
- Sister B (born 1969): turns 55 in 2024, sets aside $205.8K → gets $1,540/month from 2034
- Discounting both to 2024 at 3.5% → both ≈ **$1,076–$1,091/month**

> **Quick shortcut:** No matter when your client turns 55, CPF LIFE in today's dollars is approximately **$1,000–$1,200/month**. If they want $3,000/month in retirement, the gap of ~$1,800–$2,000 must be **DIY** (private plans, investments, savings).

| Age in 2024 | FRS at Age 55 (at 3.5%) | FRS at Age 55 (at 5.45%) |
|---|---|---|
| 41 | ~$333,000 | ~$440,000 |
| 36 | ~$395,000 | ~$575,000 |
| 31 | ~$470,000 | — |

- From 1987 ($30K) to 2022 ($192K) → FRS grew at **5.45%** compound
- Even at 3.5%, future FRS figures are daunting — with OA often wiped out by property, SA alone may struggle to meet it
- **This is why CPF must constantly evolve** — and why clients must keep increasing their protection, savings, and investment

## 12. VHR — Voluntary Housing Refund

- Return money taken from OA for property (principal + accrued interest) **back to OA** — without selling the property
- Net effect: as if you never used CPF for property
- Commonly practiced by **affluent clients** with excess cash — reasons:
  - Earn risk-free 2.5% minimum
  - Protection from creditors (CPF is bankruptcy-protected)
- Not for investment purposes — part of overall wealth preservation strategy
- Clients with VHR done → often have **$1M+ in CPF at 55**

## 13. Practical Selling Reminders

- **Stop selling $100–$200/month policies** — no longer relevant in today's inflation environment
- Endowment policies maturing after 20 years often feel inadequate (e.g., $60K for education — "too little, too late")
- **Educate clients on time value of money** — this is what differentiates us from the public
- Use CPF's own evolution as evidence: DPS went from $36K → $48K → $70K; FRS from $30K → $205K
- Three things clients must always do: **buy more insurance, save harder, put money to work**

> **Quotation to adopt:** *"Money earned and accumulated is not money until you spend it."*
> Retirement planning has two phases: **accumulation** and **drawing down**. Encourage clients to enjoy an upgraded (not downgraded) lifestyle in retirement.

## 14. Access Fund vs. CPF LIFE Income (Two Buckets Model)

At retirement, clients have two pools:

| Bucket | Source | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Lump Sum (Access Fund)** | OA balance after FRS is set aside | Liquidity, emergency, unexpected expenses — withdrawable anytime, multiple times |
| **Monthly Income** | CPF LIFE (or Retirement Sum Scheme for pre-1958 births) | Steady lifelong income for essential expenses |

### Three Options for OA Balance (at 55+)
1. Transfer OA → RA to **permanently increase** CPF LIFE payout (irreversible)
2. Keep in OA for **liquidity** and draw down as needed
3. Leave in OA as **emergency reserve**

> **Key answer to memorize:** When anyone asks "How do I get back my CPF money?" → Answer: **Lump sum (at/after 55)** + **Monthly income (from 65, via CPF LIFE)**

### Important Rules
- Money topped up to RA via RSTU is **irreversible** — cannot be withdrawn as lump sum, only drawn down as monthly income
- Cannot pledge property to withdraw RSTU top-up amounts
- RA money from top-ups can only be **drawn down** (via CPF LIFE payouts)

- **Only possible AFTER RA is created** (i.e., after turning 55)
- **Top up to ERS:** Use cash or transfer from OA → irreversible once done (like OA→SA transfer)
- **Downsize to BRS:** Must pledge property → reduces RA balance → buys smaller CPF LIFE plan

### Property Pledging Formula
- **Maximum pledgeable amount = X − BRS** (where X = amount set aside in RA, max FRS)
- If RA < BRS → cannot pledge
- If RA ≥ FRS → max pledge = FRS − BRS = $102,900
- Quick answer on death: If you die without selling the property → pledge dies with you, nothing happens to property. If you sell during lifetime → must honor the pledge.

## 9. CPF LIFE — Key Concepts

### From 55 to 65
- RA money grows at **4%** interest
- If you die during this period → interest belongs to you (your estate)

### At Age 65 (or defer up to 70)
- Buy CPF LIFE plan using RA money → RA becomes **zero** (general assumption)
- Monthly payout begins — for life
- Can defer to 70 (RA continues earning 4%) — by 70, you **must** buy

### Death Benefit
- CPF LIFE guarantees return of **premium minus total collected**
- Example: Paid $300K premium, collected $150K → death benefit = $150K
- Under CPF LIFE Standard: you typically collect back your full premium by **age 80–81**
- After that: death benefit = **$0** (you've already gotten your premium back)

### Interest After Buying CPF LIFE
- Premium continues to earn 4% — but interest goes to the **common pool**, not to you
- Common pool funds payouts for those who live long
- This is how all annuities work (commercial ones too)

> **Sales angle:** Show clients this structure → suggest diversifying: put 2–3× in CPF LIFE for guaranteed baseline, complement with **dividend income plan** for flexibility and legacy

## 10. The "$1,000 Rule" — Present Value of CPF LIFE Payouts

### Key Insight
FRS increases at **3.5% annually** (government benchmark for lifestyle inflation). When you discount future CPF LIFE payouts back to today's dollars:

- **Women: ~$1,100/month** in today's money
- **Men: ~$1,200/month** in today's money

(Women get lower monthly payouts because they live longer → more installments expected)

### Proof
- Sister A (born 1966): turns 55 in 2021, sets aside $186K → gets $1,370/month from 2031
- Sister B (born 1969): turns 55 in 2024, sets aside $205.8K → gets $1,540/month from 2034

> **Quick shortcut:** No matter when your client turns 55, CPF LIFE in today's dollars is approximately **$1,000–$1,200/month**. If they want $3,000/month in retirement, the gap of ~$1,800–$2,000 must be **DIY** (private plans, investments, savings).

## 14. Access Fund vs. CPF LIFE Income (Two Buckets Model)

At retirement, clients have two pools:

| Bucket | Source | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Lump Sum (Access Fund)** | OA balance after FRS is set aside | Liquidity, emergency, unexpected expenses — withdrawable anytime, multiple times |
| **Monthly Income** | CPF LIFE (or Retirement Sum Scheme for pre-1958 births) | Steady lifelong income for essential expenses |

### Three Options for OA Balance (at 55+)
1. Transfer OA → RA to **permanently increase** CPF LIFE payout (irreversible)
2. Keep in OA for **liquidity** and draw down as needed
3. Leave in OA as **emergency reserve**

> **Key answer to memorize:** When anyone asks "How do I get back my CPF money?" → Answer: **Lump sum (at/after 55)** + **Monthly income (from 65, via CPF LIFE)**

### Important Rules
- Money topped up to RA via RSTU is **irreversible** — cannot be withdrawn as lump sum, only drawn down as monthly income
- Cannot pledge property to withdraw RSTU top-up amounts
- RA money from top-ups can only be **drawn down** (via CPF LIFE payouts)

## 1. Property Pledging — Overview

### What Is Property Pledging?
- Pledging = withdrawing money from your RA down to BRS
- RA money is otherwise untouchable; pledging is the mechanism to access it
- You **cannot** withdraw below BRS

### Property Eligibility (Two Criteria Only)
1. **Singapore residential property** (public or private — commercial units excluded)
2. **Lease must extend beyond your age 95** (not 95 years of lease remaining — the lease just needs to last past when you turn 95, even by one day)

Other factors **don't matter**: fully paid or not, joint tenancy or tenancy in common, sole or co-owner — as long as you meet the two criteria above.

### Maximum Pledgeable Amount
- **BRS of the year you turn 55** (not the current year)
- Formula: **X − BRS**, where X = amount set aside in RA (max = FRS)
- If X < BRS → cannot pledge at all
- Interest earned in RA cannot be withdrawn — only the original set-aside amount is relevant

---

## 2. Three Types of Property Pledging

### Type 1: Online Pledge
**Condition:** P + I ≥ amount you wish to pledge

- **P** = Principal (CPF used to pay for property)
- **I** = Accrued interest on that principal
- If P + I > pledgeable amount → can do everything online (no documentation needed)
- Processing time: ~**5 working days** (plan at least 7 working days for safety, especially near year-end)

**How to check P + I:**
- SingPass → Finance → CPF → CPF Housing Withdrawal
- Shows: Principal Withdrawn (P) + Accrued Interest (I) = Total that must be returned upon selling

**When selling property:**
- P + I is returned to OA
- Pledged amount (without interest) is transferred from OA back to RA
- Balance stays in OA

> **Important note:** CPF has **hidden** the online pledge link due to anti-scam measures. You need a direct link (provided separately) — log in with SingPass to check if pledging is available.

### Type 2: Manual Pledge
**Condition:** P + I < amount you wish to pledge

- The portion up to P + I → can be done online
- The balance above P + I → must visit CPF Board for manual pledge
- **Additional underwriting required:** property valuation, outstanding loan, co-owner's agreement
- Processing time: **couple of weeks** (not 5–7 working days)

**Example:** P + I = $60,000, want to pledge $102,900 (BRS)
- $60,000 → online pledge
- $42,900 → manual pledge
- When selling: must return total $102,900 to RA (without interest)

### Type 3: Auto Pledge
**Condition:** You set aside less than FRS at age 55, but you own a property with P + I

- CPF **automatically** pledges the deficit (up to BRS maximum) — no payout to you
- Purpose: to give you "Met FRS" status (with cash and property)
- You are **not consulted** — it happens automatically

**Why Auto Pledge Matters:**
- If you've "Met FRS" → all future CPF contributions go to **OA** (liquid, withdrawable)
- If you've "Not Met FRS" → contributions must go to **RA** first (locked up)
- Auto pledge also means: if you liquidate investments and the proceeds go to OA, you can withdraw them (because you've "met" FRS)

**After auto pledge, you can still do additional online pledging:**
- Additional pledge = X − BRS (where X = what you actually set aside)

**If you have no P + I** (inherited property, spouse paid, cash purchase):
- Cannot auto pledge
- Can still do full manual pledge ($102,900) subject to underwriting

## 3. The $5,000 Rule

When OA + SA is **insufficient** to meet FRS at age 55:
- CPF will always **leave $5,000 behind** in OA
- Everything else goes to RA
- This is a universal rule — CPF never takes the last $5,000

**Example:** OA = $100K, SA = $100K → CPF takes $195K (leaves $5K), puts $195K in RA

## 8. Voluntary Housing Refund (VHR)

### What Is VHR?
- Return money you previously took from OA to pay for property (P + I) → **without selling your house**
- Money goes back to **OA** → earns **2.5% minimum**
- Like a fixed deposit without the fixed period — withdraw anytime

### Requirements
- Must have P + I > 0 (if you never used CPF for property, or property was inherited/paid by spouse → P + I = 0 → cannot do VHR)
- Can return any amount up to your total P + I

### Key Features
- No holding period (but interest only accrues after a full month)
- If you've met FRS → OA money is fully liquid
- Guaranteed minimum 2.5% return — better than many short-term fixed deposits

### Critical Warning — VHR and Existing Pledges
- **VHR is treated the same as selling your house** for pledge purposes
- If there's an existing pledge (online, manual, or auto), VHR money goes to OA → immediately transferred to RA to honor the pledge
- **Cannot withdraw** the VHR amount that goes to honor the pledge
- Always check for existing pledges before advising VHR

### Application — Client Engagement Strategy
When meeting clients approaching/past 55:

1. **Share VC opportunity** — contribution rate dropped, can top up voluntarily
2. **Introduce VHR** — "Would you like a fixed deposit without the fixed period, guaranteed 2.5%?"
3. Check their P + I on SingPass → show how much they can park in OA
4. While checking, you gain visibility into their **full financial picture** (cash, CPF balances, portfolio)
5. Then ask: *"Is all of this enough for retirement?"* → open retirement planning conversation

### Why P + I Is Actually a Good Thing
- **With P + I:** Can do online pledging quickly, can perform VHR to park cash at 2.5%
- **Without P + I:** Cannot do online pledge, cannot do VHR, limited options to get money into CPF in lump sum

## 9. Accrued Interest Explained (Common Client Complaint)

**Client complaint:** *"Why must I return P + I when I sell my house? It's my own money!"*

**Correct explanation:**
- CPF's primary purpose is **retirement**, not property purchase
- Using OA for property = a **policy loan** — you borrowed from your retirement fund
- The "interest" you return is just the 2.5% your money **would have earned** had it stayed in OA
- Once returned, you earn 2.5% going forward — it's all your money
- The interest portion is **100% yours** — you pay nothing extra
- Government needs P + I returned to make up any auto-pledge deficit and ensure retirement adequacy

### The Core Question: Should You Put Everything Into Annuity?

**$426,000 is almost half a million — should it all go into one basket?**

- Payable for life

- Earliest payout is 65

- CPF LIFE is only truly beneficial if you **live long** (past 88)

- **Break-even at 4% return: age 88**

> Just like life insurance — you buy it hoping you won't need it. Annuity — you buy it hoping you WILL need it (by living long). The risk is dying at an "average" age.

|---|---|---|---|
| 66 | $18,600 | $266,400 | 3.40% |
| 70 | $93,000 | $192,000 | 2.83% |
| 75 | $186,000 | $99,000 | ~2.5% |
| **80** | **$279,000** | **$6,000** | **~2.2% (worst point)** |
| 81 | $285,000+ | $0 | ~2.2% (rock bottom) |
| 85 | $372,000 | $0 | ~3.2% |
| **88** | **~$430,000** | **$0** | **~4.0% (break-even)** |
| 90+ | Higher | $0 | >4% (benefiting from pool) |

**Key thresholds:**
- **Age 80–81:** Collected back all principal → death benefit = $0 → worst IRR
- **Age 88:** Break-even at 4% → from here on, you're taking from the common pool
- **Beyond 88:** The longer you live, the better the return → this is where annuity shines

> *"If you make it to 81, carry on living. If you make it to 88, you can die peacefully. Beyond 88, carry on living — you're taking other people's interest."*

---

## 6. CPF LIFE Plans — Standard vs Basic vs Escalating

### Standard (Default)
- Highest payout, level throughout
- 100% of RA used as premium
- Death benefit = Premium − Total Collected → reaches $0 around age 80–81
- Can still do property pledging after payout starts (but pledge amount calculated by CPF Board, not BRS)

### Basic (Legacy Plan)
- Lower payout than Standard
- **Only 10% of RA used as premium** (buys a deferred annuity starting at age 90)
- **90% stays in RA** → drawn down like Retirement Sum Scheme from 65 to 89
- At 90+: deferred annuity kicks in
- Death benefit: RA balance + 10% premium refund (no interest) → much higher bequest than Standard
- At age 80: still ~$145,000 in death benefit vs $0 for Standard
- **Best IRR** among the three plans (hovering slightly above 4%)

### Escalating
- Starts with lowest payout
- Compounds at **2% per year** for life
- Only better than Standard if you live **past 95**
- Low take-up rate; don't actively push but don't dismiss for very long-lived clients

### For Women
- Same premium → lower monthly payout (longer life expectancy = more installments expected)
- Difference between Standard and Basic: ~$80/month
- For men: ~$130/month difference

---

## 7. Retirement Sum Scheme (RSS) — For Older Generation

### How RSS Works (for those born before 1958, or RA < $60K)
- RA money drawn down over **20 years** (65 to 85)
- After RA depletes: additional interest fund (from first $60K combined balance) provides a few more years
- BRS: ~5 extra years; FRS: ~3 extra years; ERS: ~1 extra year
- Then **no more income** → this is the fundamental weakness

### RSS vs CPF LIFE Comparison
| Feature | RSS | CPF LIFE |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payout | **Higher** | Lower |
| Death benefit | **Higher** (RA balance + interest = yours) | Lower (premium − collected) |
| Duration | Limited (20 years + buffer) | **Lifetime** |

### Minimum $60,000 Rule for CPF LIFE
- At age 65: RA must have **minimum $60,000** to auto-qualify for CPF LIFE
- Below $60,000 → reverted to RSS (draw to deplete)
- Can still manually apply for CPF LIFE with less than $60K, but payout will be minimal ($50–$100/month)
- RSS minimum payout: **$350/month**, drawn to depletion

---

## 8. Should You Defer CPF LIFE? (65 vs 70)

### The Common Advice (from CPF Board): Defer to get a bigger plan
- $305K at 65 → $1,650/month
- $375K at 70 → $2,200/month (extra $550/month)

### The Reality: Don't Defer (Unless Health Is Poor)

**Comparison over 5 years (65–70):**
- **Don't defer:** Collect $1,650 × 12 × 5 = **$99,000** in hand by age 70
- **Defer:** Collect $0, but RA grows to $375K

**Break-even analysis:**
- $99,000 ÷ $550 (monthly difference) = **15 years** → break-even at age **85**
- At 85: both have collected ~$396,000 total
- But the non-deferred person collected **more in present dollars** (time value of money)
- True break-even (with time value): **well beyond 85**

### When TO Defer
**Only if mortality is in question** (recent heart attack, stroke, serious illness):
- If you're likely to die around 78 → deferring means your **bequest is $375K** (vs $305K if you started at 65)
- The extra $70K in bequest can matter for family
- Logic: if you're going to die relatively soon, the extra RA growth becomes death benefit for your family

### Practical Advice to Clients
Two questions:
1. *"Do you need the money?"* → If no, still start at 65 (get $99K in hand)
2. *"How is your health?"* → If good, start at 65. If poor/uncertain, consider deferring for higher bequest

## 9. Diversification Recommendation — The Complete Picture

### The Retirement Income Timeline

| Period | Primary Funding | Products |
|---|---|---|
| **55–65** | Excess fund (OA), personal savings, investments | Access to lump sum; plan premium payments |
| **65–85** | Traditional plans + CPF LIFE (supporting role) | Retirement Saver, PRO, Great Retirement Income, dividend income plan |
| **85+** | CPF LIFE (primary role) + dividend fund (if units remain) | Annuity becomes critical; other assets likely depleted |

### Recommended Portfolio Mix (Subjective, Client-Dependent)
- **CPF LIFE:** 2–3× (FRS to ERS) — guaranteed baseline, covers longevity risk
- **Traditional plans:** Pay for 5–10 years, receive income from 65–80/85 — covers the "likely" retirement period at ~3%+ return
- **Dividend income plan:** Risk element but offers liquidity, death benefit beyond 80, potentially >4% returns, income for life (as long as units exist)
- **Cash/OA reserve:** Liquidity for unexpected expenses

> *"Don't put all your money into one basket. CPF LIFE is the best annuity — but it's still an annuity. Diversify across CPF LIFE (for longevity), traditional plans (for the likely period), and dividend funds (for flexibility and legacy)."*

## Overview & Purpose

This session focuses on a **CPF Projection Spreadsheet** (Version 25 V2/V2a) that combines CPF funds with a client's existing assets into one retirement portfolio, giving clients a complete picture of their retirement readiness.

**Key differentiator:** Most financial consultants either only discuss CPF balances without projections, or only work with current assets without factoring in projected CPF contributions. This spreadsheet does both — and that's what makes clients say, *"You're the first person who put everything together for me."*

---

## Why Cash Flow (Not Net Present Value)?

The spreadsheet uses a **cash flow approach** rather than net present value (NPV). Here's why:

- NPV may look adequate on paper, but it doesn't capture **timing mismatches** — e.g., a client needs $10,000/month at age 65–75 but only receives $4,000 from a plan + $3,000 from assets = $7,000. There's a $3,000/month shortfall causing faster reserve depletion.
- Cash flow shows **when money comes in, when it goes out, how much in, how much out** — so you can see whether funding is adequate year by year.
- Conclusion: **Cash flow is the best method for retirement planning.**

---

## The Five Stages of Retirement Planning

| Stage | Purpose | Simple Description |
|-------|---------|-------------------|
| **Stage 1** | How much & in what form does CPF return your money? | One-page report at age 55 |
| **Stage 1A** | What basic lifestyle can CPF alone provide (retirement age to 85)? | Try-and-error to find sustainable monthly draw |
| **Stage 2** | Is it enough? | Compare CPF lifestyle vs. client's desired lifestyle |
| **Stage 3** | Identify other financial assets | Insurance, investments, rental income, business income |
| **Stage 4** | Merge everything & reassess | Combine CPF + other assets, check if gap remains |
| **Stage 5** | Fine-tune & bridge the gap | This is where your proposal comes in |

## Spreadsheet Version Selection

**Two versions exist: 25 V2 and 25 V2a**

- **25 V2** — Uses 2025 CPF contribution rates. Correct for everyone, but less precise for younger clients.
- **25 V2a** — Uses projected 2030 contribution rates (after all upcoming changes are fully implemented). More accurate for clients **born after 1975** (they won't turn 55 before 2030, so current transitional changes won't affect them).

**Rule of thumb:**
- Born **after 1975** → Use 25 V2a (more accurate)
- Born **before 1975** → Use 25 V2 (they're affected by transitional rate changes)
- **Not sure?** Just use 25 V2 — it's correct for everyone, just slightly less precise for younger clients.
- Never use V2a for those born before 1975 (it overstates their contributions).

## Stage 1: The One-Page Report

**What to enter:** Name, DOB, gender, income, bonus, salary increment %, statement date, three CPF balances (OA, SA, MA), housing loan deduction (if any).

**What you get:** A one-page report showing:
- **Projected balances at age 55** (OA, SA, MA)
- **Excess money at 55** = (OA + SA) − Projected Full Retirement Sum (FRS)
- **Projected CPF LIFE monthly payout from 65**

**How excess money is calculated:**
> OA balance at 55 + SA balance at 55 − Projected FRS = Excess (withdrawable lump sum)

**How CPF LIFE payout is calculated:** The spreadsheet's internal calculator uses the projected FRS amount to estimate the CPF LIFE Standard Plan payout. Gender matters — females receive lower payouts than males.

**Key projection assumptions built into the spreadsheet:**
- FRS grows at **3.5% per year** (based on Lawrence Wong's earlier announcements)
- BHS grows at **4.7% per year** (average of last 6 years' historical growth)
- CPF OA interest: **2.5%**
- Inflation: **3%**

## Stage 1A: What Basic Lifestyle Can CPF Alone Provide?

This answers: *"If I only rely on CPF, what monthly lifestyle (in today's dollars) can I sustain from my desired retirement age to 85?"*

**Method: Trial and error**

1. On the Input Tab, key in the retirement age (always enter in cell **D50** regardless of retirement age)
2. Key in a trial monthly income figure in today's dollars
3. Check the **Cash Flow Tab → Column L** — the last positive number must end at **age 85**
4. If it goes negative before 85, reduce the income figure. If there's too much left over at 85, increase it.

**Understanding the Cash Flow Tab:**

| Column | What It Represents |
|--------|--------------------|
| Starting balance (green) | Reserve at retirement age (excess at 55 + contributions + interest to retirement age) |
| Expenses (blue, negative) | Desired lifestyle in future dollars (today's dollars inflated at 3%) |
| CPF LIFE income (orange, positive) | Fixed monthly payout from age 65 |
| Cash flow budget required | Deficit = expenses − CPF LIFE income (how much to withdraw from reserve) |
| Ending balance (Column L) | Reserve remaining after withdrawal + interest earned |

**Key terms:**
- **RRI (Retirement Readiness Index) Age** = The last age where Column L is still positive = the "last good year"
- **Substandard Year** = First year where reserve can't fully fund the lifestyle gap (partial funding only)
- **CPF LIFE Standard Year / "Mother's Day"** = Years where only CPF LIFE income remains, no reserve left

**The School Analogy:**
- Mother gives $5/day (= CPF LIFE, fixed)
- You want to spend $10/day (= desired lifestyle)
- You have $1,003 in the bank (= CPF excess/reserve)
- You draw $5/day from the bank to top up
- Last good day = bank still has enough to draw the full $5
- Substandard day = bank has less than $5, can only partially top up
- Mother's Day = bank is empty, you only have mother's $5

## Stage 2: Is It Enough?

Ask the client: *"What monthly lifestyle do you desire in retirement?"*

- If client says $5,000 but CPF alone provides $3,050 → **CPF covers 61%** (3,050 ÷ 5,000)
- The RRI age drops (e.g., from 85 to 75) because higher withdrawals deplete reserves faster
- **Do not start selling yet** — proceed to Stage 3 and 4 first

## Stages 3 & 4: Identify, Merge & Reassess

**Stage 3 — Identify the client's other financial assets:**
Five common asset classes: life insurance/savings, investments, rental income, business income, other

**Stage 4 — Merge with CPF and reassess:**
- Add the additional income streams into the cash flow
- Recalculate the coverage percentage
- Example: CPF provides 61%, other assets provide another 20% → total 81% covered

**Critical point:** If you skip fact-finding (Stages 3–4), you risk:
- Trying to sell when the client is already self-sufficient (and they won't buy)
- Missing existing coverage and losing credibility
- Not knowing the true gap to bridge

---

## Stage 5: Fine-Tune & Bridge the Gap

This is where your proposal fits. After showing the client their total picture (CPF + existing assets), the remaining gap becomes the case.

**Graph interpretation for selling:**
- The **red line** (deficit) shows how much the client must withdraw from reserves each year
- Goal: Bring the red line **one-third closer to the zero line** (not necessarily to zero — that's financial independence and is extremely difficult)
- Why one-third? Clients may not spend their full budget; actual spending is often around two-thirds of budgeted
- **Selling income plans** (annuities, retirement income products) adds orange bars, reduces the red deficit, and extends the reserve's lifespan to 85+

---

## Why Plan to Age 85?

- Statistically, most Singaporeans aspire to live around 85
- Planning beyond 90 discourages clients (requires much more accumulation)
- Beyond 85: CPF LIFE annuity continues paying until death, consumption naturally decreases with age, medical needs are covered by Shield plans + MediSave
- Minimum recommendation: **3x FRS** (gives ~$1,000/month in today's dollars at age 85). 2x FRS is too thin. 4x FRS is even better.

---

## Today's Dollar vs. Future Dollar

All retirement planning uses **today's dollars** so clients can feel the purchasing power.

- Example: $3,000/month today's dollars at 3% inflation for 31 years = **$7,625/month** in future dollars
- CPF LIFE paying $2,910/month may sound reasonable now, but in future purchasing power it becomes insignificant
- Illustration: "My pocket money in the 1970s was $0.50/day. Today's students get $5–$10/day. Same concept."

**Two ways to beat inflation:**
1. **Spend the money now** (buy property, goods before prices rise)
2. **Spend on investments** — historically, appropriately managed investments beat inflation over time (use this to position single premium ILPs/UTs)

**Retirement planning is selfish planning** — it's for yourself. Protection planning is for loved ones. Retirement planning is for you because you'll still be around and must be self-sufficient.

## Stage 2: Leading the Conversation — "Is It Enough?"

### How to Transition from Stage 1 to Stage 2

After presenting the one-page report (Stage 1), lead into Stage 2 with this flow:

4. **Switch from Report tab to Input tab** while asking questions.

| Question | Notes |

| "For your kind of lifestyle, what do you think is inflation?" | Usually 3%, already pre-filled |

## Simulating Property Purchase in the Spreadsheet

### Step-by-Step: Future Property Purchase

**Example: Client buying a $400K BTO, collecting keys April 2027**

1. **Find projected OA balance at purchase date:**
   - Go to Input tab → temporarily change the statement date to the purchase month (e.g., April 2027) to see the projected OA balance
   - **Or** go to CPF Projection tab → find the row for the month *before* the purchase month (conservative approach, since CPF contributions may not arrive on the 1st)
   - Example: March 2027 shows $244,515 in OA → use $244,514 (reduce by $1 to avoid rounding errors)
   - **Remember to change the statement date back** after checking

2. **Key in the housing single payment:**
   - Input tab → Housing Single Payment Using CPF OA → enter $244,514
   - If you get an error, reduce by $1 (the projected amount is rounded; the actual internal value may be slightly less)

3. **Calculate the loan:**
   - HDB borrowing limit: **75% of purchase price** (25% down payment)
   - Down payment comes from CPF OA (wiped out at key collection)
   - Client can choose to keep up to $20,000 in OA or wipe out entirely
   - Loan amount = Purchase price − Down payment (OA used)

4. **Calculate monthly installment:**
   - Use the financial calculator link in the spreadsheet (Calculator Quick Link tab, Row 8)
   - Input: loan amount, interest rate (HDB = 2.6%), loan tenure
   - Get the monthly installment amount
   - Ask client: "Will you finance 100% from CPF, or share with spouse?" Split accordingly.

5. **Key in monthly housing deduction:**
   - Input tab → Row 29/30 → enter monthly amount (e.g., $600)
   - Enter start age accurately: Go to Projection tab → find the month installments begin (usually 1 month after down payment) → note the age
   - End age = Start age + loan tenure (e.g., 38.69 + 15 = 53.69)
   - Format in the cell as "38.69+15" so you remember how you derived it

6. **Verify in Projection tab:**
   - The $600 deduction should appear monthly from the start month
   - Count the entries — should equal tenure × 12 (e.g., 180 for 15 years)
   - Confirm the client's monthly OA contribution exceeds the installment (otherwise reserves will drop)

### Current Loan vs. Future Loan

- **Future loan:** Enter the start month/year in the date field → deductions begin from that date
- **Current loan:** Leave the start date **blank** → deductions begin immediately from the statement date
- For existing loans, use estimates: ask when they got the house (calculate their age then) and how many years they borrowed → age at purchase + tenure = end age. No need for decimal precision on existing loans.

- **Default: Always use FRS** for projections
- **ERS (3x or 4x):** Only propose for clients around age 54 with very high reserves — locks in more money but generates higher CPF LIFE income
- **BRS:** Propose for clients with very low reserves or those who need to retire early due to disability — allows money to be taken out via property pledging
- Don't explain BRS/ERS to clients in their 30s–early 50s — options may change by the time they reach 55

### Salary Scenarios

| Situation | How to Handle |
|-----------|---------------|
| Part-time work | Key in the part-time salary. If still above CPF ceiling ($6,800 in 2024), it doesn't affect CPF contributions |
| Salary has a maximum ceiling | Put 0% increment |
| Contemplating becoming homemaker | Don't factor in until they're certain (e.g., 1–2 years before). Too many assumptions reduce accuracy |
| Potential retrenchment | Ask: "Do you intend to stay unemployed for good, or is it temporary?" If temporary, don't factor in. If permanent, key zero salary |
| High income earners (pilots, executives) | CPF contribution is capped at the monthly ceiling — the spreadsheet auto-caps. High earners are actually better candidates because CPF covers a smaller portion of their lifestyle |

### The Two-Thirds Rule for High Income Earners

The industry best practice for retirement lifestyle estimation: **two-thirds of last drawn pay.**

Example: Client earning $28,000/month → retirement lifestyle target should be ~$14,000/month (50–67%), not $8,000/month. One real client retired at $8,000/month from a $28,000 salary and regretted it — she couldn't maintain the spending habits she'd built over decades.

Use this as a guide, not a hard rule. For very high earners, 50% may suffice. Adjust based on the individual.

## Medical Premium Expenses in the Spreadsheet

**Recommendation: Do NOT project medical premiums separately.** Instead, build them into the lifestyle income figure.

**Reasons:**
- Medical inflation ≠ lifestyle inflation (medical runs at 5–6%, lifestyle at 3%)
- Premium structures change unpredictably (riders get repriced, plans get restructured)
- Projecting premium inflation as a financial consultant carries liability risk — "we are not insurance companies, we are just a rep"
- MediSave interest (4%) on BHS is designed by the government to be roughly self-funding for basic medical premiums (MSL + IP + CareShield Life + riders)

**Better approach:** When asking "How much would you like per month?", tell the client to include medical spending in that number. Encourage them to go **higher rather than lower** to account for this ambiguity.

## Stage 3: Identifying Retirement Assets

### What to Ask the Client

> "Has anyone ever compiled a summary of your financial assets? Have you done one yourself?"

- If **yes**: "Great — send it to me and I'll work with it."
- If **no**: "No problem. It's high time you should compile what you have so you know where you stand. Go gather your information — savings plans, investments, rental income — and share with me."

For insurance: Ask for **revised benefit illustrations** (post illustrations) — not the original proposal. Old illustrations are outdated and will produce inaccurate projections.

### How to Categorize Assets

**Include for retirement funding:**
- Savings/endowment plans maturing near or after retirement
- Pro Wealth / S-SWB / Great Wealth Multiplier (assume lump sum surrender)
- Retirement income plans (regular payout)
- Investment-linked policies / Unit trusts (with assumed surrender)
- Rental income from investment properties
- Cash set aside and committed for retirement

**Exclude from retirement funding:**
- Life insurance with critical illness riders (needed during retirement when CI probability is highest — provides liquidity for non-claimable expenses: helpers, home renovation, alternative treatments, equipment)
- Assets the client may spend or liquidate before retirement ("I might sell when the market is up" / "I don't know what I'll do with it")
- Bank balances the client hasn't committed to lock in for retirement
- High income that isn't being saved

**The principle:** Anything not accountable — not committed and locked in for retirement — is not captured. It doesn't matter how much someone earns; it matters how much they save and commit.

## Stage 4: Merging & Reassessment — Input Current Portfolio

The Input Current Portfolio tab has **four tables**:

### Table 1: Saving/Insurance Plans (Single Output, No Investment Risk)

For traditional plans that produce a **one-time lump sum** — endowments maturing, insurance plans surrendered, savings bonds maturing.

**What to enter:**
- Column A: Age at maturity or assumed surrender
- Column B: Projected surrender value or maturity value

**For plans with no fixed maturity** (e.g., Pro Wealth / Great Wealth Multiplier): Assume lump sum surrender. Simpler and less ambiguous than projecting drawdowns.

**When to surrender:** Don't pick an arbitrary age. Go to Cash Flow → Column L → find the first year with zero or negative balance → that's when the client needs the money → surrender at that age.

- If the illustration only shows values in 5-year steps (60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 85) and you need age 80 but it's not available, take the **earlier** value (77 instead of 82) — better to be conservative than too late.

**For life insurance you want to keep as emergency liquidity:** Input at an advanced age (e.g., 81, 86) to show the client that "if you're very healthy but totally broke at 80+, you can still surrender these as a last resort."

### Table 2: Investment Plans (Single Output, With Investment Risk)

For ILPs, unit trusts, equity, commodities — anything with **no guarantee**.

**What to enter:**
- Column A: Assumed surrender age
- Column B: Current value (as of statement date)
- Projected return % (e.g., 5%)

The system computes internally: n = assumed surrender age − statement age → projects current value forward at the stated return rate.

**Critical concept — Keep N Small:**

Do NOT project investment returns over long periods. Even a modest-sounding 5% compounded over 18 years produces unrealistic-looking numbers.

**Why:** The return is assumed, not guaranteed. A client confident of 23% returns (from an investment course) may end up losing money. If you project high returns over a long N, the spreadsheet shows no gap — but reality may differ dramatically.

**Strategy:**
- Set assumed surrender age **close to current age** (within ~7 years)
- During annual reviews, if the client is on track, stretch the assumed surrender age forward by 5 years
- This contains the compounding effect and keeps projections honest
- You have time to rectify if returns don't materialize

**After assumed surrender:** The spreadsheet assumes 2.5% return on the proceeds (reinvested conservatively). Consider voluntary CPF contributions or top-ups if the client doesn't need the cash immediately.

### Table 3: Rental Income

**What to enter:**
- Monthly rental amount (given by client, not assumed by you)
- Annual inflation rate for rental (also given by client)

**Important:** Rental income only appears in the cash flow **from the retirement age onward** — not before. Before retirement, the client is spending rental income as part of their current lifestyle, not locking it in for retirement. We can't account for what isn't committed.

**Investment property with a loan:**
- Enter the gross rental (e.g., $6,000/month)
- Enter the loan repayment as a negative deduction (e.g., −$5,000/month until age 65)
- The spreadsheet nets them: low net income during loan period → high income after loan completion
- This is a **cash** loan, not CPF — different from the housing section in the main input tab

**Up to 5 properties** can be entered. If more, combine similar ones into a single row.

### Table 4: Regular Income / Payout Plans (and Negative Cash Flows)

For **retirement income plans** that pay out regular amounts over multiple years — the only table that produces ongoing cash flow streams (appears in Column H of the Cash Flow tab).

**How to enter:** Copy the payout schedule from the fact-find file → paste into Table 4 using **Paste Values** (not regular paste — to avoid carrying over formulas). Paste into Column C, starting from the correct age row.

**This table also accepts negative numbers** for:
- Children's/godchildren's education funding from retirement cash flow
- Endowment premiums still being paid after retirement
- Housing loan payments that continue beyond retirement (when CPF stops paying)
- Any recurring outflow during retirement years

**Housing loan extending beyond retirement example:**
If CPF was paying $490/month for housing until age 57, but retirement is at 60 and the loan runs to 63 — CPF stops contributing at 60 (no more income). The remaining 3 years of loan payments ($490 × 12 = $5,880/year) must be captured as negative entries in Table 4 from age 60 to 62.

### Reading the Cash Flow After Integration

Each column in the cash flow now shows a different income source:

| Column | Source | Type |
|--------|--------|------|
| F | CPF LIFE | Fixed annual income (orange on graph) |
| G | Rental income | Inflating annual income (blue on graph) |
| H | Current portfolio regular income | From Table 4 payout plans (green on graph) |
| I | Current portfolio lump sums | From Tables 1 & 2 (one-time injections) |

**The red line on the graph** = deficit (how much client must withdraw from reserves each year)

**Goal:** Bring the red line to approximately **one-third** away from the zero line.

**As you add more assets,** Column L extends further. If it crosses age 85, the client is fully funded — no gap to bridge.

- Client had CPF over $1 million + 6 retirement income policies
- Cash flow showed Column L exceeding 85 comfortably
- **You cannot sell her anything** — she's fully funded

### Case 2: The 28-Policy Client ($8,000/month lifestyle)

- Cold lead from webinar, had policies from every major insurer
- 10+ retirement income plans, all small individual amounts
- Cash flow showed she was more than adequately funded
- Client said "You're the first person to put everything together" and voluntarily opened a case

### The Two-Thirds Rule in Practice

One retired client (age 68) draws $8,000/month but her last drawn pay was $28,000. Two-thirds would have been ~$14,000–$19,000. She regrets not planning for more — habitual spending patterns from working years don't simply disappear at retirement.

### Life Planning Beyond Finances

One client — a loner with no children, workaholic, devoted to her husband — retired to spend time with him. He died unexpectedly while exercising, just months before her planned retirement. She moved back to her mother's house (age 80+).

**Lessons:**
- Our parents' home always has room for us; the reverse isn't always true
- You must have a house you can call your own
- You must have money — you cannot fully rely on children
- Retirement planning isn't complete without **estate/legacy planning**
- Accumulation is important, but so are hobbies, social connections, and emotional well-being — the financial piece is just one part of a rounded retirement

## Stage 5: Fine-Tuning (Use With Caution)

**Warning:** Fine-tuning can eliminate the gap entirely, leaving you with no case to sell. Use sparingly and strategically.

### Option 1: Optimize with CPF Schemes

Usually not practical for clients close to retirement — limited years for VC, RSU, VHR. VHR just transfers money between accounts, doesn't create new wealth.

### Option 2: Enhance Current Resources

Only works if you can find instruments delivering better returns than what the client currently has. If everything is already invested and the client refuses to commit more cash, there's nothing to enhance.

### Option 3: Reduce Expected Retirement Income ⚠️

**Do NOT suggest this.** If you tell the client "reduce from $6,000 to $5,300 and you'll be fine," you lose your case. Only adjust if the client **voluntarily** says they're willing to lower expectations.

### Option 4: Defer Retirement Age ⚠️

**Do NOT suggest this either.** Deferring from 60 to 63 adds CPF contributions and reduces drawdown years — may close the gap entirely. Only factor in if the client raises it themselves.

### Option 5: Graduated Lifestyle Reduction (Advanced)

The spreadsheet allows different lifestyle amounts for different age bands:

| Age Band | Example Amount |
|----------|---------------|
| 60–64 | $6,000 |
| 65–69 | $5,800 |
| 70–74 | $5,500 |
| 75–79 | $5,200 |
| 80–85 | $5,000 |

Rationale: As people age, they naturally spend less. Even with reduced today's-dollar amounts, future-dollar spending still increases due to inflation — so the client isn't actually receiving less in nominal terms.

**Caution:** Once you manually edit these cells, the formula is disturbed. To restore, use Undo (Ctrl+Z) repeatedly. The formula in each cell references the one above it (e.g., E52 = E50). If you know Excel, you can re-link; otherwise, just undo.

**Recommendation:** For clients who are close to their target (like Miss Singer at 88%), don't fine-tune. Just sell and close the case.

## Stage 5: Solution — Bridging the Gap

### Step 1: Calculate the Total Deficit

Go to Cash Flow → highlight Column K (Cash Flow Budget Required) from the first negative year through age 85 → read the total. Also note any remaining positive balance in Column L at the year before the gap begins.

Example: Miss Singer's deficit from 82–85 = **−$428,861**. But she still has $70,000 at age 82. Net gap ≈ $430,000.

### Step 2: Determine the Solution Type

Two options to bridge the gap:

**Option A — Boost Liquidity (Lump Sum):**
Use flexible withdrawal products (Pro Wealth / Great Wealth Multiplier / S-SWB / Pro Plus / Retirement Saver).

- Generate a quotation that provides ~$430,000 at maturity
- Key the maturity amount into **cell M36** (or the row corresponding to the age when Column L first hits zero, e.g., age 82)
- Column L should now stay positive through age 85

**Option B — Boost Passive Income (Cash Flow):**
Use retirement income plans (Great Retire Income / Supreme Retirement / Dividend Fund).

- Generate a quotation showing regular annual payouts
- Key payout amounts into **Column J** (proposal cash flow) starting from the payout age
- The payout stream adds to the client's annual income, reducing the deficit

### Step 3: Factor In Premium Liability

**Critical:** If the premium payment period extends beyond the retirement age, those premiums must be entered as **negative values** in the cash flow during retirement years.

Example: Miss Singer is 53, buys 10-year pay plan:
- Years 53–59: Premium paid from employment income (no impact on retirement cash flow)
- Years 60–62: Premium paid from retirement reserves (must key negative values in cash flow)

After entering the premium liability, Column L may show "holes" (negative values mid-stream). Adjust the solution amount upward until all of Column L stays positive through 85.

### Step 4: Verify with the Graph

After the solution is in place:
- The **red line** (deficit) should move much closer to the zero line
- New income streams (dark green = proposal) should combine with orange (CPF LIFE), blue (rental), and light green (existing plans) to nearly match the blue expense line
- The reserve should sustain through age 85

### Solution Examples by Product Type

| Product | Type | How to Input |
|---------|------|-------------|

## Three Groups of CPF Members (Above 55)

### Group 1: Born Before 1958

- CPF LIFE is **optional** — default scheme is Retirement Sum Scheme (RSS)
- Most have already started collecting RSS payouts
- RSS pays a fixed monthly amount until the RA is depleted (not for life)

**How to find the payout amount:**
- Check YS OA — it will state the monthly payout amount
- If deferred and not yet collecting, use the **Retirement Sum Calculator** (only accessible via the member's own SingPass — you cannot access it). Use CPF LIFE Estimator as an approximation: generate a Standard Plan estimate and key that in.

**How to handle RSS in the spreadsheet:**
- Do NOT enter the RSS payout in the CPF LIFE field (it would show income for life, which is wrong)
- Instead, enter **zero** for CPF LIFE
- Go to **Table 4** (Input Current Portfolio) → enter the RSS payout as a regular income stream for the limited number of years it pays out

### Group 2: Born 1 Jan 1958 – 30 Jun 1960

- CPF LIFE is **compulsory** — they are "pioneers" of CPF LIFE
- They are **policyholders** — already chose their plan at age 55
- Premium paid in **two installments**: half at 55 (immediately), half at 65 (RA balance with interest)

**How to find the payout amount (three methods, in order of preference):**
1. Call CPF hotline → ask: What plan? How much paid? What's the expected monthly payout?
2. Find the **Policy Certificate** (rare — most people don't have it)
3. Check **YS OA** → find the stated payout amount → take that number ÷ 2 → key into the spreadsheet (the YS OA shows a range; take the average)

**Why Group 2 is disadvantaged:**
- They pay premium at 55 (lose interest on that portion if they die before 65)
- Group 3 pays the same total premium but only at 65, keeping full interest until then
- Both groups receive the same monthly payout — unfair to Group 2
- This is why the government changed the system for Group 3

### Group 3: Born After 30 Jun 1960

- CPF LIFE is **compulsory** — choose plan at age 65 (or defer to 70)
- Full RA used as premium in **one installment** at the point of selection
- Use **CPF LIFE Estimator / Monthly Payout Calculator** to generate the payout amount
- Three plans available: Standard, Basic, Escalating — once chosen, cannot change

### CPF LIFE Basic: 10% vs 20% Premium Rule

For CPF LIFE Basic, the premium going into the common pool depends on **when you start**:

| Start Age | Premium to Common Pool | Reason |
|-----------|----------------------|--------|
| 65 | 10% of RA | 25 years to accumulate before payout begins at 90 |
| 70 | 20% of RA | Only 20 years to accumulate — needs more premium for same payout |
| Between 65–70 | Interpolated between 10%–20% | |

This is why Basic plan policyholders retain 90% of their RA (at 65) — only 10% goes to the annuity pool. The remaining 90% continues earning interest and is drawn down for monthly payouts until age 90, when the annuity component kicks in.

## Self-Employed: How to Use the Spreadsheet

### Type 1: Expat / No CPF at All

- Key in date of birth and statement date (today's date)
- Leave salary as **zero** — no CPF contributions generated
- All CPF fields show zero — Stage 1 is instantly complete (nothing in CPF)
- Proceed directly to Stage 2 (desired lifestyle) → Stage 3/4 (other assets) → Stage 5 (solution)
- Works for any foreigner, non-working spouse, or person with zero CPF

### Type 2: Singaporean Self-Employed (MediSave Only)

- Self-employed contribute only to MediSave (mandatory)
- Leave salary as **zero** in the spreadsheet
- Reason: Annual MediSave contribution is small (~$3,000–$8,160 depending on income). Overflow to SA/OA is negligible and doesn't meaningfully impact retirement planning.
- MediSave contribution cap: chargeable income × age-based rate, capped at monthly ceiling × 12 (e.g., $6,800 × 12 = $81,600 annual cap, then apply the rate)
- Treat the same as an expat for planning purposes — all retirement provision comes from outside CPF

### Type 3: Self-Employed with Prior CPF (Worked Before)

- Has existing CPF balances from previous employment
- Now self-employed, contributing only to MediSave
- **Still key salary as zero** — the existing balances are already captured from the statement
- The spreadsheet will project existing balances forward with interest
- If paying a housing loan from CPF OA, the loan will gradually deplete the OA
- SA continues growing at 4% untouched until 55

### Type 4: Self-Employed Making Fixed Voluntary Contributions

For those who voluntarily contribute a fixed annual amount to CPF:

**Method: Work backward from the contribution amount**

| Annual Contribution | Calculation | What to Key In |
|--------------------|-------------|----------------|
| $20,000 | $20,000 ÷ 37% = $54,054 → $54,054 ÷ 12 = **$4,505/month** | Salary: $4,505, Bonus: 0, Increment: 0% |
| $35,000 | $35,000 ÷ 37% = $94,595 → $94,595 ÷ 12 = $7,883 → **exceeds salary ceiling** ($6,800) | Redo: $94,595 ÷ $6,800 = 13.91 months → Salary: $6,800, Bonus: **1.91 months**, Increment: 0% |
| $37,740 (max) | $37,740 ÷ 37% = $102,000 → $102,000 ÷ 12 = $8,500 → **exceeds ceiling** | Redo: $102,000 ÷ $6,800 = 15 months → Salary: $6,800, Bonus: **3 months**, Increment: 0% |

**Verification:** Go to CPF Projection tab → add up 12 months of contributions → should equal the target annual amount.

**Key rule:** If the calculated monthly salary exceeds the current CPF salary ceiling ($6,800 in 2024), you must reject it and redistribute using the bonus field. Always set salary increment to 0% for fixed contribution simulations.

# Lecture Notes

## SA Closure: The Golden Opportunity

### Timing & Announcement

The SA (Special Account) closure will be announced in the second half of the year (possibly during National Day Rally). It is **not** 1 January 2025 — the exact date is pending. Once announced, the "heat is on."

### Who Is Affected

- **Only people above 55 years old.** Below 55, everything remains per normal — SA continues to exist and earn 4%.
- For those above 55: SA balances will be transferred based on whether the client has met their FRS.

### What Happens to SA Money at Closure

| Situation | Where SA Money Goes |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Met FRS** (with cash, or cash + property via auto-pledge) | SA → **OA** (earning 2.5% instead of 4%) |
| **Not met FRS** (deficit exists) | SA → **RA first** (to top up toward FRS) → any remaining balance → **OA** |

### How to Check if FRS Is Met

Log into client's CPF account → **Retirement Dashboard** → scroll to **Retirement Sum**:
- If it says *"You have met your full retirement sum"* with 100% cash → all SA goes to OA
- If it says *"set aside in cash and property"* → client has auto-pledged; SA still goes to OA (property covers the FRS deficit)
- If neither → SA goes to RA first to fill the FRS gap, balance to OA

### SA Closure & CPFIS (SA Investments)

- SA closure will **not** trigger forced liquidation of existing CPFIS-SA investments
- Investments stay invested until the client voluntarily liquidates
- Upon liquidation: proceeds go to **RA first** (if FRS not met) → balance to OA
- For regular premium CPFIS-SA plans: how future premiums are funded post-closure is **not determined by CPF** but by the financial institution. Clients should write to their insurer to ask whether future deductions will come from OA or cash.

### The Rule of 72 Objection Handler

When clients ask "Why should I move money earning 2.5% in OA?":

> "At 4% in SA, your money doubles in 18 years. At 2.5% in OA, it takes 28 years. If you're above 55, adding 28 years — how many doublings do you realistically have? The money will be eroded. Part of it should go into an instrument delivering 4% or above."

**Product positioning:** ILP Dividend Funds across most companies offer 6–8% dividend payouts — a natural complement to the 2.5% OA rate.

## The OA Surplus Opportunity (Post-SA Closure)

### Why OA Will Grow Much Faster

For clients above 55 who have met FRS and BHS:
- **Current state:** CPF contributions split across OA, SA, MA. After BHS is met (~March each year), MA overflow goes to OA. SA earns 4% separately.
- **Post-SA closure:** 100% of all CPF contributions go directly to OA. SA balance (e.g., $300K) also dumps into OA immediately. Result: OA grows dramatically faster.

### Two Sales Opportunities

1. **Single Premium (from accumulated SA → OA lump sum):**
   - The SA lump sum now sitting in OA at 2.5% can be used to purchase single premium products
   - Can split into 3–5 installments depending on company product options
   - This is the "invest what you've accumulated" money

2. **Regular Premium (from monthly OA surplus):**
   - Monthly CPF contributions that previously went to SA now flow to OA
   - This recurring OA surplus (could be $1,000–$2,000+/month extra) can fund regular premium plans
   - Deduction directly from OA account surplus
   - This is the "save from what you earn" money

> "Next year, selling is very different. You don't have to talk about cash anymore — you talk about CPF OA. It's like the 1990s when we could use CPF OA to buy endowment policies."

### Action Items for Advisors

1. Sort your client list — target those turning 55 in the next 1–2 years and those already above 55
2. Prepare a simple general message about the SA closure announcement
3. Reach out immediately once the announcement is made
4. Consider organizing a mini webinar or Zoom session for groups of mutual clients
5. Don't wait until the official closure date — clients can withdraw SA money and act now

## CPF LIFE vs. Retirement Income Plans: Orange & Apple

### CPF LIFE (The "Apple")

**Pros:**
- Currently the best annuity in the market
- Highest combined living and death benefits
- Payable for life
- Government-backed

**Cons:**
- Earliest payout is 65 (cannot start earlier)
- Lose liquidity until 65 — money is locked in RA
- Limited to one-time withdrawal via property pledging
- No surrender value
- Not ideal for average life expectancy — the break-even at 4% is around **age 88** for Standard Plan
- If you die at 80–81, you've only recouped your premium (zero interest earned — 15 years of interest donated to the common pool)
- Return works out to slightly above 2% for average lifespan

> "Annuities are good only if you live long. Beyond 85, the annuity works very well. Annuities combat longevity risk; life insurance combats premature death. Don't tie all your money into an annuity — diversify."

### Retirement Income Plans / ILP Dividend Funds (The "Orange")

**Pros:**
- Can start payout **before 65**
- Can offer higher monthly payout (though for limited years for traditional plans)
- Offers liquidity (surrender value, policy loan)
- ILP dividend funds can pay **perpetually** (like CPF LIFE) assuming declarations don't change
- Good complement to CPF LIFE

**Cons:**
- Traditional plans: payout limited to a fixed period
- ILP plans: returns not guaranteed, dividends may change
- Insurance company risk vs. government backing

**Important disclaimer:** You cannot say orange is better than apple — they have different offerings and cannot be directly compared. But the orange **may meet your client's needs better** in certain situations.

## ERS vs. FRS: Liquidity vs. Income

### The Decision Framework

Choosing between FRS and ERS is like choosing how much to put into a single premium plan:

- **ERS (more money locked in)** → Higher CPF LIFE payout, but less liquidity
- **FRS (default)** → Standard CPF LIFE payout, standard liquidity
- **BRS (less money, property pledged)** → Lower CPF LIFE payout, but maximum liquidity now

> "It's simply: do you want more income or more liquidity? There's no right or wrong answer."

### Does ERS Affect the Spreadsheet Projection?

No — the difference between FRS and ERS in terms of stretching the cash flow is negligible. The money is the same pool; it's just how much is locked versus liquid. The spreadsheet draws down from both anyway.

### ERS Caution for the 4x Option (Coming 2025)

- 4x ERS for someone turning 55 next year = ~$426,000 locked in RA
- Once committed, it's **irreversible and irrevocable**
- No access to it except via monthly CPF LIFE payouts (from 65/70)
- One-time property pledge withdrawal is the only escape (limited to 1 BRS block)
- If there's an urgent cash need, you cannot accelerate CPF LIFE payouts

> "I recommend not going all-in on ERS for clients who only have CPF and not much cash. Once locked, there's no liquidity."

## Property Pledging: Key Rules

### What Qualifies

- Must be a **residential property** (HDB or private)
- Must have **remaining lease beyond the owner's age 95**
- Must meet **P+I criteria** (paid using CPF) for online pledging; otherwise manual pledging at CPF office

### Withdrawal Rules

- **One block only** can be withdrawn (1 BRS block from the original FRS — not from ERS top-ups)
- This one block can be spread across multiple withdrawals over multiple years (e.g., $15K this year, $50K next year, $30K the year after — aggregate = 1 BRS)
- Interest earned in RA **cannot be withdrawn**
- ERS/RSU top-up amounts are **never withdrawable** — only the original green FRS block

### Auto-Pledge

- Happens automatically at 55 if you don't have enough cash to meet FRS
- Once auto-pledged, you have met FRS (cash + property)
- Future CPF contributions go to OA (not RA)
- To de-pledge: do a **VHR (Voluntary Housing Refund)** — return the P+I amount to OA → it transfers to RA → officially de-pledges → allows future pledging again
- RSU (Retirement Sum Top-Up) also meets FRS but does **not** de-pledge — you cannot pledge again after RSU

### Upon Death

- The pledge **dies with you** — no obligation to honor it
- The property passes to beneficiaries per the will or intestacy rules
- CPF account closes upon death — nothing returns to a closed account
- The property can be sold at full value; proceeds go to beneficiaries

## CPF LIFE Groups Recap (Quick Reference)

### Group 1: Born Before 1958

- CPF LIFE is **optional** — default is RSS (Retirement Sum Scheme)

- **Default payout age: 65**

- **Default payout age: 70**
- 3–6 months before turning 65, CPF **invites** them to start early — if they don't reply, default is 70
- Reminders appear in annual YS OA that they can start anytime between 65–70
- At 55: only set the **budget** (FRS/ERS/BRS) — do not choose the CPF LIFE plan yet

|-----------|----------------------|
| 65 | 10% of RA |
| 70 | 20% of RA |
| In between | Interpolated |

Why more at 70: shorter accumulation period before payout at 90 → needs more premium for the same monthly income. Same principle as insurance — delay = higher premium.
