# Day 14: Testimonials that actually convert

> **Today in one line:** A testimonial earns trust when it shows the before, the during and the after. Miss one beat and it reads as a claim.

**What to remember**

1. A testimonial persuades when it shows the client's before, during and after, with real specifics in each beat.
2. Ask the client three questions about their story, and the answers become your three beats.
3. Before your first close, post an honestly framed live case, mentor's case or training case.
4. End a testimonial with a small free resource and a DM keyword to turn it into an offer post.

## Why most testimonials fall flat

Most advisor testimonials look like this: "Jenny is a wonderful advisor. Very patient, very professional, highly recommended!"

The reader learns nothing from it. There's no situation, no change and nothing at stake. It's a compliment, and compliments get scrolled past.

A testimonial works when it shows the client moving from one state to another because of something you did together. Without that arc it's praise, and readers assume praise was arranged.

## The pre-during-post structure

![The pre-during-post structure: before (the situation), during (the work), after (the new state)](/next-60-days/images/n60-day-14-m0.webp)

Before is what the client's life, portfolio or worry looked like when you met. Be specific. "Ryan had three old ILPs he didn't understand and was paying $780/month without knowing why" beats "Ryan needed help."

During is what the two of you actually did: "We mapped the cashflow, cut one redundant plan, restructured the two that worked." Write it as a list of things done, with no product pitch.

After is where the client is now, in concrete terms: "He's $340/month lighter, has a clearer picture of his coverage, and knows exactly what his next review looks like."

## A worked example

> Before: "When I first met Amir, he was 34, had two kids under 5, and was paying $1,100/month across four policies. He hadn't read any of them in five years. His wife had just left full-time work and the household cashflow was tighter than it had ever been."
>
> During: "We went through every policy document line by line. I showed him which two were still doing their job (his wife's CI and the kids' savings plans), which one overlapped, and which one was costing him $260/month for nothing. We restructured the overlap, cancelled the dead plan, and topped up a gap we found in his own critical illness cover."
>
> After: "Three months later, Amir's household pays $180/month less in premiums, his critical illness cover went from $150K to $500K, and he knows exactly what his family would do if bad news came. He tells me that clarity was the biggest change."

Each beat carries about three specifics, so the reader can see Amir's situation change.

## How to ask a client for a testimonial

The hard part is getting a client to say something useful. "Would you mind leaving me a quick testimonial?" gets you "Jenny is amazing!"

Ask for their story in three parts instead:

> "When we first started, what was the situation with your finances? What was frustrating or confusing, or keeping you up at night?"
>
> "What did we actually do together? What's one thing we changed or figured out?"
>
> "Three months in, what's different now, and how does it feel?"

You get three paragraphs to stitch into before, during and after. Then ask if you can edit for length and post it. Most clients agree when the questions were specific enough to answer.

Before anything goes up, get the client's clear consent to share their story, in writing if you can. The Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) requires consent to use someone's personal data. Swapping in a different name to skip that step turns a real story into a made-up one.

Then put the post through your firm's approval process. MAS treats your social media posts as advertising that your firm has to oversee. Keep returns, payout amounts and past performance out of the story: it's about what changed in the client's situation and how you worked together.

## The new-FC workaround (no closed cases yet)

No closed cases yet? You still need Q1 (social proof) posts, and there are three honest ways to make them.

### Option A: a live case-in-progress

Write a testimonial-shaped post about a Fact-Find you did this week, with names removed.

> "Met a 38-year-old dad this week. Had four policies, hadn't looked at any of them since 2019. We spent 90 minutes going through them. Here's what we found, and what we're changing."

You keep the three beats and say honestly where things stand. The after becomes "here's what the next 30 days look like" in place of "here's where we ended up."

### Option B: a mentor's case (with permission)

> "My mentor recently closed a case I want to share, with his permission and his client's, because it changed how I think about CI coverage."

Attribute it clearly. You're showing the kind of work you'll do, without taking credit for it.

### Option C: a training case, honestly framed

> "In our training this week, we worked through a case of a 42-year-old parent with overlapping policies. Here's what we figured out, and why I think most people have this same blind spot."

The reader gets the insight, and you get credit for the thinking.

Whichever you use, don't fake it. A made-up testimonial is the quickest way to lose your warm market once someone notices.

## The offer-post variant

Now combine yesterday's Q5 offer post with a pre-during-post testimonial:

> [pre-during-post testimonial, 3 short paragraphs]
>
> "If you've got old policies you haven't looked at in a while and you're wondering if yours might be a similar story, DM me 'audit' and I'll send you the same 1-page checklist we used to go through Amir's plans. There's nothing to book, you just get the checklist."

One post does three jobs: the testimonial earns trust, the ask is small (a checklist with no call or meeting), and the keyword saves them working out what to say.

## Do this today

1. Pick your case: send a client the three questions, or use Option A, B or C.
2. Write it in three beats, with about three specifics each.
3. End with a small free resource and a DM keyword.
4. Get consent for any client story and your firm's approval, then post it. It's one of your 3 posts this week.

## Sources

- [Harnessing the science of persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini, Harvard Business Review (October 2001)](https://hbr.org/2001/10/harnessing-the-science-of-persuasion) - social proof: people are persuaded by what similar others have done, which is what a specific testimonial shows
- [Guidelines on Standards of Conduct for Digital Advertising Activities (25 September 2025) - Monetary Authority of Singapore](https://www.mas.gov.sg/regulation/guidelines/guidelines-on-standards-of-conduct-for-digital-advertising-activities) - representatives' social media posts are digital advertising, overseen by their firm, and each post must not be misleading on its own
- [Personal Data Protection Act - Personal Data Protection Commission Singapore](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/overview-of-pdpa/the-legislation/personal-data-protection-act) - organisations need consent to collect, use or disclose personal data, which covers a client's story in a post

## Quiz

**Q1. The pre-during-post structure for a testimonial captures:**
- A) The product, the pitch, the close
- B) The before-situation, the work you did, the after-state (correct)
- C) Morning, afternoon, evening
- D) Hook, explain, CTA

**Why:** The three beats show the client moving from one state to another because of an action. Without the before, the reader has nothing to compare against. Without the during, there's no sign you did anything, and without the after there's no reason to believe it worked. Drop any beat and the testimonial reads as praise.

**Q2. The best way to ask a client for a useful testimonial is:**
- A) *"Could you leave me a quick testimonial?"*
- B) Three structured questions: what the situation was, what we did, what's different now (correct)
- C) Send them a draft to approve word-for-word
- D) Record a video interview

**Why:** A generic ask gets a generic answer ("he's great!"). Three specific questions get the client to tell the real story in the shape you need, and you stitch their answers into a pre-during-post post. The client finds it easy to answer, and you end up with a quote you can use.

**Q3. A Week 3 FC has not closed any cases yet. Which is NOT a legitimate way to create a Q1 (social proof) post?**
- A) A live case-in-progress, with anonymised details, honestly framed
- B) A mentor's case shared with the mentor's and client's permission
- C) A training case reframed as *"here's what we worked through this week"*
- D) A fabricated testimonial with a made-up client name (correct)

**Why:** A, B and C are all honest, because the reader knows where the case stands. D destroys your credibility the moment someone in your warm market notices, and people usually do notice.

**Q4. "Jenny is a wonderful advisor. Very patient, very professional, highly recommended!" What's wrong with this as a testimonial?**
- A) It's too short
- B) It's a compliment: there's no situation, no change and nothing at stake (correct)
- C) It uses the wrong pronouns
- D) It's grammatically incorrect

**Why:** A compliment describes a person. A testimonial describes what changed for the client. Without before, during and after, the reader has no baseline and no concrete outcome to believe. It sounds nice, but prospects discount it as arranged praise.

**Q5. In the BEFORE beat, which is stronger?**
- A) "Ryan needed help with his finances."
- B) "Ryan had three old ILPs he didn't understand and was paying $780/month without knowing why." (correct)
- C) "Ryan was looking for an advisor."
- D) "Ryan had questions about insurance."

**Why:** "Three old ILPs" and "$780/month" give the reader a situation they can picture. "Needed help" or "had questions" could be anyone, so there's nothing for the reader to recognise. The before beat works when the reader sees themselves in it.

**Q6. Combining a testimonial with an offer post works well for advisors because:**
- A) It's longer than most posts
- B) It earns trust with the case, makes the ask small (a checklist, no call) and gives a DM keyword so they know what to send (correct)
- C) It uses more hashtags
- D) It looks more professional

**Why:** One post does three jobs. The testimonial earns credibility. Offering a checklist with no call attached makes replying feel cheap, and the keyword means they don't have to work out what to say. A plain testimonial or a plain offer post only does part of that.

**Q7. A client gives you this three-part answer: "(BEFORE) I had four old policies I hadn't read. (DURING) We reviewed every line and cut two. (AFTER) I'm $180/month lighter and finally know what I'm covered for." What's your next move?**
- A) Post it word-for-word without asking
- B) Ask permission to edit it for length and post it; most clients say yes when the questions were specific enough to answer (correct)
- C) Wait 6 months before using it
- D) Use it but change the client's name to someone else

**Why:** Ask permission, edit lightly, then post. Posting without consent breaks trust if the client sees it, even when the quote is glowing, and PDPA requires consent to use their personal data. Swapping in another name (D) turns a real story into a fabricated one. Clients who answered three specific questions usually agree, because they knew you'd do something with their answers.
