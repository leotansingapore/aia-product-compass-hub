## Limited time?

Recently I met a client who ran an acai business while studying full time at university. He runs it as a solopreneur, and it was amazing how he handled operations and sales and juggled his studies at the same time. He was highly motivated, and I could tell he really loved his work. Now he wants to automate operations further with an acai vending machine, which I thought was really cool.

So yes, running a business, whatever it is, is not easy, especially if you're adding full-time studies on top of it. Time management was one of my biggest concerns when I first started. I entered this career as a student advisor while holding an NUS scholarship called the Global Engineering Program, or GEP for short. It meant I had to cram 4 years of full-time engineering studies into just 3.

This was my timetable on NUSMods. As you can see, I didn't have a single free day. At most I had 2 to 3 hours a day for the business and spent the rest on studies, and I still graduated with honours.

![[first-14-days/images/leo-uni-schedule.webp|My NUSMods timetable]]

So I could only work part-time as a student advisor, with at most 2 hours a day for the role while I juggled tutorials and lectures.

I think the question isn't whether you have time, but whether you can find time. If building something meaningful and growing as a person matter to you, there are many small pockets of time in the day you can squeeze into.

The travel time, the "little in-betweens". In the army we all know "rush to wait, wait to rush", right? Life is the same. There are lull periods throughout the day, and it's up to us to use those pockets for personal growth.

I only had 2 hours a day for the role while balancing my other commitments in school. Now I run 4 six-figure companies while working as a financial advisor. So I don't think it's about whether you "have time". It's about your mindset towards your time. Think of your time as a budget that resets every day. You have a 24-hour "time budget", just like everyone else. What sets the top 1% apart is how they use it.

These are the productivity principles I relied on:

1. The Pareto principle (80/20 rule): I realised 80% of my results came from just 20% of my activities. Meeting clients and following up on leads brought in the most value, while admin and perfecting presentations could be put off or delegated. Focusing on that 20% changed my productivity.
2. Outsourcing: I didn't need to do everything myself, so I handed scheduling, emails and paperwork to virtual assistants and support staff. It cost some money, but it let me focus on work only I could do, like meeting clients and planning how to grow.
3. Batching tasks: instead of jumping between different types of work, I grouped similar tasks together. I set aside blocks of time for calls, another for planning and another for paperwork. That kept me focused and saved me the mental drain of switching back and forth.
4. Elimination: not everything on my to-do list was necessary. By asking "does this really need to be done at all?", I cut out low-value activities like unnecessary meetings and overthinking small tasks. That freed up time and mental energy for what mattered.

This was my weekly schedule as a student advisor.

![[first-14-days/images/student-advisor-schedule.webp|My weekly schedule as a student advisor]]

I didn't have a VA back then. Everything was manual, and 80% of it was done without outsourcing. I didn't have a mentor to guide me either, so my scripts, slides and decks were all made from scratch. All meetings were physical, and everything ran on paper, so admin took wayy more time than it does now.

If I could build my business with just two hours a day, I know you can manage your time well too.

## Do you need a big office?

No. Good advisors meet clients at their homes, in cafes and hotel lobbies, or over video call. Clients care whether you're competent and give them the right advice. They don't care how big your office is.

An office that costs $5,000 a month is $60,000 a year of overhead. I'd rather that money went into ads, VAs and tools that bring in clients. What you should check at any agency is whether there are meeting rooms when you need one, and whether the training and systems are any good.
