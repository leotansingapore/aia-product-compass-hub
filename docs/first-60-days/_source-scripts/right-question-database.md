# Asking the Right Questions: Script Database

The single most leveraged sales skill is asking the right question instead of making the right statement. People defend statements; they own conclusions they reach themselves.

This file is a **bank of right questions**, organised by where you'd use each one. Steal liberally, then make them yours.

## How to use this database

1. **Pick the use-case.** Objection handling (Day 44 Q step), fact-finding (Day 47 SPIN), story closing (Day 45 6P), or generic discovery.
2. **Pick a question that fits the prospect.** Adapt names, numbers, and life-stage language.
3. **Run the 3-point right-question check** before you ask:
   - Does it lead to my objective?
   - Is it specific enough to force a specific answer?
   - Is it logical and indisputable — can the prospect disagree without sounding unreasonable?
4. **Listen.** The answer is the data; your next question depends on it.

---

## Section 1 — The 3-point right-question checklist (run on every question)

Every question below has been pre-tested. When you write your own, gate them through:

| Gate | What to check |
|---|---|
| **1. Leads to objective** | Is there a clear path from this answer to where I want the conversation to go? If "yes" gives me the same outcome as "no," the question is dead weight. |
| **2. Specific** | Does the question force a specific answer? Hedge-friendly questions ("how do you feel about retirement?") leak conviction. Specific questions ("what age do you plan to retire?") capture it. |
| **3. Logical and indisputable** | Can the prospect disagree without sounding unreasonable? "Would you rather pay $30/month now or $300/month in 10 years?" passes. "Don't you think you should plan for the future?" fails — it sounds preachy. |

If a question fails any gate, rewrite before asking.

## Section 2 — Right Questions for Objection Handling (Day 44 Q step)

Use these as the **Q** in the Acknowledge → Question → Reframe pattern. Always follow with a reframe and the time ask.

### 2.1 Diagnostic question-backs (most leveraged)

When the objection is vague, ambiguous, or could mean three different things — the right-question move here saves you from reframing against the wrong one.

> **"Not interested."**
> "Just so I respect your time properly — when you say not interested, is it not interested in insurance specifically, not interested in talking to advisors at all, or not the right timing? Any of those is fine, I just want to know which."

> **"I'll think about it."**
> "Totally fair — and I want to make sure I'm not the reason you're hesitating. Is the part you want to think about the offer itself, the timing, or whether I'm the right person to do it with?"

> **"Send me info first."**
> "Of course. So I send the right thing — is it the product details you want, a comparison with what you already have, or just a sense of what a meeting would look like?"

> **"I already have policies."**
> "Smart — most people I respect already have something. Quick one before I assume — when you say already have, is it (a) something from when you were younger and you haven't checked it, (b) a plan a parent or employer set up for you, or (c) something you reviewed recently and feel solid about?"

> **"Maybe later."**
> "Got it — and that's reasonable. Just so I'm not chasing you when it's not the right time, what would have to change for later to become now?"

> **"Let me speak to my wife / husband / partner first."**
> "Absolutely. So when I do come back, it's productive — what do you think their first question would be? I'd rather have a clean answer ready than have you go back and forth twice."

### 2.2 Pinpoint question-backs (cold-call, find the agenda)

The Day 43 cold-call script uses these to surface the prospect's actual problem so the meeting agenda is *theirs*.

> **For young professionals:** "Quick one — currently, are you investing already, or is your money still sitting in the bank?"

> **For young parents:** "When you think about your kids' first 5 years of life, the bigger worry is the cost of preschool, the cost of healthcare if something goes wrong, or just being able to take time off if you needed to?"

> **For pre-retirees:** "If retirement happened tomorrow at your current savings rate, would you be comfortable, stretched, or genuinely worried? No wrong answer."

> **For SME owners:** "If you couldn't run the business for 6 months because of an injury, who would step in — and how long would the cash flow hold?"

### 2.3 Re-engagement question-backs (after a soft no)

For follow-up touches inside the 6-touch sequence on Day 44.

> **2 weeks after a soft no:** "Hey [name] — random one. Last time we spoke you mentioned [specific concern]. Did anything end up shifting on that, or still where you left it?"

> **After they've ghosted twice:** "I'm guessing the timing's still off, which is totally fine. One question though — what would have to change for me to be useful to you again, even if it's 12 months out?"

## Section 3 — Right Questions for Fact-Find (Day 47 SPIN)

The four SPIN phases each have a right-question shape. Use these as templates, not scripts — the right wording depends on what the prospect just said.

### 3.1 Situation questions (warm up the data)

Purpose: get factual context. Keep these short and few — too many feels like an interrogation.

> "What's your current setup with savings — anything regular, or mostly ad-hoc?"

> "On the protection side, what do you have — and roughly how much cover?"

> "When you last looked at your CPF balance, was that recent or has it been a while?"

> "Mind sharing roughly what's coming in monthly, just so I have ballpark — don't need exact?"

### 3.2 Problem questions (surface the gap)

Purpose: name the pain the prospect already half-knows is there. The prospect should hear themselves describe a gap.

> "If your current cover had to pay out tomorrow, do you have a sense of whether it'd actually be enough — or is that something you've never really stress-tested?"

> "When was the last time you actually checked what you're paying for — versus just letting it auto-deduct?"

> "If you had to retire today, how many years of your current lifestyle would your savings cover?"

### 3.3 Implication questions (make the gap weighty)

Purpose: surface the consequences of inaction. This is where SPIN converts at 1-in-3 instead of 1-in-10.

> "If your kid needed an emergency surgery the basic plan didn't cover, where would the $40k come from?"

> "If you couldn't work for 6 months, what would your family have to give up first — the house, the schools, or the lifestyle?"

> "If you only realised at 60 that the savings rate was wrong, how much extra would you have to set aside in your last working years to catch up?"

> "If inflation runs at 4% and your CPF Life is fixed, how does the gap look at age 75?"

### 3.4 Need-Payoff questions (close on their own conclusion)

Purpose: get the prospect to articulate the value of solving the problem, in their own words. They convince themselves.

> "If you could close that gap with $300/month — same as eating-out budget — would you sleep better?"

> "If we sorted this in two meetings, what would change for you the next time you check your account?"

> "What would it mean for your family if you knew, definitively, that the worst-case scenario was covered?"

> "Would having a single document that shows where everything stands be useful — and worth the 90 minutes to build it?"

## Section 4 — Right Questions for Story Closes (Day 45 6P)

A 6P story without a closing question is forgotten by the next morning. The closing right-question is what hands the story's relevance over to the prospect.

The pattern: after the **Purpose** beat, ask a question that maps the story onto *their* life.

| Story type | Closing right-question |
|---|---|
| **CI gap (crisis arc)** — Marcus | "If a $30k cap was the difference for Marcus's family, what would the cap on your current cover be?" |
| **Compounding (positive arc)** — Priya | "What's stopping you from being Priya at [their age] instead of waiting?" |
| **Hospital plan (expensive lesson)** — Ben | "If you couldn't work for six months, what would that cost you?" |
| **Underinsured during hospitalisation** | "If you were the one needing the upgrade today, would you take the public-ward 6-month wait or the $48k out of pocket?" |
| **Saved early, retired well** | "If you started today at the same rate they did, where would you be in 10 years?" |
| **Bought wrong product, regretted it** | "How would you tell the difference between the right product and a product that just looks right?" |
| **Plan paid out (validation story)** | "If you needed the policy to actually do what it promises tomorrow, would you trust yours to do it?" |
| **Referral story** | "If a close friend asked you the same question you asked me today, what would you tell them?" |

## Section 5 — Generic right-question patterns (use anywhere)

These work in any phase of any conversation. Memorise the shapes; the words flex.

### 5.1 The "if not now, when" question
Forces a temporal commitment without sounding pushy.

> "If not now, when does this become important enough to look at?"

### 5.2 The "binary force" question
Removes the hedge by giving two specific paths.

> "Is this more about [specific concern A] or [specific concern B]?"

### 5.3 The "self-comparison" question
Anchors them against their own past or future self.

> "Versus where you were 5 years ago on this — better, worse, or about the same?"

### 5.4 The "third-party reference" question
Lowers defensiveness by routing through other people.

> "If a friend in your exact situation asked you for advice, what would you tell them?"

### 5.5 The "magic wand" question
Surfaces the actual desired outcome past the politeness layer.

> "If you could wave a wand and have one thing about your finances change tomorrow, what would it be?"

### 5.6 The "10-year regret" question
Surfaces inaction cost without being preachy.

> "10 years from now, if nothing's changed — what's the thing you'd most regret not addressing?"

### 5.7 The "fact-vs-feeling" question
Tests whether their objection is rational or emotional — both are fine, but you handle them differently.

> "Is the hesitation more about the numbers not adding up, or more about not being sure it's the right time?"

### 5.8 The referral-ask question family
The end-of-meeting right-question that turns a single client into a network. Day 38 covers the principle; these are the question variants.

> **Standard:** "Can you think of 2-3 friends who would benefit from the kind of conversation we just had?"

> **Specific-segment version (stronger):** "If you had to pick 2-3 friends who are roughly your age and just starting families, who would come to mind first?"

> **Permission-led version:** "Would you be comfortable making an introduction to anyone you know who's in a similar life stage to you?"

> **The "magic-wand" referral:** "If you could clone yourself and meet me in 3 different friends' bodies, who would those 3 friends be?"

**Why specific-segment beats standard:** the standard version leaves the client to filter from their entire network; the segment version pre-filters to a relevant pocket and only asks for 2-3 names from there. Same gate-1 (specific names) but stronger gate-2 (forces a tighter answer).

**The follow-up right-question when they give a name:** "Tell me a bit about them - what's their situation?" - immediately moves the conversation from a name to a context, which is what you actually need to make the warm-introduction text useful.

## Section 6 — Right-question anti-patterns (questions that fail the 3-point check)

Avoid these. Each one breaks at least one gate.

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **"Don't you think you should plan for the future?"** | Fails Gate 3 — sounds preachy. Prospect agrees defensively or shuts down. |
| **"How do you feel about your finances?"** | Fails Gate 2 — too vague. Generates "fine" 95% of the time. |
| **"Have you ever thought about insurance?"** | Fails Gate 1 — doesn't lead anywhere. Yes or no, you're stuck. |
| **"What if something happened to you tomorrow?"** | Fails Gate 3 — moralistic. Triggers reflex defence ("nothing will happen, I'm healthy"). |
| **"Are you covered enough?"** | Fails Gate 2 — what does "enough" mean? Forces hedge. |
| **"Wouldn't you agree that..."** | Fails Gate 3 — leading and obviously rhetorical. Prospect feels manipulated. |
| **"Why don't you have insurance?"** | Fails Gate 3 — interrogative. Prospect defends rather than reflects. |

**Fix all of these with one move:** add specificity, drop the moralism, route through a peer comparison or a binary.

Example fix:
- ❌ "Don't you think you should plan for the future?"
- ✅ "If your savings rate at 30 had to fund 25 years of retirement starting at 65, what would you need it to be?"

Same goal. The first sounds like a sermon. The second forces the prospect to do the maths and arrive at the conclusion themselves.

## Section 7 — Building your own right questions

When you draft a question, run it through this:

1. **Write the question.**
2. **Predict the three most likely answers.**
3. **For each answer, write the next question.**
4. **If any branch dead-ends — rewrite the original.**

Right questions have **a future** — every answer leads somewhere. Wrong questions terminate the conversation.

The discipline is small but the payoff is huge. 5 minutes on your top 5 questions before a meeting is the highest-ROI prep you can do.
