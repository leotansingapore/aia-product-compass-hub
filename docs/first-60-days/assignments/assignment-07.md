> **The gist.** Write 100 reasons you're in this career in one sitting, then distil the real ones into a single sentence. It sounds simple. It isn't - the first 20 are the lines you'd say at a party; the last 40 are the ones that keep you in the chair when discipline runs out.

**You'll do three things:** write the 100, mark the ones that actually move you, then compress them into one sentence that's still true on a bad week.

**The shape of it:** number the page 1 to 100 -> write one why per line without censoring -> push past the wall around #30 -> read them all back and highlight the real ones -> write one sentence that captures them.

## Why it's worth the hour

Day 5 calls purpose the load-bearing wall - the thing that keeps you showing up once discipline and financial pressure run out. The catch is that almost no new FC actually knows their why. They know a slogan, and the slogan won't survive Month 4.

The real why is buried under the obvious ones. You have to write past "financial freedom," past "I want flexibility," past "my parents would be proud," to reach the reasons that are actually load-bearing. The only way to get there is to keep writing after the obvious reasons run out. By #20 you'll feel done - that's the party answers talking. By #50 you'll feel stuck - that stuck feeling is where the honest ones start.

## How to run it

Set aside one uninterrupted sitting, 60 to 90 minutes, phone face down. Pen and paper beats typing - typing makes the whys shorter and more polished, which is the opposite of what you want.

1. **Number the page 1 to 100 first.** Seeing all 100 slots tells your brain you're not finishing in five reasons.
2. **Write one why per line.** Don't censor - if it feels embarrassing or selfish or contradicts an earlier one, write it anyway. Repeat-with-variation counts ("to prove I can do hard things" and "to prove it to my dad specifically" are two different whys). No skipping numbers, and speed beats polish.
3. **Push past the wall.** Most people stall around #25 to #35 - that wall is the point. Switch frame when you stick: from money to people, from people to identity, from today to who you'll be at 65. Or ask the inverse - "what would I have to give up if I quit and went back to a salaried job?" - which usually unblocks another 20 lines.
4. **Distil.** With all 100 down, read top to bottom and highlight the ones that actually move you now (versus the ones that look right but feel hollow). Then write one sentence that captures the highlighted ones. That's your draft why. It'll change by Year 5; it still has to exist now.

## What passing looks like

- The first 20 are short, obvious, a bit performative. *"Financial freedom." "More time with my kids."*
- The middle 40 get specific and uneven - some sharp, some clumsy. *"I don't want my mum to ever feel guilty about her medical bills."*
- The last 40 read like someone telling the truth to themselves - they name people, fears, regrets, futures, and they're uncomfortable to share, which is what makes them real.

If your last 40 sound like your first 20, you stopped before the work started. Reset and try again.

Your distilled sentence should be in your voice (not corporate), about a change in the world rather than a personal status outcome (*"to close the financial literacy gap one household at a time"* beats *"to become a top advisor"*), and still true on a bad week. This is the **Why** layer of the Why -> How -> What structure in [Day 5](../../first-60-days/week-1/day-05.md) - your *How* and *What* get easier once it's honest.

## Submission

Fill in the form. Paste your full 100 (one per line), write your distilled sentence, and answer the two reflection questions - they matter as much as the list, because they show whether the exercise actually moved you. Keep the page somewhere you'll see it; the whys that survive are the ones you re-read.
