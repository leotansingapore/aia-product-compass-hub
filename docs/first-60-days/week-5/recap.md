### [`00:21`](#t=21) - [Day 25](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/25) . Your rhythm, in plain terms
Leo's own is deliberately simple: **weekdays are for building, weekends are for appointments.** Monday to Friday he's building something, weekends he's cramming appointments, with the occasional weekday evening. Yours can be the exact inverse - weekends for family, weekdays for appointments - the point isn't which shape, it's that **there is a shape.** Ask yourself what your current momentum, rhythm, identity and weekly rituals actually are, rather than letting the week decide for you.

### [`01:09`](#t=69) - [Day 25](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/25) . The to-do list is the rhythm's backbone
Everything goes on a tracked list, categorised, with **admin and finance work batched together** so it doesn't leak into the high-value hours. **Every Sunday is a review session** across the week's to-dos. The failure mode Leo sees most often isn't laziness - it's people starting things and forgetting them because too much comes in at once. And the reason it matters more here than in a job: **we're business owners, not employees.** An employee's day repeats; here Leo genuinely doesn't know who he's meeting tomorrow until he looks at the calendar. Five new things can land in an hour. If it isn't written down, it's gone.

### [`03:20`](#t=200) - [Day 26](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/26) . 10X the inputs, not the fiddly bits
**Outputs aren't controllable; inputs are.** So the things to 10X are outreach, content, role-plays and deliberate skill practice. What has to shrink is the low-leverage work - over-perfecting small things and losing the whole day to them. Leo's example of the trap: an adviser burning five hours on a policy-servicing or claims request that is genuinely a two-minute job. Do the same audit on your personal life - he outsources meals and smoothies entirely so food is a reheat, not an hour.

### [`04:58`](#t=298) - [Day 26](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/26) . MAD - your minimum activity day
The 10X rule made concrete: **put a zero behind whatever you're currently doing**, then define the floor beneath it. As a student consultant Leo's every single day was calling, dialling, prospecting and setting appointments - **admin got exactly one block a week**, two hours on Monday afternoon at the office. 80-90% of his time was outreach. Your MAD might be two hours of calling, one post, or one appointment set. **Everything else that day is not a priority.**

### [`06:12`](#t=372) - [Day 27](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/27) . Points per week - and self-imposed fines
Leo's own accountability mechanism was a fine: **miss 30 points in a week and he paid $50 to charity**, unrecoverable until he hit the number the following week. The team's current version is going into the office to call. His view is that a fine you impose on yourself beats one imposed on you. The benchmarks: **20 points a week for students, 30 for full-timers, 50 a week consistently if you want MDRT.** Ten to twenty points a week isn't in the conversation yet.

### [`07:46`](#t=466) - [Day 27](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/27) . Activities x Skills x Knowledge - diagnose the weak one
Three inputs produce the result, and most people over-index on knowledge. **Self-diagnose which of the three is actually your bottleneck.** The medicine happens to be the same for all three - do more reps - which is why Leo's conclusion is that **activity is the biggest lever**: high activity drags skills and knowledge up behind it, while high knowledge with no activity produces nothing. What the team can guarantee is the systems, the presenters, the knowledge base and the scripts, so any reasonably capable person has a baseline. **What it can't guarantee is your activity.** That part is only yours.

### [`11:12`](#t=672) - [Day 27](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/27) . The ratios worth memorising
Rough but usable: **100 freebie leads → about 1 appointment and about 1 close.** Qualified leads run much tighter - roughly **20 to 30 leads per close.** At about $2 cost per lead, **$200 of ad spend should produce a case.** The live proof: a big case he and Gerald closed came off roughly $100-200 of ads and about **9K FYC**, from a lead that entered the pipeline early and was followed up for six months.

### [`12:20`](#t=740) - [Day 27](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/27) . Five levers, and only one you fully control
**QO** (quantity of outreach - your calls, ads, VA texting), **ASR** (the appointment set rate, which is your script), **show-up rate**, **closing rate**, and **LTV**. Even during this call Leo's VAs are texting and calling, so the baseline never stops. The formula: **200 outreaches x 5% set x 30% show-up x 30% close x $5K LTV = about $5K a week.** Then the honest ranking - **LTV takes care of itself** because of how the products are structured, and the set/show/close rates are skill. **The only thing you can change today is QO.**

### [`14:00`](#t=840) - [Day 25](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/25) . The wishing calendar
The same system that shows up again in Week 7, introduced here. Recurring annual review events, plus every birthday and milestone stored as a calendar wish. **On the 1st of each month, Leo goes back through every wish he sent the previous month and texts those people again** to convert the seed into an appointment - happy birthday, congrats on ORD, how's uni, how's the job search. That's why he always has a long reach-out list. Even a first-time rejection is just a contact you hold for five or ten years until the timing lands.

### [`15:18`](#t=918) - [Day 27](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/27) . What a case is actually worth
The numbers behind "LTV takes care of itself." At Leo's tenure the career benefit is 100%; in years one to three it's 80%. A **$1K/month APA is about $15,000 of total income.** A **$200/month APA is about $3,100** - which feels like $70 a month landing in your account, or $35 on a joint case, and that's the gap that makes new advisers panic about ad spend. **You're not earning $70; you're earning $3,100 spread out.** Protection is quietly generous too because commission recurs yearly: a **$595/year Solitaire PA is about $3,570 in total** - meaning a $50/month PA is worth roughly the same as a $200/month APA. A $3,000/year Protect Tree is about **$6,900**; a $5K/year UCC he closed recently paid about **9K**.

### [`18:25`](#t=1105) - [Day 27](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/27) . Mediocre still compounds
Do a deliberately unimpressive **$2.5K FYC a month** and the long-run income still crosses **$100K and then $200K a year** as renewals stack. Do MDRT-level FYC and you're looking at roughly **$20K a month by year 12.** The frame: **year one is all active income; almost everything after is passive.** Compare that to a corporate job where $3K becomes $4K and never scales.

### [`20:04`](#t=1204) - [Day 26](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/26) . Limiting beliefs are the real bottleneck
Leo's read after years of this is that most underperformance is a belief problem, not a skill problem - *"cold calling doesn't work," "I could never do that."* We self-sabotage by deciding it's impossible and then never testing it. **Diagnose your own limiting belief** - it's one of the most useful things to bring to a one-to-one.

### [`20:52`](#t=1252) - [Day 25](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/25) . Why the commission structure keeps you honest
On ILPs, AIA pays roughly **35% year one, 15% year two, then ~1.5% perpetual** plus ~30% perpetual on the protection side. IFAs pay much more up front - 70-80% - and far less afterwards. **That's the churn engine**: an adviser who only earns first-year commission needs the client to buy something new every year, which is why IFA clients get switched around and lose money in the process. A tied agent is paid across the life of the policy, so **staying and servicing is the profitable strategy.** The incentive and the client's interest point the same way.

### [`23:16`](#t=1396) - [Day 28](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/28) . TVM - the three use cases
Benjamin's honest answer (he uses present value in openings but not full TVM) sets up the practical framing. The three things TVM is actually for: **(1)** the future value of a retirement goal - "$3K a month of passive income today means how much in total at 65?"; **(2)** the PMT - "how much do I need to start saving now?"; and **(3)** the one Leo rates highest, **reverse-solving the interest rate of a policy a client already owns.**

### [`26:39`](#t=1599) - [Day 28](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/28) . Reverse-solve the client's existing policy, live
The move that wins meetings. A client says they pay ~$3,000 a year for 25 years and get back $90K. Leo opens a plain interest-rate calculator on screen, keys it in, and **asks the client to read out the rate: 1.48%.** Then: *"what's inflation in Singapore now?"* - about 2%. **"So this plan is losing you about 0.6% a year."** No slide, no persuasion, just the client's own numbers. He notes he rarely needs the PMT or FV calculators separately, because **the investment presenter is already a PMT calculator** (invest $427/month to reach $1M at 65) and the **retirement lifestyle calculator is already an FV calculator**.

### [`29:11`](#t=1751) - [Day 28](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/28) . The retirement number, out loud
A **$4K/month lifestyle today becomes ~$9K/month at retirement** - and because inflation keeps running through the 20 retirement years, the **average requirement is ~$12K/month**, for a total of about **$2.9 million.** Saving that in a bank account is not a real option, which is the whole reason the conversation moves to investing.

### [`30:26`](#t=1826) - [Day 29](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/29) . Rule of 72, and the cost of ten years
**72 ÷ interest rate = years to double.** At 2% that's 36 years; at 6% it's 12. Then the question Leo puts to clients: if you start ten years later, how much less do you end up with - 10%? 20%? **It's about 50%.** Start now and get $1M; start at 35 and get $400K. That's not a slogan, it's just the doubling maths, and it's the cleanest way to make starting today feel urgent.

### [`31:59`](#t=1919) - [Day 29](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/29) . The $50-a-month swing
Not just starting early - starting bigger. Moving from **$400 to $450 a month adds about $475K** at the far end; put the other way, that $50 a month costs six figures if you skip it. The team's comparison tool now renders both scenarios side by side, and starting 20 years late is roughly four times worse than starting ten late. **Compounding is a closing-rate tool**, not a maths lesson.

### [`32:46`](#t=1966) - [Day 30](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/30) . DCA and the robo-advisor objection
This is the objection-handling core of the week, and Leo used it to close a **$400/month APA the week before.** The client's challenge was fair: a robo already diversifies and rebalances - what do you add? The answer isn't features, it's behaviour. **Investing needs discipline, and returns come from time *in* the market, not timing it.** People buy when the market rises and sell when it falls. **Flexibility is the opposite of discipline** - the very ability to cash out any time is what exposes you to fear and greed, so the robo's headline advantage works against the client. Even hedge fund managers fail at timing. Discipline is what has to be outsourced, and DCA is the mechanism - which is also why every case gets put on GIRO, so the investing is automatic rather than manual.

### [`37:47`](#t=2267) - [Day 30](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/30) . DCA vs lump sum, on the tool
On the fund performance comparison, $10,000 invested over ten years returns about **172% via DCA** - and somewhat more as a lump sum, because the whole amount compounds from day one. So **lump sum is the better pitch for clients with capital sitting idle in a rising market**, while DCA is the safer default for anyone who can't time it. Same tool also stacks AIA against Prudential, Manulife, Great Eastern and Singlife directly - **AIA's global tech fund at ~1636% over ten years** and, on the long-term illustrator, roughly **a $1 million gap over the tied-agency competitors** driven mostly by cost structure.

### [`39:52`](#t=2392) - [Day 30](/learning-track/first-60-days/day/30) . Rebalancing is where the managed portfolio earns its fee
**Quarterly rebalancing, automatic, free, already inside the fees.** Most DIY investors never rebalance - and when they do, they pay transaction costs each time. The result is a portfolio that quietly skews to whatever ran hottest: all tech, or too much crypto. On PWV specifically, **AIA runs a three-fund approach that's dynamically reallocated versus competitors' single-fund approach.** Leo's framing for the difference: **company stewardship versus agent risk.** Allianz Growth Fund may be great today, but nobody can promise it stays the best fund for 40 years - and if the agent leaves the industry, nothing gets changed. AIA Investment Management keeps reallocating whether or not anyone is watching. Different asset classes lead in different environments, so the mix has to move.

### [`43:52`](#t=2632) - Wrap-up . The week in one line
Leo runs the week back: **weekly rhythm and habits, your MAD, 10X on inputs, diagnosing whether the bottleneck is activity, skills or knowledge, the ratios (QO, ASR, show-up, closing, LTV), how high LTV really is in this business, TVM for reverse-solving policy interest rates and retirement planning, compounding for urgency, and DCA plus rebalancing as the differentiator against competitors.** Week 6 turns this same maths into the client-facing fundamentals - present value, the Rule of 72 applied, CPF, the investment landscape and inflation.
